## The quick answer

The NSTEMI ECG and the STEMI ECG differ in one key way: whether the ST segment is raised. STEMI and NSTEMI are **both heart attacks**. A **STEMI** shows **new ST-segment elevation** on the ECG and usually means a coronary artery is **completely blocked**. An **NSTEMI** shows **no ST elevation**, usually means a **severe but not complete blockage**, and is confirmed by a **rise in troponin** (a blood test). Both are serious. The main practical difference is how fast treatment needs to happen.

## STEMI in brief

STEMI stands for **ST-elevation myocardial infarction**. It usually happens when a coronary artery, one of the vessels supplying the heart muscle, becomes **fully blocked**. This is most often caused by a fatty plaque in the artery wall rupturing, with a clot then forming on top of it.

On the ECG, a STEMI shows **new ST-segment elevation** in a pattern that matches the area of heart muscle supplied by one coronary artery. A **new left bundle branch block** with chest pain is also treated urgently, because it can hide a heart attack; doctors then use special criteria (Sgarbossa) rather than calling it a STEMI automatically. The usual threshold is new ST elevation of **at least 1 mm in two neighbouring (contiguous) leads**, except in V2–V3, where it is **at least 2 mm in men aged 40 or over, 2.5 mm in men under 40 and 1.5 mm in women**. The exact cut-offs vary slightly by age and sex.

Because the artery is completely shut, the priority is to **reopen it as fast as possible**. This is done either with emergency angioplasty, which opens the artery from the inside, or with clot-busting drugs.

## NSTEMI in brief

NSTEMI stands for **non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction**. It usually happens when an artery is **partially blocked** or severely narrowed, so blood flow to part of the heart muscle is reduced but not fully stopped.

On the ECG there is **no ST elevation**. Instead, there may be **ST depression** or **T-wave inversion**. The ECG can even look **near-normal**. Because of this, the diagnosis rests on a **rise in troponin**, a protein released into the blood when heart muscle is damaged.

An NSTEMI is still a heart attack and is treated **urgently**. Treatment involves medication and an angiogram (a scan of the coronary arteries), **usually within hours to a day**.

## How to tell them apart on the ECG

- **ST segment:** STEMI shows **new ST elevation**. NSTEMI shows **no ST elevation**.
- **Size of the change:** STEMI meets a threshold of **≥1 mm in two contiguous leads**, with higher cut-offs in V2–V3 (≥2 mm in men 40+, ≥2.5 mm in men under 40, ≥1.5 mm in women).
- **Pattern:** In STEMI, the elevation matches **one coronary artery's territory**. A **new left bundle branch block** with symptoms is assessed urgently using Sgarbossa criteria.
- **Other changes:** NSTEMI may show **ST depression** or **T-wave inversion**, or the ECG may look **near-normal**.
- **Blood test:** Both are diagnosed with ECG plus troponin. For NSTEMI, the **raised troponin** is what confirms the diagnosis.

## NSTEMI ECG changes: what an NSTEMI EKG can show

Because an NSTEMI is defined partly by what the ECG does *not* show, people are often surprised by how varied an NSTEMI EKG can look. The common patterns are:

| NSTEMI ECG pattern | What it looks like | What it suggests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Horizontal or downsloping ST depression** | The ST segment sits at least about 0.5 mm below the baseline in two or more neighboring leads | Reduced blood flow to the inner layer of heart muscle (subendocardial ischemia) |
| **T-wave inversion** | T waves flipped upside-down, often deep and symmetrical | Ongoing or recent ischemia |
| **Widespread ST depression with ST elevation in aVR** | ST depression in many leads, with ST elevation in lead aVR | Can point to severe narrowing of the left main artery or several arteries; treated very seriously |
| **Wellens pattern** | Deep or biphasic T waves in V2–V3 after chest pain has settled | A critical narrowing near the start of the left anterior descending artery |
| **Near-normal ECG** | No clear ST or T change | Does not rule out a heart attack; troponin decides |

The ECG is often repeated every 15–30 minutes while the pain persists, because changes can come and go. A normal first ECG does not rule out a heart attack. For more detail on the individual findings, see [ST depression](https://www.ecgmeans.com/st-depression/), [T wave inversion](https://www.ecgmeans.com/t-wave-inversion/) and [Wellens syndrome](https://www.ecgmeans.com/wellens-syndrome/).

### Why troponin matters so much for NSTEMI

Troponin is a protein released when heart muscle cells are damaged. Modern high-sensitivity troponin tests are usually taken on arrival and repeated within about 1–3 hours. A rising or falling troponin above the laboratory's upper reference limit, together with symptoms or ECG changes of ischemia, confirms a heart attack. If the troponin stays normal and the ECG shows no ST elevation, the diagnosis is usually **unstable angina** instead. NSTEMI and unstable angina are grouped together as **NSTE-ACS** (non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome).

### NSTEMI ECG vs STEMI ECG: side by side

| Feature | STEMI ECG | NSTEMI ECG |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ST segment | New ST elevation in one coronary territory | No ST elevation; may be depressed |
| Reciprocal changes | Common (ST depression in opposite leads) | Not applicable |
| T waves | May be tall early ("hyperacute"), later invert | Often inverted or flattened |
| Q waves | Often develop later | Less common |
| Can it look normal? | Rarely, in the very early minutes | Yes, fairly often |
| How it is confirmed | ECG pattern is enough to start emergency treatment | Troponin blood test |
| Usual treatment speed | Reopen the artery as fast as possible | Urgent medication and an angiogram within hours to a day |

### STEMI "equivalents" and hidden heart attacks

Some heart attacks behave like a STEMI even though the ECG does not meet the standard ST-elevation rules. Examples include a [posterior MI](https://www.ecgmeans.com/posterior-mi/) (which shows as ST depression in V1–V3), [de Winter T waves](https://www.ecgmeans.com/de-winter-t-waves/), and a heart attack hidden by [left bundle branch block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-bundle-branch-block/), where doctors use the [Sgarbossa criteria](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sgarbossa-criteria/). These are treated with the same urgency as a STEMI, which is one reason the ECG is read by trained clinicians rather than by the machine's automatic text alone.

## Symptoms can be less obvious with NSTEMI

NSTEMI is more common than STEMI in older adults, women and people with diabetes or kidney disease, and these groups are also more likely to have less typical symptoms. Instead of crushing chest pain, a heart attack may show up as breathlessness, unusual tiredness, nausea, indigestion-like discomfort, or pain in the jaw, back or upper belly. Because the ECG can also look near-normal, these heart attacks are the easiest to miss. If something feels wrong and is new, get checked rather than waiting to see if it passes.

## What each one means for you

If you are reading this because you have symptoms, such as chest pain or pressure, breathlessness, sweating, or pain spreading to your arm or jaw, **call emergency services now**. Don't wait to work out which type it might be. The ECG and troponin test are what sort STEMI from NSTEMI, and that sorting is done by the emergency team.

If you have already been diagnosed, here is what each label generally means:

- **STEMI:** Your care team treated it as a race against time, because a fully blocked artery needs reopening **as fast as possible**. This is why you may have gone straight for emergency angioplasty or received clot-busting drugs.
- **NSTEMI:** Your care was **urgent but planned**. You would have been given medication and an angiogram, **usually within hours to a day**. The less dramatic ECG does not mean it was a less real heart attack.

Both types share the same risk factors: **smoking, diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol and a family history** of heart disease. Your doctors will usually focus on these going forward to help protect your heart. Your own care team is the best source of advice about your specific results, treatment and recovery.

**Get urgent help:** call 911 (US), 999 (UK) or 112 (EU) for chest pain or pressure lasting more than a few minutes, especially with breathlessness, sweating, nausea, or pain spreading to the arm, jaw or back. Do not drive yourself to hospital.

## What usually happens next

After a STEMI or NSTEMI, most people follow a similar path:

1. **Angiogram** to see which artery is narrowed or blocked, often followed by a stent or, sometimes, bypass surgery
2. **An echocardiogram** to check how well the heart is pumping
3. **Long-term medicines** to protect the heart and lower the risk of another event — your doctor will decide which ones suit you
4. **Cardiac rehabilitation**, a supervised exercise and education program that improves recovery
5. **Risk-factor control**: stopping smoking, blood pressure, cholesterol and diabetes management

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Was my heart attack a STEMI or an NSTEMI, and what did my ECG show?
- Which artery was affected, and was it treated with a stent?
- How well is my heart pumping now?
- When can I start cardiac rehabilitation?
- Which ECG changes should I keep a copy of for future comparison?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Is an NSTEMI less serious than a STEMI?
A: Both are heart attacks and both are emergencies. A STEMI usually means an artery is completely blocked, so it needs reopening as fast as possible. An NSTEMI usually means a severe but not complete blockage and is treated urgently with medication and an angiogram, usually within hours to a day. The difference is mainly in how fast treatment needs to happen, not whether it is real.
Q: Can you have a heart attack with a normal-looking ECG?
A: Yes, this can happen with an NSTEMI. The ECG may show ST depression or T-wave inversion, but it can also look near-normal. That is why a troponin blood test is essential, because the NSTEMI diagnosis rests on a rise in troponin.
Q: What ECG change defines a STEMI?
A: A STEMI shows new ST-segment elevation in a pattern matching one coronary artery's territory. The usual threshold is at least 1 mm in two contiguous leads, with higher cut-offs in V2–V3 that depend on age and sex. A new left bundle branch block with chest pain is also assessed urgently.
Q: What causes STEMI and NSTEMI?
A: A STEMI is usually caused by a fully blocked coronary artery, most often from a ruptured plaque and clot. An NSTEMI is usually caused by a partially blocked artery or severe narrowing that reduces blood flow. Both share the same risk factors: smoking, diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol and family history.
Q: What should I do if I think I'm having a heart attack?
A: Call emergency services straight away for symptoms like chest pain or pressure, breathlessness, sweating, or pain spreading to the arm or jaw. Don't try to judge which type it is yourself. The ECG and troponin test will sort STEMI from NSTEMI and decide how quickly treatment is needed.
Q: What does an NSTEMI look like on an EKG?
A: An NSTEMI EKG may show ST depression or T-wave inversion in a group of leads, but it can also look near-normal. There is no ST elevation, and the diagnosis is confirmed by a rise in troponin.
Q: What is the difference between NSTEMI and unstable angina?
A: Both are acute coronary syndromes without ST elevation. In an NSTEMI the troponin rises, showing heart muscle damage; in unstable angina the troponin stays normal.
Q: Can an NSTEMI turn into a STEMI?
A: Yes. A partly blocked artery can close completely, which is why people with an NSTEMI are monitored closely and have repeat ECGs if pain returns.
