@@LEDE@@
An idioventricular rhythm is a slow rhythm, usually 20–40 beats per minute, generated by the ventricles themselves when no impulse reaches them from above. The QRS complexes are wide and there are no related P waves. The accelerated form (AIVR), at roughly 50–110 per minute, is commonly seen for a short time after a blocked coronary artery is reopened and is usually harmless.
@@BODY@@
## What "Idioventricular rhythm" means

The ventricles are the heart's last-resort pacemaker. If both the sinus node and the AV junction fail, or their impulses are completely blocked, cells in the ventricles fire at their slow built-in rate. Because the impulse spreads cell-to-cell through muscle instead of via the fast wiring, the QRS is wide (over 120 ms) and oddly shaped.

Accelerated idioventricular rhythm arises differently: an irritable ventricular focus briefly runs a little faster than the sinus node. It is classically a "reperfusion rhythm" after treatment for a heart attack, and also occurs with some drugs, electrolyte disturbances and occasionally in healthy athletes.

## What it looks like on the ECG

Regular, wide, bizarre-looking QRS complexes with no P wave in front. Slow (20–40) in the escape form; 50–110 in AIVR, often starting and stopping gradually.

## Common causes

- Complete heart block or sinus arrest (escape form)
- Reperfusion after a heart attack (AIVR)
- Digoxin toxicity, high potassium
- Cardiomyopathy, myocarditis
- After cardiac surgery
- Athletic heart (rare, benign AIVR)

## Should you worry?

A slow idioventricular escape rhythm is unreliable and often does not maintain blood pressure — it is an emergency managed in hospital, usually with pacing. AIVR, in contrast, is usually short-lived, well tolerated and left untreated. The ECG label alone does not distinguish how serious the situation is; the rate and the clinical setting do.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

## What usually happens next

1. In hospital: monitoring, correcting potassium and drug effects, pacing if slow and unstable
2. AIVR after a heart attack: observation only
3. Outpatient finding on a monitor: cardiology review and echocardiogram

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Was this the slow escape form or AIVR?
- How long did it last?
- What caused it, and do I need a pacemaker?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Is idioventricular rhythm the same as ventricular tachycardia?
A: No. They share the wide QRS, but VT is faster than about 120 per minute. AIVR sits between the two and behaves benignly.
Q: Is AIVR dangerous?
A: Rarely. It usually stops within seconds to minutes and treatment can do more harm than good.
Q: Why should AIVR not be suppressed?
A: If it is acting as an escape rhythm, antiarrhythmic drugs could remove the only pacemaker that is working.
