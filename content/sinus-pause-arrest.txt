@@LEDE@@
A sinus pause (or sinus arrest) is a gap in which the heart's natural pacemaker, the sinus node, briefly fails to fire, so no beat is produced for a moment. Short pauses are common and usually harmless, especially during sleep or in athletes. Long pauses, or pauses that cause dizziness or fainting, point to sinus node disease and may need a pacemaker.
@@BODY@@
## What "Sinus pause" means

The sinus node normally fires steadily. If it pauses, there is a flat gap on the ECG with no P wave and no QRS until it restarts or a lower back-up (escape) pacemaker steps in. Brief pauses, especially at night, reflect normal high vagal tone. Longer or frequent pauses suggest the sinus node is diseased — part of what is called sick sinus syndrome.

There is no single cut-off, but pauses under about 2 seconds are usually normal, 2–3 seconds are a grey zone, and pauses over 3 seconds (particularly when awake, or with symptoms) are considered significant.

## What it looks like on the ECG

A sudden flat gap with no P wave or QRS, longer than the normal beat-to-beat interval, followed either by a normal sinus beat or by an escape beat from the junction or ventricle.

## Common causes

- High vagal tone — sleep, athletes, coughing, swallowing, straining
- Age-related degeneration of the sinus node (sick sinus syndrome)
- Beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, digoxin
- Inferior heart attack
- Sleep apnoea, underactive thyroid, high potassium

## Should you worry?

Depends on context

Short pauses in a well person, especially during sleep, are usually harmless. Pauses that cause light-headedness, fainting or a feeling of the heart stopping, or long pauses while awake, need assessment, because they can reflect sinus node disease and sometimes require a pacemaker.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a very slow pulse with dizziness. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

## What usually happens next

1. Review of heart-slowing medicines
2. A Holter or longer-term monitor to capture the pauses and link them to symptoms
3. Blood tests (thyroid, electrolytes) and treatment of sleep apnoea
4. A pacemaker for symptomatic pauses with no reversible cause

### Questions to ask your doctor

- How long were my pauses, and did they occur awake or asleep?
- Could a medicine be responsible?
- Do my symptoms match the pauses on a monitor?
@@FAQ@@
Q: How long a pause is dangerous?
A: There is no exact figure, but pauses over about 3 seconds, especially while awake or with symptoms, are generally considered significant.
Q: Are sinus pauses normal?
A: Short pauses are common and normal, particularly during sleep and in athletes with high vagal tone.
Q: Does a sinus pause need a pacemaker?
A: Only when pauses cause symptoms such as fainting and no reversible cause (like a medicine) can be found.
