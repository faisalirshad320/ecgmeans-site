## What "premature ventricular contraction" means

Your heartbeat normally starts in a small area called the sinus node, which acts as the heart's natural pacemaker. A premature ventricular contraction (PVC) is an early extra beat that starts in the ventricles, the lower pumping chambers of the heart, instead of the sinus node.

PVCs are one of the most common findings on any ECG. They are usually benign, especially in a structurally normal heart. Many people have them without ever noticing. Others feel them as a skipped beat or a brief thump in the chest.

Seeing "PVC" on your report does not mean something is wrong with your heart. It means the recording caught one or more of these early beats. What matters most is how often they happen, what pattern they follow and whether you have symptoms or known heart disease.

## What it looks like on the ECG

On an ECG tracing, a PVC stands out from the normal beats around it. It shows up as:

- An **early** beat that arrives sooner than expected
- A **wide** and oddly shaped QRS complex (the spike that shows the ventricles firing), measuring **120 ms or more**
- **No P wave** before it (the small bump that normally shows the sinus node starting the beat)
- Usually a **full (compensatory) pause** afterwards, before the next normal beat

Reports often name the pattern the PVCs follow:

- **Bigeminy:** a PVC every other beat
- **Trigeminy:** a PVC every third beat
- **Couplets:** two PVCs in a row

You may also see the words **unifocal** or **multifocal**. Unifocal means all the PVCs look alike. Multifocal means they have differing shapes.

## Common causes

- Very common in normal hearts, often with no trigger at all
- Caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, stress and tiredness
- Low potassium or magnesium levels
- An overactive thyroid
- Underlying heart disease (less commonly)

## Should you worry?

Usually harmless

Occasional PVCs are harmless and extremely common, and most people who notice them feel only a skipped beat or a thump. They warrant more attention when they are very frequent, come in runs, are multifocal, or occur alongside fainting, chest pain or known heart disease. If any of those apply to you, talk to your doctor so they can decide whether further checks are needed.

## What usually happens next

For most people, the main step is reassurance. Cutting down on caffeine, alcohol and stress often helps.

If your PVCs are frequent or causing symptoms, your doctor may suggest:

- **A 24-hour heart monitor** to measure your PVC "burden," meaning the share of all your heartbeats that are PVCs
- **Blood tests** to check your electrolytes (such as potassium and magnesium) and your thyroid
- **An echocardiogram** (heart ultrasound) in some cases, to look at the heart's structure and pumping

The burden matters because a 24-hour PVC burden above roughly **10–15%** of all beats can occasionally weaken the heart over time, so it is watched more closely. When the burden is very high, PVCs can be treated with medication or with ablation, a procedure that targets the area causing the extra beats.

Most people with PVCs never need treatment beyond simple lifestyle changes. If you have questions about your own report, your doctor can explain what your result means for you.

Q: What does PVC mean on my ECG report?
A: PVC stands for premature ventricular contraction. It is an early extra beat that starts in the ventricles, the heart's lower chambers, rather than in the sinus node. PVCs are one of the most common findings on any ECG and are usually benign.

They warrant more attention when they are very frequent, come in runs, are multifocal, or occur with fainting, chest pain or known heart disease. Q: What causes PVCs?
A: PVCs are very common in normal hearts and often have no trigger. Caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, stress and tiredness can bring them on. Low potassium or magnesium, an overactive thyroid and, less commonly, underlying heart disease can also cause them.
Q: What happens after PVCs are found on an ECG?
A: Usually the next step is reassurance and cutting down on caffeine, alcohol and stress. If your PVCs are frequent or cause symptoms, your doctor may arrange a 24-hour monitor to measure the burden, blood tests for electrolytes and thyroid, and sometimes an echocardiogram. A very high burden can be treated with medication or ablation.
Q: Can I prevent PVCs?
A: You may be able to reduce them by cutting down on common triggers such as caffeine, alcohol, nicotine and stress, and by getting enough rest. Because PVCs often happen in normal hearts with no trigger at all, they cannot always be prevented. If they are frequent or bothersome, your doctor can check for causes such as low potassium or magnesium or thyroid overactivity.
