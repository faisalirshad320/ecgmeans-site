@@BODY@@
PVCs are among the most searched heart topics for a simple reason: almost everyone has them at some point, and many people feel them. This page explains what PVCs of the heart are, what a PVC looks like on an ECG, whether PVCs are dangerous, what your "PVC burden" percentage means, and what doctors usually do next.

## What "premature ventricular contraction" means

Your heartbeat normally starts in a small area called the sinus node, which acts as the heart's natural pacemaker. A premature ventricular contraction (PVC) is an early extra beat that starts in the ventricles, the lower pumping chambers of the heart, instead of the sinus node.

PVCs are one of the most common findings on any ECG. They are usually benign, especially in a structurally normal heart. Many people have them without ever noticing. Others feel them as a skipped beat or a brief thump in the chest.

Seeing "PVC" on your report does not mean something is wrong with your heart. It means the recording caught one or more of these early beats. What matters most is how often they happen, what pattern they follow and whether you have symptoms or known heart disease.

### What a PVC feels like

The PVC itself often pumps only a little blood, because it arrives before the heart has filled. The pause that follows lets the heart fill extra, so the next normal beat is stronger. That is why people describe:

- A "skipped" or "missed" beat
- A flip-flop, flutter or thud in the chest
- A strong beat after a short pause
- A brief cough or catch in the throat

Many people notice PVCs most when lying on their left side, at night, or when resting after exercise, because there is less going on to distract them.

## What does a PVC look like on an ECG?

On an ECG tracing, a PVC stands out from the normal beats around it. It shows up as:

- An **early** beat that arrives sooner than expected
- A **wide** and oddly shaped QRS complex (the spike that shows the ventricles firing), measuring **120 ms or more**
- **No P wave** before it (the small bump that normally shows the sinus node starting the beat)
- Usually a **full (compensatory) pause** afterwards, before the next normal beat

The T wave after a PVC usually points in the **opposite direction** to the main QRS spike. The wide shape happens because the signal spreads slowly from muscle cell to muscle cell, instead of racing down the heart's fast wiring.

Reports often name the pattern the PVCs follow:

- **Bigeminy:** a PVC every other beat
- **Trigeminy:** a PVC every third beat
- **Couplets:** two PVCs in a row

You may also see the words **unifocal** or **multifocal**. Unifocal means all the PVCs look alike. Multifocal means they have differing shapes.

## Common causes

- Very common in normal hearts, often with no trigger at all
- Caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, stress and tiredness
- Low potassium or magnesium levels
- An overactive thyroid
- Underlying heart disease (less commonly)

Other triggers include dehydration, poor sleep, decongestants and stimulant medicines, and recreational stimulants. Heart conditions linked to frequent PVCs include a previous heart attack, cardiomyopathy, heart valve disease and high blood pressure with a thickened heart muscle ([left ventricular hypertrophy](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-ventricular-hypertrophy/)).

## Are PVCs dangerous?

Occasional PVCs are harmless and extremely common, and most people who notice them feel only a skipped beat or a thump. They warrant more attention when they are very frequent, come in runs, are multifocal, or occur alongside fainting, chest pain or known heart disease. If any of those apply to you, talk to your doctor so they can decide whether further checks are needed.

The question doctors really ask is not "are there PVCs?" but "is the heart underneath normal?" In a heart with normal structure and pumping, PVCs very rarely cause harm. In a heart already weakened by a heart attack or cardiomyopathy, frequent PVCs are a marker that the heart needs closer follow-up.

| Usually reassuring | Worth a closer look |
| --- | --- |
| Occasional PVCs on a routine ECG | Very frequent PVCs (high burden) |
| All PVCs look alike (unifocal) | Several different shapes (multifocal) |
| PVCs disappear when you exercise | PVCs increase or come in runs during exercise |
| Normal echocardiogram | Known heart attack, weak heart muscle or valve disease |
| No family history of sudden death | A relative who died suddenly when young |
| Palpitations only | Fainting, chest pain or severe breathlessness |

**Get urgent help** if extra beats come with chest pain or pressure, fainting or near-fainting, severe breathlessness, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Call 911 (US), 999 (UK) or 112 (EU).

## PVC burden: what the percentage means

PVC burden is the share of all your heartbeats over a recording — usually 24 to 48 hours on a Holter monitor — that are PVCs. Over a day the heart beats roughly 100,000 times, so:

| PVC burden | Roughly how many PVCs per day | What it usually means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under 1% | Fewer than about 1,000 | Common in healthy people; usually no action |
| 1–10% | About 1,000–10,000 | Often benign; depends on symptoms and heart structure |
| Over 10% | More than about 10,000 | High burden; usually checked with an echocardiogram and followed up |
| Over 15–20% | More than about 15,000–20,000 | Higher risk of PVC-related weakening of the heart; treatment may be discussed |

Doctors use these bands as a guide, not a strict cut-off. Some people with a lower burden develop symptoms, and many with a high burden keep a normal heart for years. The reassuring part is that PVC-related heart weakening is usually reversible once the PVCs are controlled.

## What usually happens next

For most people, the main step is reassurance. Cutting down on caffeine, alcohol and stress often helps.

If your PVCs are frequent or causing symptoms, your doctor may suggest:

- **A 24-hour heart monitor** to measure your PVC "burden," meaning the share of all your heartbeats that are PVCs
- **Blood tests** to check your electrolytes (such as potassium and magnesium) and your thyroid
- **An echocardiogram** (heart ultrasound) in some cases, to look at the heart's structure and pumping

The burden matters because a 24-hour PVC burden above roughly **10–15%** of all beats can occasionally weaken the heart over time, so it is watched more closely. When the burden is very high, PVCs can be treated with medication or with ablation, a procedure that targets the area causing the extra beats.

Most people with PVCs never need treatment beyond simple lifestyle changes. If you have questions about your own report, your doctor can explain what your result means for you.

### Treatment options in more detail

- **Lifestyle changes:** less caffeine, alcohol and nicotine, better sleep, staying hydrated and managing stress.
- **Medicines:** your doctor may consider a beta-blocker or a calcium-channel blocker to calm the extra beats when symptoms are troublesome. Other antiarrhythmic medicines are used in selected cases by a specialist.
- **Catheter ablation:** a thin tube is passed through a vein to the heart, and the small spot producing the PVCs is treated with heat or cold. It is usually offered for a high burden, troublesome symptoms despite medicines, or a heart weakened by PVCs.

### Questions to ask your doctor

- What is my PVC burden, and is it high enough to need follow-up?
- Is my heart's structure and pumping normal?
- Are my PVCs all the same shape?
- Could my potassium, magnesium, thyroid or medicines be contributing?
- Do I need treatment, or are lifestyle changes enough?
@@FAQ@@
Q: What does PVC mean on my ECG report?
A: PVC stands for premature ventricular contraction. It is an early extra beat that starts in the ventricles, the heart's lower chambers, rather than in the sinus node. PVCs are one of the most common findings on any ECG and are usually benign.
Q: Are PVCs dangerous?
A: Occasional PVCs are harmless and extremely common, especially in a structurally normal heart. They warrant more attention when they are very frequent, come in runs, are multifocal, or occur with fainting, chest pain or known heart disease. A 24-hour PVC burden above roughly 10–15% of all beats can occasionally weaken the heart over time, so it is watched more closely.
Q: What causes PVCs?
A: PVCs are very common in normal hearts and often have no trigger. Caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, stress and tiredness can bring them on. Low potassium or magnesium, an overactive thyroid and, less commonly, underlying heart disease can also cause them.
Q: What happens after PVCs are found on an ECG?
A: Usually the next step is reassurance and cutting down on caffeine, alcohol and stress. If your PVCs are frequent or cause symptoms, your doctor may arrange a 24-hour monitor to measure the burden, blood tests for electrolytes and thyroid, and sometimes an echocardiogram. A very high burden can be treated with medication or ablation.
Q: Can I prevent PVCs?
A: You may be able to reduce them by cutting down on common triggers such as caffeine, alcohol, nicotine and stress, and by getting enough rest. Because PVCs often happen in normal hearts with no trigger at all, they cannot always be prevented. If they are frequent or bothersome, your doctor can check for causes such as low potassium or magnesium or thyroid overactivity.
Q: How many PVCs a day is normal?
A: Many healthy people have a few dozen to a few hundred PVCs a day without knowing. A burden under about 1% of beats is generally considered low.
Q: Can dehydration cause PVCs?
A: It can make them more likely, partly through shifts in potassium and magnesium and a faster heart rate. Drinking enough fluid is a simple step that sometimes helps.
Q: Why do I feel PVCs more at night?
A: At rest the heart rate slows, leaving longer gaps in which early beats can appear, and there is less to distract you. Lying on the left side also brings the heart closer to the chest wall, so beats feel stronger.
