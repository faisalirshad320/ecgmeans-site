@@LEDE@@
S1Q3T3 describes a combination of three findings — an S wave in lead I, a Q wave in lead III, and an inverted T wave in lead III. It is best known as a textbook sign of a pulmonary embolism (a clot in the lungs), but it is neither sensitive nor specific: most people with a PE do not have it, and it can appear in healthy people. It is a prompt to think about PE in the right setting, not a diagnosis.
@@BODY@@
## What "s1q3t3" means

A large or sudden clot in the lung arteries strains the right side of the heart. That acute right-heart strain can shift the electrical forces, producing an S wave in lead I and a new Q wave with T wave inversion in lead III. Put together, that is the S1Q3T3 pattern.

The problem is reliability. Only a minority of people with a proven PE show S1Q3T3, and the same pattern turns up in healthy people, in lung disease and with body position. The commonest ECG finding in PE is actually just a fast heart rate (sinus tachycardia).

## What it looks like on the ECG

A prominent S wave in lead I, a Q wave in lead III, and an inverted T wave in lead III. Other signs of right-heart strain — a new right bundle branch block, T wave inversion in V1–V3, or right axis deviation — may accompany it.

## Common causes

- Acute pulmonary embolism (the association it is known for)
- Other causes of sudden right-heart strain
- A normal variant or body position (it can be present with no disease)
- Chronic lung disease

## Should you worry?

Needs clinician review

On its own, in a well person with no symptoms, S1Q3T3 is often meaningless. But if it is new and you have sudden breathlessness, chest pain (especially sharp pain worse on breathing in), a fast heart rate or a swollen leg, it must be taken seriously as a possible pulmonary embolism, which is a medical emergency.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a one-sided swollen, painful leg. Call your local emergency number — a pulmonary embolism is time-critical.

## What usually happens next

1. Assessment of PE risk and symptoms
2. A D-dimer blood test and, if needed, a CT pulmonary angiogram
3. Oxygen levels, and an echocardiogram to look at the right heart
4. Treatment with blood thinners if a PE is confirmed

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Is this pattern new, and could it mean a clot in my lungs?
- Do I need a D-dimer or a CT scan?
- Are there other signs of right-heart strain on my ECG?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Does S1Q3T3 always mean pulmonary embolism?
A: No. Most people with it do not have a PE, and most people with a PE do not show it. It is only a clue in the right clinical setting.
Q: What is the most common ECG finding in a pulmonary embolism?
A: A fast heart rate (sinus tachycardia), not S1Q3T3.
Q: Can S1Q3T3 be normal?
A: Yes, it can appear in healthy people and with body position, which is why it is interpreted alongside symptoms.
