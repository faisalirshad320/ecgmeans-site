A normal PR interval is **120–200 ms** (0.12–0.20 seconds), or 3–5 small squares on standard ECG paper. It is the time from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS — how long the impulse takes to travel from the atria, through the AV node, to the ventricles. @@BODY@@
## What is a normal PR interval?

The normal PR interval in adults is 120–200 milliseconds. On a standard ECG printed at 25 mm per second, that is 3–5 small boxes, or up to one large box. It is one of the first numbers printed at the top of an ECG report, usually labelled "PR" or "PR int," and the machine measures it automatically.

A normal PR interval tells your doctor that the electrical signal left the atria, paused for the right length of time at the AV node, and reached the ventricles on schedule.

## What "PR interval normal range" means

Most of the PR interval is the deliberate pause at the AV node, which gives the atria time to empty into the ventricles. Anything that slows the node lengthens the PR: high vagal tone, age, beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers and digoxin. Anything that bypasses it shortens the PR — most notably an extra conducting strand (accessory pathway), as in Wolff-Parkinson-White.

Your ECG report prints the PR as a number in ms near the top of the page. The machine measures it automatically and reasonably reliably.

### PR interval normal range at a glance

| PR interval | Small boxes (25 mm/s) | Seconds | What it usually means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Under 120 ms** | Fewer than 3 | Under 0.12 s | Short PR — check for a delta wave (WPW) or a junctional rhythm; can be a normal variant |
| **120–200 ms** | 3–5 | 0.12–0.20 s | Normal |
| **201–300 ms** | More than 5, up to 7.5 | 0.20–0.30 s | First degree AV block — usually harmless |
| **Over 300 ms** | More than 7.5 | Over 0.30 s | Marked first degree AV block — more likely to cause symptoms |
| **Changing beat to beat** | Varies | Varies | Look for second degree AV block (Wenckebach) or AV dissociation |

## What it looks like on the ECG

The time from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS complex: the P wave plus the flat PR segment that follows it. Normal: 3–5 small squares. Prolonged: more than one large square. Short: less than 3 small squares.

## How to measure the PR interval

### How do you measure the PR interval in small boxes?

Count the small boxes from the very start of the P wave to the very start of the QRS complex, then multiply by 40: on standard ECG paper running at 25 mm/s, each small box is 40 ms (0.04 s) and each large box of five small boxes is 200 ms (0.20 s).

- 3 small boxes = 120 ms (0.12 s), the lower limit of normal.
- 4 small boxes = 160 ms (0.16 s), the middle of the normal range.
- 5 small boxes, or one large box = 200 ms (0.20 s), the upper limit of normal.

Two common mistakes: the PR interval includes the P wave itself, not only the flat line after it (that flat part is the PR segment), and it should be measured in a lead where the start of the P wave is easy to see. In normal sinus rhythm the PR interval stays the same from beat to beat, so a PR that changes between beats is worth pointing out to a clinician.

### Step-by-step method

1. **Check the paper speed.** It should be 25 mm/s (printed on the strip). At 50 mm/s each small box is only 20 ms.
2. **Choose a lead with a clear P wave.** Lead II is usually best.
3. **Find the very first upward (or downward) movement of the P wave** away from the baseline.
4. **Find the very first movement of the QRS** — this may be a small Q wave or the start of the R wave.
5. **Count the small boxes between those two points** and multiply by 40 to get milliseconds.
6. **Repeat on two or three beats.** In sinus rhythm the number should stay the same.

Many people find it easier to practise on real tracings — see our [EKG practice strips](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ekg-practice-strips/) and [how to read an EKG strip](https://www.ecgmeans.com/how-to-read-an-ekg-strip/).

### PR interval vs PR segment

| | PR interval | PR segment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Starts at** | Beginning of the P wave | End of the P wave |
| **Ends at** | Beginning of the QRS | Beginning of the QRS |
| **Includes the P wave?** | Yes | No |
| **Used for** | AV conduction time (normal 120–200 ms) | Spotting PR depression, for example in [pericarditis](https://www.ecgmeans.com/pericarditis-ecg/) |

## The PR interval and heart rate

The PR interval shortens a little as the heart rate rises and lengthens a little at slow rates, because the AV node conducts faster when adrenaline is high. That is why a PR of 210 ms in a sleeping athlete can be completely normal, and why a child's PR is read against charts for heart rate as well as age.

## What is a normal PR interval in children?

Children have shorter PR intervals than adults, so a PR under 120 ms can be normal in a child, and the upper limit of normal rises with age from about 110–120 ms in newborns to about 190 ms in teenagers. Approximate upper limits from a children's hospital reference chart:

- Newborns (first month): about 110–120 ms
- 1 month to 3 years: about 140–150 ms
- 3 to 12 years: about 160–180 ms
- 12 to 16 years: about 180–190 ms
- Adults: 200 ms, occasionally up to about 210 ms at slow heart rates

The PR interval also shortens as the heart rate speeds up, so a child's ECG is read against charts for both age and heart rate, and reference ranges vary slightly between sources. A long PR in a child can be a normal variant, but it is also seen with myocarditis, some medicines, congenital heart disease, high potassium and rheumatic fever. A short PR should prompt a check for the delta wave of Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW).

## Prolonged PR interval: what it means

A prolonged PR interval — over 200 ms in an adult — is called [first degree AV block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/first-degree-av-block/). Despite the name, nothing is actually blocked: every signal still gets through, just a little slowly. Most people with a mildly prolonged PR have no symptoms and need no treatment.

### Common causes

- Prolonged: athletic vagal tone, ageing, rate-slowing medicines, inferior infarct, myocarditis
- Short: WPW or other accessory pathway, junctional rhythm, some normal variants, glycogen storage disease (rare)

### What PR interval is too long or dangerous?

A PR interval over 200 ms is first degree AV block, which is usually harmless on its own; a PR over 300 ms is called marked first degree AV block and is the point at which symptoms become more likely.

- **201–300 ms:** generally well tolerated and common in fit young people, because of high vagal tone. It has been found in more than 1 in 10 young athletes. Usually no treatment is needed.
- **Over 300 ms:** the upper and lower chambers can fall out of step, which may cause tiredness, breathlessness or light-headedness, similar to "pacemaker syndrome".
- **A PR that lengthens beat by beat until a beat is dropped:** this is second degree AV block, Mobitz type I (Wenckebach). It is usually benign, but it is a different diagnosis from a simple long PR.

Besides medicines and vagal tone, doctors look for treatable causes of a long PR, including low potassium or magnesium and infections such as Lyme disease, rheumatic fever and endocarditis. The outlook for isolated first degree AV block is generally good, but it is linked to a higher long-term risk of atrial fibrillation and of progressing to more advanced heart block, so it is worth comparing with future ECGs. Seek urgent care if a long PR comes with fainting, chest pain or a very slow pulse.

For the beat-by-beat lengthening pattern, see [second degree AV block type 1](https://www.ecgmeans.com/second-degree-av-block-type-1/).

## Short PR interval: what it means

A PR interval under 120 ms means the signal is reaching the ventricles early. In adults the most important cause is an accessory pathway that bypasses the AV node. If the start of the QRS is slurred (a [delta wave](https://www.ecgmeans.com/delta-wave/)), the pattern is [Wolff-Parkinson-White](https://www.ecgmeans.com/wolff-parkinson-white/). A short PR without a delta wave is often a normal variant. See our dedicated [short PR interval](https://www.ecgmeans.com/short-pr-interval/) page for more detail.

## Should you worry?

A mildly prolonged PR with no symptoms is benign. A short PR with a delta wave (WPW) or with episodes of racing heart needs cardiology review. A very long PR (over 300 ms) or one that is progressively lengthening also deserves a closer look.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

## What usually happens next

1. None for a mildly prolonged PR without symptoms
2. Medication review if the PR is long
3. Cardiology referral for WPW pattern, especially with palpitations

### Questions to ask your doctor

- What is my PR interval in ms?
- Is there a delta wave?
- Is it stable compared with earlier ECGs?
- Could any of my medicines be lengthening it?
@@FAQ@@
Q: What is a normal PR interval?
A: 120–200 milliseconds, or 3–5 small squares on standard paper.
Q: What does a prolonged PR interval mean?
A: First degree AV block — a slower-than-average passage through the AV node, usually harmless.
Q: What does a short PR interval mean?
A: The impulse is reaching the ventricles early, most importantly via an accessory pathway. If there is also a slurred upstroke to the QRS (delta wave), it is Wolff-Parkinson-White pattern.
Q: What is the normal PR interval in seconds?
A: 0.12 to 0.20 seconds. Each small box on standard ECG paper is 0.04 seconds.
Q: Where do you measure the PR interval from?
A: From the very start of the P wave to the very start of the QRS complex, ideally in lead II, where the P wave is usually clearest.
Q: Can a prolonged PR interval go back to normal?
A: Yes. A long PR caused by a medicine, high vagal tone, low potassium or an infection such as Lyme disease often shortens once the cause is addressed.
