@@LEDE@@
Second degree AV block type 2 — Mobitz II — is a pattern in which some impulses from the atria are suddenly not conducted to the ventricles, with no warning lengthening of the PR interval beforehand. The block sits below the AV node in the bundle branches, which makes it unpredictable and liable to progress to complete heart block. It is one of the ECG findings that usually leads to a pacemaker.
@@BODY@@
Second degree AV block type 2 goes by several names on reports and in clinic letters: *Mobitz type 2*, *Mobitz II*, *second degree heart block type 2*, *2nd degree heart block type 2*, or occasionally *Hay block*. All describe the same rhythm. This page explains what it means, how it looks on an ECG, how it differs from the much more benign type 1, and what treatment usually involves.

## What "2nd degree AV block type 2" means

Unlike Wenckebach, the conduction system here is not tiring gradually; it is intermittently failing outright. The PR interval of the conducted beats is constant, and then a P wave simply has no QRS after it. Because the disease is in the His-Purkinje system, the QRS is often wide and any escape rhythm that takes over is slow and unreliable.

When every second P wave is blocked (2:1 block) it can be hard to tell Type 1 from Type 2 on a single strip; a longer recording or a cardiologist's review settles it.

### Where the block is

The heart's wiring runs from the sinus node, through the AV node, into the bundle of His, and then down the right and left bundle branches. In type 1 block the hold-up is usually in the AV node itself, which tends to recover. In Mobitz type 2 the fault is lower — in or below the bundle of His. That part of the system has no gradual warning phase: it either conducts or it does not. About three in four cases are below the bundle of His and show a wide QRS; the rest are within it and can have a narrow QRS.

## Mobitz type 2 on the ECG: what it looks like

Regular P waves; most are followed by a QRS with a constant PR, but occasionally a P wave stands alone with no QRS. The QRS is frequently wide.

Checklist for a second degree heart block type 2 ECG:

1. **P waves arrive at a regular rate** and march through the strip.
2. **The PR interval of every conducted beat is the same** — it does not lengthen before the dropped beat, and it does not shorten after it.
3. **Suddenly, a P wave is not followed by a QRS** (a "dropped beat").
4. **The pause around the dropped beat is exactly twice** the normal beat-to-beat interval.
5. **The QRS is often wide**, because many people also have a [bundle branch block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-bundle-branch-block/) or [bifascicular block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/bifascicular-block/).

Reports sometimes describe the ratio of P waves to QRS complexes — for example 3:2 (three P waves, two conducted) or 4:3. When two or more P waves in a row are blocked, the term **high-grade AV block** is used, and it is treated just as seriously.

## Second degree AV block type 1 vs type 2

This is the comparison that matters most, because the two types carry very different risks.

| Feature | Type 1 (Mobitz I, Wenckebach) | Type 2 (Mobitz II) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PR interval | Lengthens beat by beat until a beat is dropped | Stays the same before and after the dropped beat |
| Dropped beats | Predictable, after a warning | Sudden, no warning |
| Usual site of block | AV node | Bundle of His or bundle branches |
| QRS width | Usually narrow | Often wide |
| Seen in healthy people? | Yes — athletes, during sleep | Rarely, if ever |
| Risk of complete heart block | Low | Substantial |
| Pacemaker | Only if it causes symptoms or is below the AV node | Usually recommended, even without symptoms |

## Symptoms of 2nd degree heart block type 2

Some people have no symptoms and the rhythm is found on a routine ECG. Others notice:

- Dizziness or light-headedness
- Fainting or near-fainting
- Tiredness and reduced exercise tolerance
- Breathlessness
- A feeling of missed or skipped beats
- Chest discomfort

Symptoms are more likely when several beats in a row are dropped and the heart rate falls.

## Common causes

- Degenerative fibrosis of the conduction system (most common, with age)
- Anterior heart attack
- Cardiomyopathy, sarcoidosis, amyloidosis
- After heart surgery or valve procedures
- Drug toxicity (less common than in Type 1)

Other causes your team may check for include high potassium ([hyperkalemia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/hyperkalemia-ecg/)), Lyme disease, inflammation of the heart muscle, and conduction damage after a TAVI (transcatheter valve) procedure.

## Should you worry?

Mobitz II is treated as a warning sign. It can progress without notice to complete heart block, which may cause collapse. People with this finding are normally assessed promptly, monitored, and referred for a permanent pacemaker even if they currently have no symptoms.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase. A very slow pulse with dizziness or confusion is also a reason to call your local emergency number.

## Treatment: the pacemaker

US guidelines from the American College of Cardiology, American Heart Association and Heart Rhythm Society recommend a permanent pacemaker for acquired Mobitz type II second degree AV block that is not due to a reversible or physiological cause — regardless of symptoms. The reason is the risk of sudden progression to [third degree (complete) heart block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/third-degree-av-block/).

While waiting for a pacemaker, the team may keep you on a heart monitor and have external pacing pads ready. Medicines that speed the AV node, such as atropine, often help little in Mobitz II, because the block is below the node.

A pacemaker is fitted under the skin below the collarbone, usually under local anesthetic, and most people go home within a day or so. Afterwards your ECG will show a [paced rhythm](https://www.ecgmeans.com/paced-rhythm/) whenever the device is working.

## What usually happens next

1. Same-day medical assessment if this is a new finding
2. Continuous monitoring in hospital if symptomatic
3. Echocardiogram and blood tests for reversible causes
4. Permanent pacemaker in most cases

Your doctor will also review your medicines. Drugs that slow conduction — such as beta-blockers, some calcium-channel blockers, digoxin and certain antiarrhythmics — may be adjusted by your team.

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Am I being referred for a pacemaker?
- Is there any reversible cause?
- What should I do if I feel faint before the procedure?
- Is my QRS wide, and do I also have a bundle branch block?
- Could this be 2:1 block that needs a longer recording to classify?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Is Mobitz 2 an emergency?
A: It is urgent rather than always an emergency. If you have fainted or feel faint, it should be treated as an emergency.
Q: Can Mobitz 2 be cured?
A: Not usually, because it reflects scarring of the conduction system. A pacemaker corrects the problem reliably.
Q: Can I drive with Mobitz 2?
A: Rules vary by country; many require you to stop driving until a pacemaker is fitted. Ask your clinician.
Q: Is second degree heart block type 2 worse than type 1?
A: Yes. Type 1 is often benign and seen in healthy people, while type 2 reflects disease lower in the conduction system and can progress suddenly to complete heart block.
Q: Can Mobitz type 2 turn into third degree heart block?
A: Yes, and it can happen without warning. That risk is the main reason a pacemaker is recommended even when there are no symptoms.
Q: What is high-grade AV block?
A: It is a form of second degree block in which two or more P waves in a row are not conducted. It is managed in the same way as Mobitz II.
