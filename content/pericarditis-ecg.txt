@@LEDE@@
Pericarditis is inflammation of the sac around the heart. On the ECG it classically produces widespread, saucer-shaped (concave) ST elevation across many leads, together with PR-segment depression. The key task for a clinician is to separate it from a heart attack, which also raises the ST segment but in a different pattern. The ECG is only part of the picture, alongside symptoms and blood tests.
@@BODY@@
## What "pericarditis" means on the ECG

When the lining around the heart is inflamed, the irritation affects the electrical recording from a wide area rather than from one artery's territory. That is why pericarditis causes ST elevation in most leads at once, instead of the grouped, one-territory pattern of a heart attack. PR-segment depression — the short segment just before the QRS dipping down — is a helpful additional sign.

Classic pericarditis evolves through stages over days to weeks: widespread ST elevation and PR depression first, then the ST segments return to normal, then the T waves flatten and invert, then the ECG recovers.

## What it looks like on the ECG

Widespread concave ("smiley") ST elevation in most leads, PR-segment depression (and PR elevation in lead aVR), and no reciprocal ST depression of the kind seen in a heart attack. The changes are diffuse rather than confined to one region.

## Common causes

- Viral infection (the commonest cause)
- After a heart attack or heart surgery
- Autoimmune conditions such as lupus and rheumatoid arthritis
- Kidney failure (uraemia)
- Some medicines, and occasionally cancer or tuberculosis

## Should you worry?

Pericarditis is usually treatable and often settles with anti-inflammatory medicines, but chest pain always needs assessment, because the ECG changes can resemble a heart attack and because a large build-up of fluid around the heart (pericardial effusion or tamponade) can be dangerous. Pericarditis pain is classically sharp, worse lying flat and better sitting forward.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

## What usually happens next

1. Clinical assessment and comparison with the heart-attack pattern
2. Blood tests (inflammatory markers, troponin) and an echocardiogram to look for fluid
3. Treatment of the cause, usually with anti-inflammatory medicines and colchicine
4. Monitoring for a pericardial effusion

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Are you sure this is pericarditis and not a heart attack?
- Is there any fluid around my heart on the echo?
- What is causing the inflammation, and how is it treated?
@@FAQ@@
Q: How do doctors tell pericarditis from a heart attack on an ECG?
A: Pericarditis causes widespread concave ST elevation with PR depression and no reciprocal changes; a heart attack causes ST elevation in one artery's leads, often with reciprocal depression elsewhere. Symptoms and troponin help confirm.
Q: Is pericarditis serious?
A: It is usually treatable, but it needs assessment because it mimics a heart attack and can be complicated by fluid around the heart.
Q: What does PR depression mean?
A: A downward shift of the short segment before the QRS; it is a supportive sign of pericarditis.
