## What "Accelerated idioventricular rhythm" means

Normally, each heartbeat starts in a small natural pacemaker in the upper part of the heart called the sinus node. In **accelerated idioventricular rhythm (AIVR)**, a pacemaker site in the ventricles, the lower pumping chambers, takes over and sets the pace instead.

The word "accelerated" is used because this rhythm runs faster than the normal ventricular escape rhythm, which is the heart's slow built-in backup. However, it is still slower than **ventricular tachycardia (VT)**, a fast and potentially dangerous rhythm from the same chambers. AIVR sits in between the two.

AIVR is classically seen as a benign **"reperfusion rhythm"**. This means it often appears after a blocked coronary artery reopens, either on its own or after treatment. In that setting, doctors often take it as a sign that blood flow has returned to the heart muscle.

## What it looks like on the ECG

On an ECG, AIVR shows up as a **regular, wide-QRS rhythm**. The QRS complex is the spike on the tracing that shows the ventricles being activated. In AIVR it is **≥120 ms** wide. That is wider than usual because the electrical signal starts in the ventricle and spreads more slowly than it does through the heart's normal wiring.

Key numbers:

- **Heart rate: 40-120 bpm**
- This is by definition faster than the **~20-40 bpm** ventricular escape rate
- It is slower than the **>120 bpm** seen in ventricular tachycardia
- **QRS width: ≥120 ms**

AIVR often appears when the normal sinus rate slows down and the ventricular focus takes over. At the start and end of an episode, the ECG commonly shows **fusion beats** and **capture beats**. These are beats where the normal rhythm briefly mixes with, or takes back control from, the ventricular rhythm. They are a typical feature of AIVR and help doctors recognise it.

## Common causes

- **Reperfusion after a heart attack** (treated or spontaneous). This is the classic setting.
- **The time just after thrombolysis or angioplasty**, which are treatments used to reopen a blocked artery
- **Digoxin toxicity**, from too much of the heart medicine digoxin
- **Cardiomyopathy or myocarditis**, which are diseases or inflammation of the heart muscle
- **Occasionally in healthy athletes with high vagal tone**, where a naturally slow resting heart rate lets the ventricular rhythm show through

## Should you worry?

Depends on context

AIVR itself is usually benign and self-limiting. The rate is not fast enough to compromise the circulation, and it rarely needs treatment. What matters most is the situation it appears in. After a blocked artery has been reopened, it is generally reassuring. However, the underlying heart attack, cardiomyopathy or other cause still needs proper care and follow-up.

## What usually happens next

In most cases, AIVR is **simply observed** rather than treated. Doctors usually avoid trying to suppress it with medication. While it is happening, it may be the only rhythm keeping the heart pumping, so stopping it could be harmful.

Instead, the focus is on the **underlying cause**. For example, that could mean managing recovery after a heart attack, checking for digoxin toxicity, or assessing and treating a heart muscle condition.

Your doctors will also carefully distinguish AIVR from true **ventricular tachycardia**. Both produce wide QRS complexes, but VT is faster and dangerous. The heart rate, and features such as fusion and capture beats as the rhythm starts and stops, help tell them apart. If you have questions about your own ECG, your care team can explain what it means in your particular situation.
@@FAQ@@
Q: What does accelerated idioventricular rhythm mean on my ECG?
A: It means a pacemaker site in your ventricles, the heart's lower chambers, was setting your heart rate instead of the usual sinus node. The rate is typically 40-120 bpm. That is faster than the heart's normal backup escape rhythm but slower than ventricular tachycardia. It is classically seen after a blocked coronary artery reopens.
Q: Is accelerated idioventricular rhythm dangerous?
A: AIVR is usually benign and self-limiting because its rate is not fast enough to compromise the circulation. It rarely needs treatment. The context matters most. It is reassuring after reperfusion, but any underlying heart attack or cardiomyopathy still needs care.
Q: What causes accelerated idioventricular rhythm?
A: The classic cause is reperfusion, when blood flow returns after a heart attack, either on its own or after treatment such as thrombolysis or angioplasty. Other causes include digoxin toxicity, cardiomyopathy and myocarditis. It can occasionally be seen in healthy athletes with high vagal tone.
Q: Will I need treatment for AIVR?
A: Usually not. AIVR is normally just observed, and suppressing it can be harmful because it may be the only rhythm maintaining the heart's output. Treatment focuses on the underlying cause rather than the rhythm itself.
Q: How is AIVR different from ventricular tachycardia?
A: Both rhythms start in the ventricles and show wide QRS complexes of 120 ms or more. AIVR runs at 40-120 bpm and is usually benign. Ventricular tachycardia is faster, above 120 bpm, and dangerous. Doctors distinguish between the two carefully.
