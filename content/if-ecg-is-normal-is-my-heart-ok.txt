It cannot see blocked arteries that are not currently causing damage, mild valve disease, or a rhythm problem that comes and goes. Whether your heart is OK depends on what question is being asked. This page explains what a normal ECG (EKG) does and does not tell you, which symptoms mean the assessment should continue, and which tests answer the questions an ECG cannot.
@@BODY@@
## The short answer

The ECG is very good at what it does — recording the heart's electrical activity at one moment. If you feel well and your doctor finds nothing on examination, a normal ECG is good news and usually all that is needed.

The limits come from what the test measures. Most coronary artery disease produces no ECG change at rest; in people with stable angina, the resting ECG is often completely normal — around half in many series. Palpitations that are not happening during the recording leave no trace. Valves and the pumping strength of the heart are only seen indirectly, if at all.

That is why doctors match the test to the question: a stress test or CT angiogram for blood supply, an echocardiogram for structure and valves, a Holter or event monitor for intermittent rhythm problems.

## What a "normal ECG" actually means

A report that reads "Normal sinus rhythm — Normal ECG" means the computer and the reader found:

| Measurement | Normal adult range | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heart rhythm | Sinus rhythm | Each beat starts in the heart's natural pacemaker |
| Heart rate | About 60–100 bpm (50s often normal at rest) | Not too fast or too slow at that moment |
| PR interval | 120–200 ms | Normal conduction from upper to lower chambers |
| QRS duration | Under 120 ms | No bundle branch block or wide-complex rhythm |
| QTc | Roughly under 450 ms (men), under 460–470 ms (women) | Normal recovery time of the ventricles |
| Axis | Roughly −30° to +90° | No major shift in the heart's electrical direction |
| ST segments and T waves | No significant elevation, depression or inversion | No sign of acute injury or strain at that moment |
| Q waves | No pathological Q waves | No obvious old heart attack |

Our page on [normal ECG values](https://www.ecgmeans.com/normal-ecg-values/) explains each of these in more detail.

## What a normal ECG can and cannot rule out

| A normal ECG makes these unlikely | A normal ECG does NOT exclude these |
| --- | --- |
| A large heart attack happening right now (but not every one — see below) | Stable narrowing of the coronary arteries |
| A serious rhythm problem at the time of the recording | Palpitations or arrhythmias that come and go |
| Complete or advanced heart block during the recording | Mild to moderate valve disease |
| Marked thickening of the heart muscle in many cases | Early cardiomyopathy or heart failure with preserved pumping strength |
| Major electrolyte disturbances such as very high potassium | High blood pressure damage in its early stages |
| | Some inherited rhythm conditions that appear only intermittently |

Our guides to [what an EKG can detect](https://www.ecgmeans.com/what-can-an-ekg-detect/) and [whether an EKG shows blockage](https://www.ecgmeans.com/does-an-ekg-show-blockage/) cover these limits in more depth.

## Can you have a heart attack with a normal ECG?

Yes. A small proportion of heart attacks show a normal or near-normal first ECG, especially in the first hours, or when the affected area is one the standard 12 leads see poorly, such as the back wall of the heart. Some heart attacks are of the NSTEMI type, where the ECG may show only subtle changes.

That is why emergency teams with a patient who has chest pain do not rely on one ECG. They use:

- **Repeat ECGs** every 15–30 minutes if pain continues, to catch changes as they develop
- **Blood troponin tests**, often repeated over a few hours, to detect heart muscle damage
- **Extra leads** when a hidden area is suspected

More on this in [can an EKG detect a heart attack?](https://www.ecgmeans.com/can-an-ekg-detect-a-heart-attack/)

## Conditions that can hide behind a normal ECG

- **Intermittent arrhythmias** such as paroxysmal atrial fibrillation or SVT, which are only seen when they happen. See [heart palpitations with a normal ECG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/heart-palpitations-normal-ecg/).
- **Inherited rhythm conditions:** the [Brugada syndrome](https://www.ecgmeans.com/brugada-syndrome/) pattern can come and go, pre-excitation in [Wolff-Parkinson-White](https://www.ecgmeans.com/wolff-parkinson-white/) can be intermittent, and some people with long QT have a QTc close to normal on a single tracing (see [QT prolongation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/qt-prolongation/)).
- **Cardiomyopathies:** most people with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy have an abnormal ECG, but not all; early arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy may show only subtle signs such as an [epsilon wave](https://www.ecgmeans.com/epsilon-wave/), or none.
- **Valve disease:** usually found by a murmur and confirmed by echocardiogram — see [EKG vs echocardiogram](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ekg-vs-echocardiogram/).
- **Stable coronary artery disease:** often silent on a resting ECG until the heart is stressed.

## Normal vs "borderline" or "nonspecific" results

Sometimes the report does not say "Normal ECG" but something close, such as "borderline ECG" or "nonspecific ST-T changes". These are not the same as normal, but they are often harmless. See [borderline ECG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/borderline-ecg/) and [nonspecific ST abnormality](https://www.ecgmeans.com/nonspecific-st-abnormality/) for what those phrases mean.

## When a normal ECG should not end the assessment

If you have no symptoms, a normal ECG plus a normal examination is good news. If you have any of the following, a normal ECG should not end the assessment; the right further test depends on the symptom:

- Chest tightness, pressure or pain **on exertion** that settles with rest
- **Breathlessness** that is new or out of proportion to activity
- **Fainting**, especially during exercise or without warning
- **Palpitations** that last minutes, recur, or come with dizziness
- A **family history** of sudden death at a young age or an inherited heart condition
- **Swollen ankles**, unexplained tiredness or reduced exercise ability

## What tests usually come next

| Your concern | Usual next test | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Symptoms on exertion | Exercise stress test, stress echo or CT coronary angiogram | Looks at blood supply under stress or directly at the arteries |
| Palpitations | Holter, patch or event monitor; smartwatch ECG recordings | Captures rhythms that come and go |
| Murmur, breathlessness or swelling | Echocardiogram, sometimes a BNP blood test | Shows the valves, chambers and pumping strength |
| Fainting | Monitoring, echocardiogram, sometimes exercise testing | Looks for rhythm and structural causes |
| Family history of inherited conditions | Specialist review, echo, repeat ECGs, genetic testing | Some patterns are intermittent |
| Risk factors only | Blood pressure, cholesterol and diabetes checks | Risk is managed regardless of the ECG |

## Special groups

- **Athletes:** a normal pre-participation ECG is reassuring, and many athletes have harmless training-related changes. A normal ECG does not replace assessment of symptoms or family history.
- **Pregnancy:** ECG changes such as a faster rate and a slight axis shift are normal in pregnancy. A normal ECG is reassuring, but new breathlessness or chest pain still needs review.
- **Children:** normal values differ with age, so a children's ECG is read against age-specific ranges.
- **Older adults and pre-operative checks:** a normal ECG is a useful baseline for comparison later, but does not by itself say how well the heart will cope with surgery.

## Get urgent help

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase. Do not let a normal ECG earlier in the day stop you from calling 911 (999 in the UK, 112 in Europe) if new chest pain starts.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Does a normal ECG answer the question I came with?
- Do my symptoms need a different test, such as an echo, monitor or stress test?
- Should the ECG be repeated if my symptoms come back?
- What is my overall cardiovascular risk, and what should I do about it?
- Does my family history mean I need further screening?
@@FAQ@@
Q: If my ECG is normal, is my heart healthy?
A: A normal ECG is reassuring for rhythm, conduction and acute damage at the time of the test. It does not rule out narrowed arteries, valve disease or problems that come and go, so symptoms may still need other tests.
Q: Can you have a heart attack with a normal ECG?
A: Yes. A small proportion of heart attacks show a normal initial ECG, which is why blood troponin tests and repeat ECGs are used.
Q: Can a normal ECG rule out blocked arteries?
A: No. A resting ECG is often normal in stable coronary disease; a stress test or CT coronary angiogram is used when blockage is suspected.
Q: Why do I have palpitations if my ECG is normal?
A: A standard ECG records only about 10 seconds. If palpitations are not happening during the test, a Holter, patch or event monitor is needed to catch them.
Q: Can an ECG be normal with heart failure?
A: It can, though it is uncommon in established heart failure. An echocardiogram and a BNP blood test are more reliable ways to check for it.
Q: What heart problems can a normal ECG miss?
A: Intermittent arrhythmias, stable coronary artery disease, mild valve disease, early cardiomyopathy and some inherited rhythm conditions whose patterns come and go.
Q: How often should I have an ECG?
A: There is no fixed schedule for healthy people. It is done when symptoms, risk factors, medicines or a procedure call for it.
Q: Is a normal EKG the same as a normal echocardiogram?
A: No. An EKG records electrical activity; an echocardiogram is an ultrasound that shows the heart's structure, valves and pumping strength.
