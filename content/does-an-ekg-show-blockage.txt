@@LEDE@@
"Blockage" is one of the words people fear most after a heart test. It usually refers to coronary artery disease, where fatty plaque narrows the arteries that feed the heart muscle. The EKG is often the first heart test anyone has, so it is natural to ask whether it would have found a blockage. The short answer is that a resting EKG is a poor tool for that job, and a normal one does not mean your arteries are clear. This page explains why, what the EKG can hint at, how "heart block" is a different thing altogether, and which tests actually look for narrowed arteries.
@@BODY@@
## Does an EKG show blockage?

Usually not directly. An EKG (also written ECG; it is the same test) records the electrical signals that make the heart beat. It does not take a picture of the arteries, and it does not measure blood flow.

A coronary artery can be narrowed by 50%, 70% or even more and still supply enough blood when you are sitting still. The heart muscle is not short of oxygen at rest, so its electrical signals look normal. The problem only shows when the heart has to work harder, such as during exercise or stress. A resting EKG, done lying down for ten seconds, simply does not test that.

So:

- **A normal resting EKG does not rule out a blockage.** Many people with significant coronary artery disease have a normal resting EKG.
- **An abnormal EKG does not prove a blockage.** Many EKG changes have other causes.

## Two different meanings of "blockage"

This is a common mix-up, and it matters.

| Term | What it means | Does an EKG show it? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Artery blockage** (coronary artery disease) | Plaque narrowing the blood vessels that supply the heart muscle — a plumbing problem | Usually not at rest. May show indirect clues |
| **Heart block** (AV block, bundle branch block) | A delay or interruption in the heart's electrical wiring — an electrical problem | Yes. The EKG is the main test for heart block |

If your report says "first degree AV block", "right bundle branch block" or "left anterior fascicular block", that is an electrical finding. It does not mean a blocked artery. See [first degree AV block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/first-degree-av-block/), [right bundle branch block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/right-bundle-branch-block/) and [left bundle branch block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-bundle-branch-block/) for what these mean.

The two can be linked. A heart attack can damage the conduction system and cause heart block, and a new left bundle branch block can be a sign of heart disease. But the words describe different problems.

## Can an EKG detect a blockage at all? The clues it can give

A resting EKG can sometimes point toward coronary artery disease. These are clues, not proof:

- **Signs of a past heart attack.** [Pathological Q waves](https://www.ecgmeans.com/pathological-q-waves/) suggest an area of heart muscle that died after an artery blocked in the past.
- **ST depression.** A dip in the ST segment can mean reduced blood supply, especially if it appears during chest pain. See [ST depression](https://www.ecgmeans.com/st-depression/).
- **T wave inversion.** Deep, symmetrical inverted T waves in a group of leads can reflect reduced blood flow. A specific pattern called [Wellens syndrome](https://www.ecgmeans.com/wellens-syndrome/) warns of a tight narrowing in the main artery at the front of the heart.
- **New left bundle branch block.** This can follow heart muscle damage.
- **ST elevation during symptoms.** This is an acute, complete blockage — a heart attack — not a stable narrowing. It is an emergency.

Most of these findings also have harmless or unrelated causes, which is why a doctor interprets them in context.

**Get urgent help:** If you have chest pain or pressure, pain spreading to the arm, jaw or back, breathlessness, sweating or fainting, call 911 (999 in the UK, 112 in Europe). A sudden, complete artery blockage is a heart attack, and the EKG is most useful in exactly that situation. Do not wait for a routine test.

## Tests that do show blockages

Doctors choose among these depending on your symptoms, age and risk factors.

| Test | What it shows | How it works | Pros | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Resting EKG** | Rhythm, past heart attack, some signs of strain | Stickers on the skin for 10 seconds | Fast, cheap, no risk | Often normal even with blockages |
| **Exercise stress EKG** | Whether reduced blood flow appears when the heart works hard | EKG recorded on a treadmill or bike | Widely available, shows exercise capacity | Less accurate than imaging; needs you to exercise; false results in some people |
| **Stress echocardiogram** | Areas of heart muscle that stop moving well under stress | Ultrasound before and after exercise or medicine | No radiation; also shows valves and pumping | Image quality varies with body shape |
| **Nuclear stress test** (SPECT or PET) | Areas of poor blood flow to the muscle | Small tracer injection plus imaging at rest and stress | Good accuracy; works with medicine if you cannot exercise | Uses radiation; takes several hours |
| **Coronary calcium score** | Amount of calcified plaque in the arteries | Quick CT scan without dye | Good for judging long-term risk | Does not show how narrow an artery is; misses soft plaque |
| **Coronary CT angiogram (CCTA)** | Picture of the arteries, including plaque and narrowings | CT scan with contrast dye in a vein | Shows the arteries directly without a catheter | Uses dye and radiation; heart rate must be slow and steady |
| **Cardiac catheterization (invasive angiogram)** | Detailed X-ray images of the arteries | Thin tube passed from the wrist or groin, dye injected | The reference test; blockages can be treated with a stent at the same time | Invasive; small risk of bleeding and other complications |

In practice, a person with typical chest pain and risk factors might go straight to a CT angiogram or stress imaging test. Someone with an abnormal stress test may then be offered a catheter angiogram. Someone who is having a heart attack goes straight to the cath lab.

## Can an EKG show blockage during a heart attack?

Yes — this is where the EKG is most powerful. When an artery closes completely and suddenly, the EKG often shows [ST elevation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/st-elevation/) within minutes. The leads that show it even suggest which artery is involved: the inferior leads (II, III, aVF) usually point to the right coronary artery, and the front chest leads to the left anterior descending artery.

Partial blockages during a heart attack (NSTEMI) can be harder to see. For details, see [can an EKG detect a heart attack?](https://www.ecgmeans.com/can-an-ekg-detect-a-heart-attack/)

## Symptoms that suggest a blockage

Because the resting EKG is often normal, symptoms are usually the better guide. Narrowed coronary arteries commonly cause **angina**: a pressure, tightness, heaviness or burning in the chest that comes on with exertion, cold weather, a heavy meal or emotional stress, and eases within minutes of rest. It can spread to the arm, neck, jaw or back.

Other possible signs include:

- Breathlessness on exertion that is new or getting worse
- Unusual tiredness with activity, especially in women and older adults
- Discomfort felt only in the jaw, arm, back or upper stomach
- Symptoms that come on at lower and lower levels of effort

People with diabetes may have few or no symptoms. If you notice any of these, tell your doctor even if a recent EKG was normal. Chest pain at rest, or pain that is new and lasts more than a few minutes, is an emergency.

## Will an EKG show blockage if I have no symptoms?

Usually not. Routine EKGs in people without symptoms rarely find coronary artery disease, and the US Preventive Services Task Force advises against routine EKG screening for low-risk adults who feel well. For people without symptoms, doctors more often estimate risk from age, blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes and smoking. A coronary calcium score may help in some people whose risk is borderline.

## What usually happens next

1. **If your EKG is normal and you have no symptoms:** usually no further heart testing, but your doctor may review your risk factors.
2. **If you have chest discomfort with exertion, unusual breathlessness, or strong risk factors:** your doctor may order a stress test or a coronary CT angiogram even if the EKG is normal.
3. **If the EKG shows a possible old heart attack or ischemic changes:** expect an echocardiogram and, often, a stress or CT test.
4. **If a significant blockage is found:** treatment ranges from medicines and lifestyle changes to stents or bypass surgery, depending on the location and severity.

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Did my EKG show anything suggesting reduced blood flow or a past heart attack?
- Given my symptoms and risk factors, do I need a test that looks at my arteries?
- Is a stress test, CT angiogram or calcium score the best next step for me?
- If my report mentions "block", is that an electrical finding or an artery problem?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Can an EKG detect a 70% or 90% blockage?
A: Not reliably at rest. Even severe narrowing can leave the resting EKG normal because blood flow is enough when the heart is not working hard. Stress testing or imaging is needed.
Q: Is a heart block the same as a blocked artery?
A: No. Heart block is an electrical conduction delay seen on the EKG. A blocked artery is plaque narrowing a blood vessel. They can occur together but are different problems.
Q: Will an EKG show blockage in the legs or neck?
A: No. An EKG only records the heart's electrical activity. Blockages in the leg or neck arteries are checked with tests such as an ankle-brachial index or carotid ultrasound.
Q: Can a stress test miss a blockage?
A: Yes, sometimes. No test is perfect. If symptoms continue despite a normal stress test, your doctor may suggest a CT angiogram or catheter angiogram.
Q: Does an echocardiogram show blockages?
A: Not directly. A resting echo shows how the muscle and valves work. A stress echo can reveal areas that weaken during exercise because of a narrowed artery.
Q: What is the best test to check for heart blockage?
A: An invasive coronary angiogram is the reference test, and a coronary CT angiogram is the main non-invasive way to see the arteries. Which is best depends on your situation.
