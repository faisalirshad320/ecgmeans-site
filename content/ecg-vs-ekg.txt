@@ANSWER@@
ECG vs EKG: there is no difference. Both are abbreviations for electrocardiogram, the same quick, painless test that records the heart's electrical activity through stickers on the skin. ECG follows the English spelling; EKG comes from the German "Elektrokardiogramm". EKG is common in the US, partly because it is harder to confuse with EEG, a brain-wave test.
@@LEDE@@
If your doctor said "ECG" and the appointment letter says "EKG", you have not been booked for two different tests. The two abbreviations describe exactly the same thing, done with the same machine, and read in the same way. The difference is history and habit, not medicine. This page explains where each name comes from, why American hospitals tend to prefer EKG, what the test actually measures, the different kinds of ECG you might be offered, and how it compares with two tests people often mix it up with: the EEG and the echocardiogram.

## ECG vs EKG: the short answer

When people compare ECG vs EKG, they are comparing two names for one test. **ECG** and **EKG** both stand for **electrocardiogram** (sometimes electrocardiograph, which is the machine). The equipment, the electrode positions, the results and the way doctors interpret them are identical. A report labeled "ECG" in London and one labeled "EKG" in Chicago can be read side by side.

| | ECG | EKG |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stands for | Electrocardiogram (English spelling) | Elektrokardiogramm (German spelling) |
| What it records | Electrical activity of the heart | Electrical activity of the heart |
| How it is done | Electrodes on chest, arms and legs | Electrodes on chest, arms and legs |
| Where the name is common | UK, Europe, Australia, most medical journals | US, especially in hospitals and clinics |
| Results | Identical | Identical |

On this site we use both terms, as most US doctors do.

## What does EKG stand for?

EKG stands for **Elektrokardiogramm**, the German word for electrocardiogram. Breaking the word down:

- **Electro / Elektro** — electrical
- **Cardio / Kardio** — heart (from the Greek *kardia*)
- **Gram / Gramm** — a recording or drawing

So both versions literally mean "a recording of the heart's electricity". German has a "K" where English has a "C", which is the whole reason for the different letters.

## Why is it called EKG and not ECG in the US?

There are two main reasons, both historical rather than scientific:

1. **Early research came largely from German-speaking Europe.** The practical electrocardiograph was developed in the early 1900s by Dutch physiologist Willem Einthoven, who received the Nobel Prize for it in 1924. Much of the early literature was in German, and "EKG" traveled with it into American medicine.
2. **"ECG" sounds and looks a lot like "EEG".** An EEG (electroencephalogram) records brain waves. On a busy ward, spoken aloud or in handwriting, "ECG" and "EEG" are easy to mix up. Using EKG reduces the chance that someone orders or performs the wrong test. This safety habit is a big part of why EKG has stayed popular in the US.

Today both terms are fully accepted. Many US hospitals say "EKG" at the bedside but print "ECG" on the report, and cardiology journals use "ECG". If your paperwork says one and your doctor says the other, it is the same thing.

## What is an EKG (ECG) and what does it measure?

An [EKG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/what-is-an-ekg/) records the tiny electrical signals that make your heart beat. Each heartbeat starts with a signal from the sinus node, spreads through the upper chambers (the P wave), through the lower chambers (the QRS complex) and then resets (the T wave). The machine draws these as waves on paper or a screen.

From those waves a doctor can see:

- **Heart rate** — too fast, too slow or normal
- **Rhythm** — regular [sinus rhythm](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-rhythm/), or an irregular rhythm such as [atrial fibrillation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/atrial-fibrillation/)
- **Conduction** — whether signals are delayed, as in heart block or bundle branch block
- **Signs of a heart attack**, current or old, or reduced blood supply
- **Signs of thickened heart muscle** or strain on a chamber
- **Effects of electrolytes and medicines**, such as high or low potassium or a long [QT interval](https://www.ecgmeans.com/qt-interval/)

What it cannot show directly is the heart's structure — valves, pumping strength or blockages inside the arteries. For more on that, see [what an EKG can detect](https://www.ecgmeans.com/what-can-an-ekg-detect/) and [whether an EKG shows blockages](https://www.ecgmeans.com/does-an-ekg-show-blockage/).

## Types of ECG / EKG

All of these are "ECGs". They differ in how many electrodes are used and how long they record.

| Type | How long it records | Electrodes / device | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Resting 12-lead ECG** | About 10 seconds | 10 electrodes on limbs and chest | Standard test in clinics, emergency rooms, pre-op checks |
| **Holter monitor** | Usually 24–48 hours | Small recorder with a few chest electrodes | Palpitations or dizziness that happen daily |
| **Event monitor / patch monitor** | Days to about a month | Wearable patch or recorder; you press a button when symptoms occur, or it records automatically | Symptoms that come and go less often |
| **Implantable loop recorder** | Up to a few years | Tiny device placed under the skin of the chest | Rare, unexplained fainting or palpitations |
| **Exercise stress ECG** | 10–20 minutes on a treadmill or bike | 12-lead electrodes while exercising | Chest pain on exertion, exercise capacity |
| **Smartwatch / single-lead ECG** | About 30 seconds | Watch or handheld device; one lead | Spotting possible atrial fibrillation at home |

A smartwatch ECG records a single lead — roughly similar to lead I on a full ECG. It can be very useful for catching an irregular rhythm when you feel it, but it cannot detect a heart attack and is not a substitute for a 12-lead test. For electrode positions on the full test, see [12 lead ECG placement](https://www.ecgmeans.com/12-lead-ecg-placement/).

## ECG vs EEG vs echocardiogram

The tests most often confused with an ECG are the EEG and the echocardiogram ("echo"). They measure very different things.

| | ECG / EKG | EEG | Echocardiogram |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full name | Electrocardiogram | Electroencephalogram | Echocardiogram (heart ultrasound) |
| Organ | Heart | Brain | Heart |
| What it measures | Electrical activity of the heart | Electrical activity (brain waves) of the brain | Structure and movement — chambers, valves, pumping |
| How | Sticky electrodes on chest and limbs | Small electrodes on the scalp | Ultrasound probe with gel on the chest |
| Typical time | About 5–10 minutes total | About 20–60 minutes for a routine test | About 30–60 minutes |
| Finds | Rhythm problems, heart attack signs, conduction delays | Seizures, epilepsy, some sleep and brain disorders | Valve disease, weak pumping (ejection fraction), enlarged chambers |

For full side-by-side guides, see [EEG vs EKG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/eeg-vs-ekg/) and [EKG vs echocardiogram](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ekg-vs-echocardiogram/). An abnormal ECG is often followed by an echo, because the two tests answer different questions about the same heart.

## What to expect during the test

Whether it is called an ECG or an EKG, the experience is the same:

1. You lie down and expose your chest, wrists and ankles (a gown is usually offered).
2. A technician may clip small areas of chest hair and wipe the skin.
3. Ten sticky electrodes are attached and connected by wires to the machine.
4. You lie still and breathe normally for about 10 seconds while it records.
5. The electrodes are peeled off. The whole visit usually takes 5–10 minutes.

It is painless, uses no needles and no radiation, and sends no electricity into your body — it only listens. Results are often available straight away, though a doctor should confirm the machine's printed interpretation. Our guide on [how to read an ECG report](https://www.ecgmeans.com/how-to-read-ecg-report/) explains the numbers and phrases you may see, and [normal ECG values](https://www.ecgmeans.com/normal-ecg-values/) lists the usual ranges.

## Does it matter which term your doctor uses?

No. You do not need to request one or the other, and insurers and hospitals treat them as the same test. What matters is the **type** of ECG (resting, Holter, stress, etc.) and whether your results are reviewed in the context of your symptoms.

**Get urgent help** if you have chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a racing heartbeat that does not settle — call 911 (US), 999 (UK) or 112 (Europe). Do not wait for an outpatient ECG appointment. A normal ECG also does not rule out every heart problem; see [if my ECG is normal, is my heart OK?](https://www.ecgmeans.com/if-ecg-is-normal-is-my-heart-ok/)
@@FAQ@@
Q: Is ECG and EKG the same?
A: Yes. They are two abbreviations for the same test, the electrocardiogram. The equipment, procedure and results are identical.
Q: What is the difference between ECG and EKG?
A: Only the spelling. ECG comes from the English word electrocardiogram and EKG from the German Elektrokardiogramm.
Q: What does EKG stand for?
A: Elektrokardiogramm, the German word for electrocardiogram — a recording of the heart's electrical activity.
Q: Why do American doctors say EKG?
A: Partly because early electrocardiography literature was in German, and partly because EKG is harder to confuse with EEG, a brain-wave test.
Q: How long does an EKG take?
A: The recording takes about 10 seconds, and the whole appointment usually 5–10 minutes. Holter and event monitors record for days or weeks.
Q: Can an EKG detect a heart attack?
A: A 12-lead EKG can show signs of a current or past heart attack, but a normal one does not rule it out. Blood tests and repeat ECGs are often needed.
Q: Is a smartwatch ECG the same as an EKG?
A: It is a single-lead ECG. It can detect an irregular rhythm such as atrial fibrillation but cannot replace a full 12-lead EKG.
