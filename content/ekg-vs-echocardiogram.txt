## Is an echocardiogram the same as an EKG?

No. The names sound alike and both are painless heart tests, so they are easy to mix up, but an echocardiogram and an EKG measure completely different things. An EKG (electrocardiogram) is an electrical recording. An echocardiogram — "echo" for short — is an ultrasound scan. The word "electrocardiogram" refers to the EKG; "echocardiogram" refers to the scan. So when people compare an echocardiogram vs an electrocardiogram, they are comparing an ultrasound with an electrical tracing.

One more source of confusion: ECG and EKG are the same test. EKG comes from the German spelling. See [ECG vs EKG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-vs-ekg/) for the full story.

## What "Echocardiogram vs EKG" means

The EKG is quick, cheap and excellent for rhythm problems, conduction blocks and an acute heart attack. It is poor at judging chamber size or pump strength, and a normal EKG does not rule out structural heart disease.

The echocardiogram takes 20–45 minutes, uses no radiation, and directly measures how well the heart pumps and whether valves leak or narrow. It cannot capture an intermittent rhythm problem. Many of the "possible" statements on an EKG report — possible left atrial enlargement, possible LVH, septal infarct — are confirmed or dismissed by an echo.

Timings vary between hospitals and with the type of echo; some departments book up to an hour for a standard scan, and a transesophageal echo takes longer.

## EKG vs echocardiogram: side-by-side comparison

| | EKG (ECG) | Echocardiogram (echo) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What it is** | A recording of the heart's electrical signals | An ultrasound scan of the heart |
| **How it is done** | 10–12 stickers on the chest, arms and legs; you lie still | A gel-covered probe moved over the chest; you lie on your left side |
| **How long it takes** | A few minutes; the recording itself is about 10 seconds | Usually under an hour (often 20–45 minutes) |
| **What you get** | A tracing of waves (P, QRS, T) with an automated report | Moving images of the heart with measurements and colour Doppler |
| **Best at showing** | Heart rate and rhythm, conduction blocks, signs of a heart attack, electrolyte effects, QT | Pumping strength (ejection fraction), chamber size, wall thickness, valves, fluid around the heart |
| **Cannot show well** | Pump strength, valve problems, most structural disease | Rhythm problems that are not happening during the scan |
| **Radiation** | None | None |
| **Who reads it** | Computer first, then a doctor | A sonographer records it; a cardiologist reports it |
| **Typical setting** | GP or doctor's office, ER, pre-op check, ambulance | Cardiology department, echo lab, hospital ward |

### What each looks like

EKG: a paper or screen tracing of waves. Echo: moving ultrasound images of the beating heart, with measurements and Doppler colour flow.

## What an EKG can show

An EKG is the first-line test for symptoms such as palpitations, chest pain or fainting because it is fast and gives an immediate answer about rhythm. It can show:

- Fast, slow or irregular rhythms such as [atrial fibrillation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/atrial-fibrillation/)
- Heart blocks and bundle branch blocks
- Signs of a current or previous heart attack — see [can an EKG detect a heart attack?](https://www.ecgmeans.com/can-an-ekg-detect-a-heart-attack/)
- Electrical clues to thickened muscle, enlarged chambers or electrolyte problems

For the full list, see [what can an EKG detect?](https://www.ecgmeans.com/what-can-an-ekg-detect/).

## What an echocardiogram can show

An echo answers the "structure and pump" questions an EKG cannot:

- Ejection fraction — how much blood the main pumping chamber pushes out with each beat
- Whether the heart muscle is thickened ([left ventricular hypertrophy](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-ventricular-hypertrophy/)) or the chambers are enlarged
- Valve narrowing or leaking, often heard first as a murmur
- Areas of weak muscle after a heart attack
- Fluid around the heart (pericardial effusion), congenital defects and clots

## What happens during each test

### Having an EKG

You lie on a couch and expose your chest. A technician sticks 10 electrodes on your chest, arms and legs (on very hairy skin a small area may be shaved). You lie still and breathe normally while the machine records for about 10 seconds. The stickers come off and you can go straight back to normal activities. The printout and computer report are available immediately, although a doctor confirms the final reading. Lead positions are explained on our [12-lead ECG placement](https://www.ecgmeans.com/12-lead-ecg-placement/) page.

### Having an echocardiogram

A standard echo is a transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE). You lie on your left side in a darkened room while a sonographer presses a probe, covered in cool gel, against several points on your chest. You may hear whooshing sounds — that is the Doppler measuring blood flow. You may be asked to hold your breath briefly. Some EKG stickers are often attached so the scan can be timed to your heartbeat. There is nothing to recover from afterwards; a cardiologist reviews the images and writes the report, which may take a few days to reach your doctor.

### Other types of echo

- **Stress echo:** images taken before and after exercise or a medicine that makes the heart work harder, to look for areas with poor blood supply.
- **Transesophageal echo (TEE):** a thin probe passed down the food pipe under sedation for clearer pictures, often used to look for clots before cardioversion or to examine valves closely.
- **Bubble (contrast) echo:** a small amount of agitated saline or contrast is injected to look for a hole between the chambers or to sharpen the images.

## Cost and availability

An EKG is one of the cheapest and most widely available tests in medicine — done in most clinics, urgent care centers, ERs and ambulances. An echocardiogram needs specialist equipment and a trained sonographer, so it costs considerably more and is usually booked in advance. Prices vary widely by country, insurer and facility, so check with your provider; our [where to get an EKG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/where-to-get-an-ekg/) page lists common options for the electrical test.

## When is each test used?

### Common reasons for each test

- EKG is used for: palpitations, chest pain, fainting, pre-operative checks, monitoring medicines
- Echo is used for: murmurs, breathlessness, heart failure, valve disease, abnormal EKG findings, family history of cardiomyopathy

### Why an abnormal EKG is often followed by an echo

Many EKG findings are indirect. Tall voltages may mean thickened muscle — or just a thin chest. Q waves may mean an old heart attack — or lead placement. The echo looks directly at the heart and settles the question. That is why "possible LVH," "possible septal infarct" or an [abnormal ECG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/abnormal-ecg/) result commonly leads to an echo referral.

## Echo vs EKG: which is better?

Neither; they are complementary. If the question is "what is my heart's rhythm doing right now?", the EKG wins. If the question is "is my heart's structure and pumping normal?", the echo wins. If symptoms come and go, a Holter or event monitor may be needed instead of either.

| Your question | Most useful test |
| --- | --- |
| Why is my heart racing or skipping? | EKG, then a Holter or event monitor if the EKG is normal |
| Am I having a heart attack? | EKG plus blood tests (troponin) |
| Why am I breathless or swollen? | Echo (often with an EKG) |
| Is my murmur a valve problem? | Echo |
| Is my abnormal EKG serious? | Echo, often first |
| Are my arteries narrowed? | Neither alone — a stress test or CT/angiogram |

## Should you worry?

Neither test carries risk. If you have had an abnormal EKG and your doctor has ordered an echo, that is the normal way of finding out whether the electrical finding reflects anything structural — it does not mean something serious has been found.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

## What usually happens next

1. EKG first for rhythm or acute symptoms
2. Echo when structure or function is in question
3. Holter or event monitor if the problem is intermittent
4. Stress test if blood supply is the question

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Which test answers my particular question?
- If my EKG was abnormal, will an echo settle it?
- Do I need both?
- Will I get the echo results on the day, or at a follow-up appointment?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Can an EKG detect a blocked artery?
A: It can show signs of an acute heart attack and sometimes of previous damage, but a normal EKG does not exclude blocked arteries. A stress test or angiogram is needed for that.
Q: Can an echocardiogram detect arrhythmia?
A: Only if the arrhythmia is happening during the scan. Rhythm assessment is the EKG's job.
Q: Which is better, EKG or echo?
A: Neither — they are complementary. An EKG looks at electricity; an echo looks at structure and pumping.
Q: Is an EKG the same as an echocardiogram?
A: No. An EKG records electrical signals with stickers on the skin; an echocardiogram uses ultrasound to make moving pictures of the heart.
Q: Is an echocardiogram more accurate than an EKG?
A: Each is more accurate for its own job. An echo is far better for structure, valves and pumping; an EKG is better for rhythm and for spotting a heart attack in progress.
Q: Do I need to prepare for an EKG or an echo?
A: Usually not for either. Wear a top that is easy to remove, and avoid body lotion on the chest so the stickers or probe make good contact. Special types, such as a stress echo or transesophageal echo, have their own instructions.
