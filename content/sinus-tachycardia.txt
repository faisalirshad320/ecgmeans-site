@@BODY@@
## What is sinus tachycardia?

Sinus tachycardia means two things at once. "Sinus" says the beat is starting in the right place — the sinus (sinoatrial) node, the heart's natural pacemaker in the right upper chamber. "Tachycardia" says the rate is fast, which in adults means more than 100 beats per minute. Put together, the rhythm is normal; only the speed is up.

That is very different from most other "tachycardias" on an ECG report, where the beat starts somewhere it should not. If you want the baseline for comparison, see [sinus rhythm](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-rhythm/), which is the same rhythm at 60–100 beats per minute.

## What "Sinus tachycardia" means

The sinus node speeds up under the influence of adrenaline and the sympathetic nervous system. That is appropriate during exercise or fear. On a resting ECG it often reflects the stress of the test or the illness that brought you to hospital.

When the resting rate stays above 100 with no identifiable cause and produces symptoms, doctors use the term inappropriate sinus tachycardia (IST). It is uncomfortable but not dangerous, and is separate from POTS, where the rate jumps on standing.

Doctors often split sinus tachycardia into two broad groups:

| Type | What is going on | Typical example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Physiological (appropriate)** | The sinus node is speeding up for a good reason | Running for a bus, a fever of 39 °C, a panic about the test |
| **Secondary to illness** | The body is compensating for a medical problem | Dehydration, blood loss, anaemia, infection, low oxygen, an overactive thyroid |
| **Inappropriate sinus tachycardia (IST)** | The rate stays high with no cause found | A young adult with a resting rate persistently over 100 and normal tests |

## What it looks like on the ECG

Normal P waves, QRS complexes and T waves, close together. At very fast rates the P wave may merge into the preceding T wave.

For clinicians and students, the checklist is:

- Rate above 100 per minute (in adults)
- An upright P wave in leads I and II before every QRS, with the same shape each beat
- A constant PR interval and, usually, a narrow QRS
- Gradual speeding up and slowing down if a longer strip is recorded — not an abrupt switch

If you want to check the rate on your own tracing, our [ECG heart rate calculator](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-heart-rate-calculator/) shows how the machine works it out from the spacing between beats.

## Normal heart rate ranges by age

"Above 100" is the adult cut-off. Children's hearts beat faster, so a child's ECG is read against age-specific ranges. Approximate normal resting rates (MedlinePlus):

| Age | Normal resting heart rate (beats per minute) |
| --- | --- |
| Newborn (0–1 month) | 70–190 |
| Infant (1–11 months) | 80–160 |
| 1–2 years | 80–130 |
| 3–4 years | 80–120 |
| 5–6 years | 75–115 |
| 7–9 years | 70–110 |
| 10 years and older, including adults | 60–100 |
| Well-trained athletes | 40–60 |

So a rate of 120 is sinus tachycardia in an adult but can be normal in a toddler. During exercise any healthy heart can go far higher; the maximum falls with age (a rough rule of thumb is about 220 minus your age).

## What causes sinus tachycardia?

### Common causes

- Anxiety, pain, exertion
- Fever and infection
- Dehydration or blood loss
- Anaemia
- Overactive thyroid
- Caffeine, nicotine, decongestants, asthma inhalers, stimulants
- Alcohol withdrawal
- Low oxygen, pulmonary embolism, heart failure (in unwell patients)

### Causes at a glance

| Group | Examples | Clue on the history or tests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Everyday and emotional** | Exercise, anxiety, pain, the stress of having an ECG | Rate settles when calm; often fine on a repeat |
| **Fluids and blood** | Dehydration, vomiting or diarrhoea, bleeding, anaemia | Thirst, dizziness on standing, low haemoglobin |
| **Infection and inflammation** | Fever, flu, pneumonia, sepsis | Raised temperature; roughly 10 beats faster for every degree Celsius of fever |
| **Hormones** | Overactive thyroid, pregnancy | Weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor; abnormal thyroid tests |
| **Substances and medicines** | Caffeine, energy drinks, nicotine, decongestants, asthma inhalers, stimulants, some antidepressants, alcohol or drug withdrawal | Started or increased recently |
| **Heart and lungs** | Heart failure, pulmonary embolism, low oxygen, cardiac tamponade | Breathlessness, chest pain, a swollen leg — needs urgent assessment |
| **No cause found** | Inappropriate sinus tachycardia | Persistent fast rate, normal tests |

The last two rows are why doctors take a fast rate seriously in someone who is unwell: the heart may be compensating for a problem elsewhere. A [pulmonary embolism](https://www.ecgmeans.com/pulmonary-embolism-ecg/), for example, very often shows nothing more on the ECG than sinus tachycardia.

## Inappropriate sinus tachycardia (IST)

Inappropriate sinus tachycardia is a diagnosis of exclusion: everything else has been checked and the sinus node is still running fast. Using the Heart Rhythm Society's 2015 consensus definition, it means a resting sinus rate above 100 with an average rate over 24 hours above 90, with distressing symptoms such as palpitations, tiredness or light-headedness, and no secondary cause. It is seen most often in women in their 20s and 30s.

IST is not life-threatening, but it can affect quality of life. Treatment is aimed at symptoms (see below).

### IST vs POTS

Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) is often confused with IST because both cause a fast sinus rhythm and similar symptoms. The difference is posture:

| | Inappropriate sinus tachycardia | POTS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **When the rate is high** | At rest, lying or sitting, and through the day | Mainly on standing up |
| **Key test finding** | Resting rate over 100; 24-hour average over 90 | Rate rises by 30 or more within 10 minutes of standing (40 or more in teenagers), without a big fall in blood pressure |
| **Main symptoms** | Palpitations, fatigue, breathlessness on exertion | Dizziness, brain fog, palpitations and fatigue when upright |
| **Usual approach** | Trigger review, rate-slowing medicine if needed | Fluids, salt (if advised), compression, graded exercise, sometimes medicine |

Some people have features of both. A tilt-table test or a simple stand-up test helps tell them apart.

## Is sinus tachycardia dangerous?

### Should you worry?

Most cases settle once the trigger goes. Seek prompt care if a fast heart rate comes with chest pain, breathlessness, fainting, or a one-sided swollen leg, or if it starts and stops abruptly (which suggests a different rhythm, SVT).

A persistently fast rate over weeks or months can, rarely, tire the heart muscle, which is another reason doctors look for the cause rather than ignore it.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

## Sinus tachycardia vs SVT and other fast rhythms

The ECG machine can sometimes mislabel a fast rhythm, so it is worth knowing the differences.

| Rhythm | Onset and offset | Usual resting rate | Regular? | P waves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sinus tachycardia** | Gradual | 100–150 | Yes | Normal, before each QRS |
| **[Supraventricular tachycardia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/supraventricular-tachycardia/)** | Sudden, like a switch | 150–250 | Yes | Often hidden |
| **[Atrial flutter](https://www.ecgmeans.com/atrial-flutter/)** | Sudden | Often about 150 | Usually | Sawtooth flutter waves |
| **[AFib with RVR](https://www.ecgmeans.com/afib-with-rvr/)** | Sudden or gradual | Over 100–110 | No — irregularly irregular | None |

## Sinus tachycardia treatment

There is no treatment for sinus tachycardia itself in most cases — the treatment is for whatever is driving it. In practice that means:

1. **Treat the trigger.** Fluids for dehydration, treating an infection or fever, correcting anaemia or thyroid problems, managing pain.
2. **Review substances and medicines.** Cutting back on caffeine, energy drinks, nicotine and decongestants often helps. Your doctor may review inhalers or other medicines, but do not stop a prescribed medicine on your own.
3. **Address anxiety.** Slow breathing, reassurance and, where needed, psychological therapy such as CBT.
4. **For inappropriate sinus tachycardia,** your doctor may suggest lifestyle measures first and then, if symptoms persist, a rate-slowing medicine — commonly a beta-blocker, a rate-limiting calcium-channel blocker, or ivabradine. Catheter ablation of the sinus node is rarely used because results are mixed.
5. **Short-term relief.** If you feel light-headed, lying down and taking slow, deep breaths can help while the rate settles.

Medicines that simply slow the heart are generally avoided when the fast rate is the body compensating for something else, such as blood loss or sepsis — slowing it could do harm.

## What usually happens next

1. Identify and treat the trigger
2. Blood count, thyroid function and electrolytes
3. Repeat ECG when calm
4. Holter monitor if the diagnosis is uncertain or IST is suspected

If you get palpitations but your ECG comes back normal or only shows sinus tachycardia, see [heart palpitations with a normal ECG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/heart-palpitations-normal-ecg/) for what doctors usually check next.

### Questions to ask your doctor

- What do you think is driving my heart rate?
- Could this be SVT rather than sinus tachycardia?
- Should I cut down caffeine or change any medicine?
- Do I need a Holter monitor or a stand-up test to look for IST or POTS?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Is sinus tachycardia dangerous?
A: The rhythm itself is not; the cause may or may not be important.
Q: How is it different from SVT?
A: Sinus tachycardia speeds up and slows down gradually and rarely exceeds about 150 at rest. SVT switches on and off suddenly and is typically 150–250.
Q: Can anxiety alone cause it?
A: Yes, and being connected to an ECG machine is a common trigger.
Q: What heart rate is sinus tachycardia?
A: In adults, a sinus rhythm above 100 beats per minute. Children have higher normal rates, so their thresholds depend on age.
Q: How do you get rid of sinus tachycardia?
A: By treating the cause — rest, fluids, treating fever or infection, cutting caffeine or stimulants, or managing anxiety. Rate-slowing medicine is mainly for inappropriate sinus tachycardia and is decided by your doctor.
Q: Is sinus tachycardia the same as POTS?
A: No. POTS is a condition where the heart rate jumps by 30 or more beats per minute on standing; the rhythm during it is sinus tachycardia, but sinus tachycardia has many other causes.
Q: Can dehydration cause sinus tachycardia?
A: Yes. With less fluid in the circulation, the heart beats faster to keep blood pressure up. The rate usually falls once you are rehydrated.
