@@ANSWER@@
Poor R wave progression (PRWP) means the upward R wave does not grow taller as expected across chest leads V1 to V4 — typically the R wave in V3 is 3 mm or less. It is a description, not a diagnosis. Most often it reflects electrode position, body build or a normal variant, but it can be a sign of an old heart attack on the front of the heart, so it is judged alongside symptoms and other findings.
@@LEDE@@
"Poor R wave progression" is a common computer-generated line on ECG (EKG) reports, sometimes followed by "consider anterior infarct." That second phrase is what worries people, but most people with this finding have not had a heart attack. This page explains how R waves normally behave across the chest leads, what makes them grow too slowly, how doctors tell a harmless pattern from a meaningful one, and which tests usually settle the question.
@@BODY@@
## What "poor R wave progression" means

The six chest leads (V1 to V6) sit in a line across the chest, from just right of the breastbone round to the left side under the armpit. They view the heart from front-right to left.

In a normal tracing:

- In **V1** the QRS is mostly downward — a tiny r wave and a deep S wave
- The **R wave gets taller** and the S wave shallower as you move from V1 to V6
- The point where the R wave becomes bigger than the S wave — the **transition zone** — is usually at **V3 or V4**

When the R waves stay small for too long, the computer prints "poor R wave progression." If the transition is pushed to V5 or V6, reports may also say "late transition" or "clockwise rotation."

It is a description of shape, not a disease. Follow-up studies of people with this label show that only a minority turn out to have heart muscle scarring on imaging.

## What it looks like on the ECG

There is no single agreed definition, but commonly used criteria are:

| Feature | Normal pattern | Poor R wave progression |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R wave height in V3 | Usually more than 3 mm | 3 mm or less |
| R wave growth V1 → V4 | Steady increase | Little or no increase |
| Transition zone | V3–V4 | Delayed to V5–V6 |
| R wave V2 vs V3 | V3 taller | V3 the same or smaller |
| Reversed progression | Not present | R wave gets smaller from V1 to V3 or V4 ("reversed R wave progression") |

**Reversed R wave progression** — where R waves actually shrink across the early chest leads — is more likely to mean something important (an old anterior infarct, or misplaced electrodes) than simple slow growth.

## Causes of poor R wave progression

**Common causes**

- **Chest electrodes placed too high**, too far apart or swapped — one of the commonest explanations. See [12-lead ECG placement](https://www.ecgmeans.com/12-lead-ecg-placement/)
- **Normal variant**, more common in women
- **Body build** — obesity, breast tissue, a broad or deep chest, or a chest wall shape such as pectus excavatum
- **Lung disease** — emphysema/COPD, which moves the heart and dampens the signal
- **[Left ventricular hypertrophy](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-ventricular-hypertrophy/)**, which often produces deep S waves in V1–V3

**Less common causes**

- **Old anterior or anteroseptal heart attack** — see [anterior infarct, age undetermined](https://www.ecgmeans.com/anterior-infarct-age-undetermined/) and [anteroseptal infarct](https://www.ecgmeans.com/anteroseptal-infarct/)
- **[Left bundle branch block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-bundle-branch-block/)** or [left anterior fascicular block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-anterior-fascicular-block/)
- **[Wolff-Parkinson-White](https://www.ecgmeans.com/wolff-parkinson-white/) pattern**, depending on pathway location
- **[Right ventricular hypertrophy](https://www.ecgmeans.com/right-ventricular-hypertrophy/)** and pulmonary hypertension
- **Cardiomyopathy** or infiltrative heart muscle disease, sometimes with [low voltage QRS](https://www.ecgmeans.com/low-voltage-qrs/)
- **Dextrocardia** (heart on the right side), which gives a striking reverse pattern

## Is poor R wave progression serious?

**As an isolated finding in a person who feels well, it carries little weight.** It is one of the weaker ECG pointers to an old heart attack, and on its own it is a poor predictor of scar.

Doctors take it more seriously when it comes with **risk features** such as:

- **Q waves in V1–V3** — see [pathological Q waves](https://www.ecgmeans.com/pathological-q-waves/) and [septal infarct](https://www.ecgmeans.com/septal-infarct/)
- **[T wave inversion](https://www.ecgmeans.com/t-wave-inversion/) or ST changes** in the same leads
- **Reversed R wave progression**, rather than just slow growth
- **[Fragmented QRS](https://www.ecgmeans.com/fragmented-qrs/)** — extra notches in the QRS that can point to scar
- **Symptoms** — chest pain on exertion, breathlessness or reduced exercise tolerance
- **A history or high risk of coronary disease** — diabetes, smoking, high blood pressure, high cholesterol or a family history
- **A change from a previous ECG** that was normal

## Symptoms to watch for

Poor R wave progression causes no symptoms. Report these, which could point to a heart problem behind the ECG:

- Chest pain, pressure or tightness, especially on exertion
- Breathlessness on mild effort or when lying flat
- Ankle swelling or unusual tiredness
- Palpitations, dizziness or fainting

## What tests usually come next

1. **Repeat ECG with careful lead placement.** If the pattern disappears, no further testing is usually needed.
2. **Comparison with earlier ECGs.** A long-standing, unchanged pattern is reassuring.
3. **Risk factor review** — blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes and smoking.
4. **Echocardiogram** if an old heart attack, heart muscle disease or LVH is a realistic possibility. It shows whether the front wall of the heart moves normally. See [EKG vs echocardiogram](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ekg-vs-echocardiogram/).
5. **Stress testing or coronary imaging** (such as a CT coronary angiogram) if you have symptoms suggesting angina.
6. **Cardiac MRI** occasionally, when the echo is unclear and scar needs to be confirmed or excluded.

## Treatment and management

*An illustrative example, not a real patient:* a 52-year-old woman with no symptoms has a routine ECG reported as "poor R wave progression, consider anterior infarct." A repeat ECG, with the chest electrodes placed carefully below the breast, shows normal R wave growth and a transition at V3. No further tests are needed. Had the repeat still shown the pattern alongside Q waves, an echocardiogram would have been the next step.

Poor R wave progression itself does not need treatment. If a correctly recorded ECG is normal or the echocardiogram is normal, nothing more is required. If an underlying cause is found — an old heart attack, LVH from high blood pressure, or a cardiomyopathy — treatment is aimed at that condition.

## Special groups

- **Women:** poor R wave progression is more common in women, partly because breast tissue affects electrode placement and signal size. Electrodes should be placed under the breast, not on top of it.
- **People with obesity or COPD:** the pattern is often due to body build or lung hyperinflation rather than heart disease.
- **Athletes and young people:** an isolated finding is usually a variant; young people with symptoms or a family history of heart muscle disease may be offered an echocardiogram.
- **Older adults:** the pattern is more likely to be investigated because coronary disease is more common with age.

## How it differs from look-alike findings

| Finding | How it differs |
| --- | --- |
| [Anterior infarct, age undetermined](https://www.ecgmeans.com/anterior-infarct-age-undetermined/) | A computer label usually triggered by Q waves or lost R waves; same question, often the same answer |
| [Septal infarct](https://www.ecgmeans.com/septal-infarct/) | Q waves or QS complexes specifically in V1–V2 |
| [Low voltage QRS](https://www.ecgmeans.com/low-voltage-qrs/) | Small complexes in all leads, not just poor growth in the chest leads |
| [Wellens syndrome](https://www.ecgmeans.com/wellens-syndrome/) | Deep or biphasic T waves in V2–V3 with **preserved** R wave progression after chest pain — urgent |
| [ECG artifact](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-artifact/) | Recording problems that distort waves; can coexist with lead misplacement |

## Get urgent help

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase. Call 911 in the US, 999 in the UK or 112 in Europe.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Could the chest electrodes have been misplaced? Should the ECG be repeated?
- Is the R wave progression slow, or actually reversed?
- Are there any other signs of an old heart attack on this ECG, such as Q waves?
- How does it compare with my earlier ECGs?
- Would an echocardiogram be useful in my case?
- Do my symptoms or risk factors mean I need a stress test?
@@FAQ@@
Q: What does poor R wave progression mean on an ECG?
A: It means the R waves do not grow taller as expected across chest leads V1 to V4, with the R wave in V3 typically 3 mm or less. It describes the tracing's shape and is not a diagnosis by itself.
Q: Is poor R wave progression normal?
A: It can be. A sizeable minority of healthy adults show it, particularly women and people with a broad or deep chest, obesity or lung disease.
Q: Does poor R wave progression mean I had a heart attack?
A: Only sometimes. It is one of the weaker ECG pointers to an old anterior infarct; an echocardiogram is the usual next test because it shows whether that wall moves normally, and a cardiac MRI can confirm scar if doubt remains.
Q: What causes poor R wave progression?
A: The commonest causes are electrode placement, body build and normal variation. Other causes include left ventricular hypertrophy, left bundle branch block, lung disease and an old anterior heart attack.
Q: What is reversed R wave progression?
A: It is when R waves get smaller, rather than larger, across the early chest leads. It is more likely than simple slow growth to reflect an old infarct or misplaced electrodes.
Q: Can poor R wave progression be fixed?
A: There is nothing to fix unless an underlying cause is found. If it is due to lead position, a correctly recorded ECG will not show it.
Q: Do I need an echocardiogram for poor R wave progression?
A: Not always. It is usually advised if you have symptoms, coronary risk factors, Q waves or other abnormalities, or if a repeat ECG still shows the pattern and doctors want to check the front wall.
