## Heart palpitations but normal ECG: why it happens

Palpitations are the feeling of your own heartbeat: pounding, fluttering, racing, skipping, or a "thump" in the chest or neck. An ECG (also called an EKG; they are the same test) records the heart's electrical activity, but a standard 12-lead ECG lasts only about ten seconds.

If your palpitations happen a few times a day, or a few times a week, the chance that one falls inside those ten seconds is small. So a normal ECG usually means one of two things:

1. **The palpitations were not happening during the recording.** The rhythm causing them may still be real, just not captured.
2. **The palpitations were happening, and the rhythm was normal.** Sometimes people feel a normal or slightly fast heartbeat more strongly, for example during anxiety, after caffeine, or when lying on the left side.

A normal ECG is still useful. It shows no sign of many structural or electrical problems that would make palpitations more worrying, such as a past heart attack, Wolff-Parkinson-White pattern, long QT, or significant thickening of the heart muscle. That lowers the chance of a dangerous cause.

## What do heart palpitations look like on an ECG?

When palpitations are captured, the tracing shows what caused them. The common patterns are:

| What you feel | What the ECG may show | How serious it usually is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Skipped beat, "thud", pause | [Premature ventricular contractions (PVCs)](https://www.ecgmeans.com/premature-ventricular-contractions/) — early wide beats followed by a pause | Usually harmless |
| Flutter, extra beat | [Premature atrial contractions (PACs)](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-rhythm-with-pac/) — early narrow beats | Usually harmless |
| Fast, regular pounding that builds up and settles gradually | [Sinus tachycardia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-tachycardia/) — a normal rhythm, just faster | Usually reflects anxiety, exercise, fever, caffeine, anemia or thyroid |
| Sudden very fast, regular racing that starts and stops like a switch | [Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT)](https://www.ecgmeans.com/supraventricular-tachycardia/) | Not usually dangerous, but needs assessment and is treatable |
| Fast and irregular, "all over the place" | [Atrial fibrillation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/atrial-fibrillation/) | Needs medical review; affects stroke risk |
| Racing with dizziness or fainting | Ventricular tachycardia (rare) | Emergency |
| Pounding, normal rate | Normal sinus rhythm | Awareness of a normal heartbeat |

Extra beats (PVCs and PACs) are by far the most common rhythm cause of palpitations in otherwise healthy people. Many people feel the pause and the stronger beat after it, rather than the early beat itself.

## Common causes of palpitations with a normal ECG

Most causes are not heart disease. They include:

- **Caffeine, energy drinks, nicotine and alcohol**
- **Anxiety, stress, panic attacks**
- **Poor sleep and tiredness**
- **Exercise, dehydration, fever**
- **Overactive thyroid** (hyperthyroidism)
- **Anemia** (low red blood cell count)
- **Low blood sugar, low potassium or low magnesium**
- **Medicines and supplements**: decongestants, asthma inhalers, thyroid medicine, some diet pills
- **Recreational drugs** such as cocaine or amphetamines
- **Pregnancy and hormonal changes**, including around periods and menopause

That is why a doctor often orders simple blood tests — a blood count, thyroid function, and electrolytes — alongside the ECG.

## Can anxiety cause an abnormal EKG?

Anxiety does not damage the heart, but it can change the ECG a little:

- **A faster heart rate** (sinus tachycardia), which the machine may label "abnormal" simply because the rate is over 100
- **Minor, nonspecific ST or T wave changes**, especially with fast breathing (hyperventilation). See [nonspecific T wave abnormality](https://www.ecgmeans.com/nonspecific-t-wave-abnormality/)
- **More extra beats**, as stress hormones make heart cells more excitable
- **Movement noise** if you are tense or shivering, which can look like an abnormality

Anxiety does not cause the patterns of a heart attack, such as true ST elevation or new Q waves. If the ECG changes settle on a calm repeat recording, anxiety or the moment of the test is a likely explanation.

### Panic attack with a normal ECG

Panic attacks can cause a pounding heart, chest tightness, shortness of breath, sweating and tingling — which is frightening and feels like a heart attack. If your ECG and troponin blood tests in the emergency room were normal, that is real reassurance about that episode. Treating panic disorder (with therapy, and sometimes medicine) often reduces palpitations a lot.

Do not assume every future episode is panic, though. New, different or exertion-related symptoms still need checking. See [can an EKG detect a heart attack?](https://www.ecgmeans.com/can-an-ekg-detect-a-heart-attack/)

## How to catch palpitations that come and go

If your palpitations are frequent or bothersome, the goal is to record your rhythm **while you feel them**. Options include:

| Monitor | How long it records | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Holter monitor** | Usually 24–48 hours, continuously | Daily symptoms |
| **Patch monitor** | Commonly up to about 1–2 weeks | Symptoms every few days; easier to wear than wires |
| **Event monitor** | Up to about 30 days; you press a button during symptoms (some trigger automatically) | Symptoms every week or two |
| **Implantable loop recorder** | Up to a few years; small device under the skin | Rare but significant symptoms, such as unexplained fainting |
| **Smartwatch or handheld single-lead ECG** | A 30-second recording whenever you choose | Capturing a rhythm during symptoms to show your doctor |

Keep a symptom diary during any monitoring: the time, what you were doing, and what it felt like. The doctor matches your diary to the tracing. If palpitations happen while the rhythm is normal, that is reassuring. If they match extra beats or an arrhythmia, the cause has been found.

Smartwatch recordings can be very helpful, especially for detecting atrial fibrillation, but they show only one lead. Bring the recordings to your appointment rather than relying on the watch's automatic label.

## Simple things that often help

If your doctor has found no worrying cause, these steps reduce palpitations for many people:

1. **Cut back on caffeine and energy drinks** for a few weeks and see whether the symptoms change.
2. **Limit alcohol**, especially binge drinking, which can trigger extra beats and atrial fibrillation.
3. **Stop smoking and vaping**, since nicotine is a stimulant.
4. **Improve sleep**, and ask about sleep apnea if you snore heavily or wake up gasping.
5. **Stay hydrated and eat regular meals.**
6. **Manage stress** with regular exercise, breathing techniques, or talking therapy. Slow breathing can calm a racing sinus rhythm.
7. **Review medicines and supplements** with your doctor or pharmacist; do not stop prescribed medicines on your own.

Knowing that extra beats are harmless often makes them less noticeable over time, because worry itself makes people more aware of their heartbeat.

## When palpitations need urgent attention

Most palpitations are harmless, but some combinations need quick action.

**Get urgent help** — call 911 (999 in the UK, 112 in Europe) — if palpitations come with:

- Chest pain, pressure or tightness
- Fainting or nearly fainting
- Severe shortness of breath
- A racing heartbeat that will not settle

**See a doctor soon** (within days) if:

- Palpitations happen during exercise
- You have known heart disease, a weak heart, or a past heart attack
- There is a family history of sudden death before age 50, or of inherited heart conditions
- Palpitations are becoming more frequent or longer
- You are pregnant and have new palpitations

## What usually happens next

1. **History and examination.** Your doctor asks when symptoms happen, how they start and stop, and about caffeine, alcohol, sleep, stress and medicines.
2. **Blood tests**, often a blood count, thyroid tests and electrolytes.
3. **A heart monitor** if the symptoms are frequent enough to catch.
4. **An echocardiogram** in some people, to check the heart's structure, especially if there are many extra beats or any abnormal findings.
5. **Reassurance and simple changes** for most people: less caffeine and alcohol, better sleep, managing stress. Your doctor may discuss medicine if symptoms are frequent and distressing.

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Could my palpitations be extra beats, and would a monitor confirm that?
- Which monitor is best given how often my symptoms happen?
- Should my thyroid, blood count or potassium be checked?
- Are any of my medicines or supplements likely to cause this?
- Which symptoms should make me go to the emergency room?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Can a normal ECG miss an arrhythmia?
A: Yes. A standard ECG records about ten seconds, so rhythms that come and go are often missed. A Holter, patch or event monitor is used to catch them.
Q: Can stress cause an abnormal EKG?
A: Stress can speed up the heart and cause minor nonspecific changes or extra beats. These usually settle on a calm repeat ECG and do not mean heart damage.
Q: Should I worry about palpitations if my ECG is normal?
A: Usually not, especially if you are otherwise well and have no red-flag symptoms. Tell your doctor if they are frequent, happen with exercise, or come with dizziness or chest pain.
Q: Why do I feel palpitations at night?
A: Lying down, especially on your left side, brings the heart closer to the chest wall and the room is quiet, so you notice your heartbeat more. Extra beats can also be more noticeable when the heart rate is slow.
Q: Can low iron cause palpitations?
A: Yes. Anemia makes the heart beat faster to deliver oxygen, which can cause palpitations. A blood count checks for it.
Q: Can an Apple Watch or smartwatch detect palpitations?
A: It can record a single-lead ECG during symptoms and is good at spotting atrial fibrillation. It cannot replace a full 12-lead ECG or a medical monitor.
