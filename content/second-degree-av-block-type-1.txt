## What "Second degree AV block type 1" means

**Second degree AV block type 1** goes by several names, and they all mean the same thing: Mobitz type 1, Mobitz I, Wenckebach, Wenckebach heart block, and 2nd degree heart block type 1. "AV" stands for atrioventricular — the AV node is the electrical gateway between the upper chambers (atria) and the lower chambers (ventricles). ECG and EKG are the same test.

The AV node becomes progressively more tired with each impulse: the PR interval stretches beat by beat until a P wave arrives that it cannot pass on. The pause lets it recover and the next PR is short again. The grouped beating this creates is the hallmark.

Because the AV node is the site, the QRS is usually narrow and the escape rhythm, if ever needed, is reliable. That is why it behaves very differently from Type 2, where the block is lower down.

### Where it fits among the heart blocks

| Degree of AV block | What happens | Usual seriousness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [First degree](https://www.ecgmeans.com/first-degree-av-block/) | Every beat is delayed (PR over 200 ms), none dropped | Usually harmless |
| **Second degree type 1 (Mobitz I / Wenckebach)** | Delay grows until one beat is dropped, then resets | Usually benign |
| [Second degree type 2 (Mobitz II)](https://www.ecgmeans.com/second-degree-av-block-type-2/) | PR constant; beats dropped suddenly | Serious; often needs a pacemaker |
| [2:1 AV block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/2-1-av-block/) | Every second beat dropped; type cannot be told from one strip | Needs review |
| [Third degree (complete)](https://www.ecgmeans.com/third-degree-av-block/) | No impulses get through; atria and ventricles beat independently | Serious; usually needs a pacemaker |

## What it looks like on the ECG

Groups of beats in which the PR gap gets longer and longer, then a P wave with no QRS after it, then the pattern repeats. The gaps between R waves shorten just before the dropped beat.

## Wenckebach ECG: how to recognise it

On a Wenckebach ECG strip, look for these features in order:

1. **P waves march regularly.** The atrial rhythm is steady; the P-to-P spacing stays roughly constant.
2. **The PR interval lengthens beat by beat.** It is shortest on the first beat after a pause and longest just before the dropped beat.
3. **One P wave stands alone.** A P wave appears with no QRS after it — the dropped beat.
4. **The cycle repeats.** After the pause the PR is short again, and the stretching starts over.
5. **Grouped beating.** QRS complexes appear in clusters separated by pauses. This "group beating" is often the first thing noticed, before the PR intervals are measured.

### The finer points

- **Ratios.** The pattern is described by how many P waves occur for each QRS in a cycle — 3:2, 4:3 and 5:4 are common. A 4:3 Wenckebach has four P waves and three QRS complexes per group.
- **Biggest jump first.** The PR usually increases most between the first and second beats of a cycle, then by smaller amounts. This is why the R-R gaps actually shorten slightly before the dropped beat — a classic but counter-intuitive clue.
- **The pause.** The pause containing the dropped beat is shorter than two normal beat-to-beat gaps.
- **Atypical Wenckebach.** Not every cycle follows the textbook. The PR may barely change, or change irregularly. The key test is to compare the PR just before the dropped beat with the PR just after it: in Mobitz I the PR after the pause is shorter.
- **Narrow QRS.** Usually under 120 ms, because the block is above the ventricles' wiring.

## Mobitz type 1 vs Mobitz type 2

This is the most important distinction, because the two behave very differently.

| Feature | Mobitz type 1 (Wenckebach) | Mobitz type 2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **PR interval before the dropped beat** | Progressively lengthens | Constant |
| **PR after the dropped beat** | Shorter than before it | Same as before it |
| **Site of block** | Usually the AV node | Usually below the AV node (His-Purkinje system) |
| **QRS width** | Usually narrow | Often wide (bundle branch block pattern) |
| **Common in healthy people** | Yes — athletes, during sleep | No |
| **Effect of exercise or atropine** | Conduction often improves | Often unchanged or worse |
| **Risk of complete heart block** | Low | Higher; can occur suddenly |
| **Pacemaker** | Rarely needed | Usually recommended |

**2:1 block is the grey zone.** When every second P wave is dropped, there is no run of consecutive beats to watch the PR, so the type cannot be confirmed from that strip alone. A narrow QRS and a long PR on the conducted beats point toward the AV node (Mobitz I behaviour); a wide QRS points lower. See [2:1 AV block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/2-1-av-block/).

## Common causes

- High vagal tone — athletes, sleep
- Beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, digoxin
- Inferior heart attack
- Myocarditis, Lyme disease
- After cardiac surgery
- Age-related conduction disease

Amiodarone and some other antiarrhythmic medicines can also slow the AV node. During an inferior heart attack, Wenckebach is usually temporary and resolves as the heart recovers.

## Symptoms of 2nd degree heart block type 1

Most people have none, particularly when the block occurs during sleep or in fit people. When symptoms happen, they come from the slower heart rate:

- Light-headedness or dizziness
- Tiredness or reduced exercise tolerance
- A sensation of a missed or skipped beat
- Rarely, fainting — which needs prompt assessment

## Should you worry?

Seen on a night-time monitor in a fit person, it needs no treatment. It deserves attention if it occurs while awake and active, if it causes dizziness or fainting, or if it appears with a wide QRS or during a heart attack. Progression to complete heart block is uncommon.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

### Wenckebach heart block in athletes and during sleep

Trained endurance athletes have high vagal tone — a strong "braking" signal from the nervous system to the heart. During rest and sleep this can slow the AV node enough to produce Wenckebach. The same happens in many healthy people at night, and it is often seen on Holter monitors. A useful reassuring sign is that the block disappears as the heart rate rises with activity.

## What usually happens next

1. Review of rate-slowing medicines
2. Holter monitor to see when it happens and how often
3. Exercise test — Wenckebach that disappears with exertion is reassuring
4. Pacemaker only if it causes symptoms with no reversible cause

Bloods may include potassium and thyroid function, and an echocardiogram may be considered if there are signs of structural heart disease. Do not stop a prescribed medicine on your own; your doctor may adjust it if it is contributing.

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Did this occur during sleep or while awake?
- Is my QRS narrow?
- Should I stop any medicine?
- Is there any sign of Mobitz II or a higher-grade block?
- Do I need any restrictions on exercise or driving?

## A worked example

> **Holter summary:** *"Sinus rhythm, rate 42–148. Episodes of second degree AV block, Mobitz type I, 01:00–05:30. No pauses over 3 seconds. No higher-grade AV block."*
> **Patient:** 31-year-old marathon runner, no symptoms, monitor fitted after a slow pulse was noticed at a sports medical.

The Wenckebach episodes all occurred at night, when the heart rate was lowest. The QRS was narrow, and daytime recordings — including during a run — showed normal 1:1 conduction. This is the classic pattern of high vagal tone in a trained athlete. No treatment was needed and no restriction on training was advised.
@@FAQ@@
Q: Is Mobitz 1 dangerous?
A: Usually not. It is often a normal finding in athletes and during sleep.
Q: What is the difference between Mobitz 1 and Mobitz 2?
A: In Mobitz 1 the PR lengthens before a beat drops; in Mobitz 2 the PR is constant and beats drop without warning. Mobitz 2 is more serious.
Q: Does Wenckebach need a pacemaker?
A: Rarely — only when it causes symptoms and cannot be reversed.
Q: Is Wenckebach the same as second degree heart block type 1?
A: Yes. Wenckebach, Mobitz type 1, Mobitz I and second degree AV block type 1 are all names for the same rhythm.
Q: Can second degree AV block type 1 go away?
A: Often. It may come and go with sleep and activity, and it frequently resolves if a contributing medicine is changed or after recovery from an inferior heart attack or myocarditis.
Q: Can I exercise with Wenckebach heart block?
A: Most people with Wenckebach and no symptoms can, especially when the block disappears with exertion. Check with your doctor, who may suggest an exercise test first.
Q: What is a 4:3 Wenckebach?
A: A cycle of four P waves and three QRS complexes — three beats are conducted with lengthening PR intervals, then the fourth P wave is blocked.
