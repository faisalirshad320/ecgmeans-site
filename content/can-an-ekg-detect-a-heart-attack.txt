## Can an EKG detect a heart attack?

Yes, often. But it is not a perfect test, and it is never used alone.

An EKG (also called an ECG; the two names mean the same test) records the heart's electrical signals for about ten seconds. When part of the heart muscle loses its blood supply, the injured muscle changes those signals. The EKG can see that change, sometimes within minutes.

How well it works depends on the type of heart attack:

- **STEMI** (ST-elevation myocardial infarction). A coronary artery is usually fully blocked. The EKG shows a clear pattern, so this type is the one the EKG detects best. It is a time-critical emergency, and the EKG is what triggers the team to open the artery.
- **NSTEMI** (non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction). The artery is usually partly blocked, or blood flow is cut off on and off. The EKG may show ST depression or T wave changes, or it may look completely normal. Most heart attacks seen in hospitals are of this type.

So the honest answer is: an EKG can show a heart attack, and when it does the result is very useful. When it does not, a heart attack is still possible. That is why doctors pair it with blood tests. See [STEMI vs NSTEMI](https://www.ecgmeans.com/stemi-vs-nstemi/) for a side-by-side comparison.

**Get urgent help:** Call 911 for chest pain or pressure, pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck or back, shortness of breath, cold sweat, nausea, or sudden lightheadedness. Do not drive yourself, and do not go to a clinic for "just an EKG". Paramedics can record an EKG in the ambulance and send it ahead to the hospital, which is often the fastest route to treatment.

## What does a heart attack look like on an EKG?

Doctors look for a pattern in groups of neighboring leads (the twelve "views" of the heart), not one odd wave. The main signs are:

| EKG sign | What it looks like | What it suggests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ST elevation** | The flat segment after the QRS spike is raised above the baseline in two or more neighboring leads | Full-thickness injury from a blocked artery (STEMI). The leads involved point to the wall of the heart affected |
| **Reciprocal ST depression** | ST segment pushed down in the leads facing the opposite side of the heart | Supports a true STEMI rather than a harmless mimic |
| **Hyperacute T waves** | Tall, broad, bulky T waves | A very early sign, sometimes seen before ST elevation appears |
| **ST depression or T wave inversion** | ST segments dip below the baseline, or T waves flip downward | Reduced blood flow; common in NSTEMI and unstable angina |
| **New left bundle branch block** | Wide QRS complexes with a typical shape | Can hide heart attack signs; doctors use special rules (Sgarbossa criteria) to read it |
| **Pathological Q waves** | Deep, wide downward dips at the start of the QRS | Heart muscle that has died. Usually develops over hours to days, and often stays for life |

Some heart attack patterns are less obvious. A **posterior** heart attack shows ST depression in the front chest leads rather than elevation. **Wellens** and **de Winter** patterns are warning signs of a critically narrowed artery even without classic ST elevation. Doctors are trained to look for these "STEMI equivalents".

Learn more about each sign: [ST elevation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/st-elevation/), [ST depression](https://www.ecgmeans.com/st-depression/), [hyperacute T waves](https://www.ecgmeans.com/hyperacute-t-waves/), [pathological Q waves](https://www.ecgmeans.com/pathological-q-waves/) and the [Sgarbossa criteria](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sgarbossa-criteria/).

### Heart attack EKG vs normal EKG

In a normal EKG, the ST segment sits flat on the baseline, T waves are upright in most leads, and there are no deep Q waves. In a STEMI, the ST segment in the affected leads curves upward, sometimes merging with the T wave into a "tombstone" shape, with mirror-image depression elsewhere. The difference can be striking. With NSTEMI, the difference from normal can be small or absent, which is why the tracing alone cannot settle the question.

## Why a normal EKG does not rule out a heart attack

This is the most important point on the page. Several things can make an EKG look normal, or nearly normal, during a heart attack:

1. **Timing.** Changes can come and go as a clot forms and breaks up. A ten-second recording may catch a quiet moment.
2. **Location.** Some parts of the heart, especially the back wall and the area supplied by the circumflex artery, are poorly seen by the standard twelve leads.
3. **NSTEMI by nature.** Many smaller heart attacks never cause ST elevation at all.
4. **Existing EKG changes.** A pacemaker, bundle branch block or thick heart muscle can hide new changes.

That is why emergency guidelines say the EKG should be recorded and read within about 10 minutes of arrival, and then repeated if the first one is not conclusive and symptoms continue.

## How doctors confirm or rule out a heart attack

The EKG is the starting point. The diagnosis is built from three pieces together.

1. **Serial EKGs.** Repeat tracings, often every 15–30 minutes while pain continues, can catch changes that were not there at first. Comparing with an old EKG also helps tell a new change from a long-standing one.
2. **Troponin blood tests.** Troponin is a protein released when heart muscle is injured. Under the international definition of a heart attack, a rise in troponin is required for the diagnosis. Modern high-sensitivity tests are usually repeated after 1–3 hours to look for a rising or falling level.
3. **Your symptoms and history.** Chest pain pattern, risk factors, and other findings guide how cautious the team needs to be.

If those are reassuring, some people go on to have a stress test or coronary CT scan to look for a narrowed artery. See [does an EKG show blockage?](https://www.ecgmeans.com/does-an-ekg-show-blockage/) for what those tests add.

## Can an EKG detect a past heart attack?

Often, yes. A heart attack that has fully healed can leave a lasting mark. The most common one is a **pathological Q wave** in the leads that face the scarred area. Other lasting signs include poor R wave progression across the chest leads or persistent T wave inversion.

Your report might say something like:

- *"Inferior infarct, age undetermined"*
- *"Anterior infarct, age undetermined"*
- *"Septal infarct, age undetermined"*

"Age undetermined" means the software sees Q waves that could fit a heart attack but cannot tell when it happened. Importantly, these machine statements are often wrong. Lead placement, body shape, breast tissue, lung disease or the heart's position can all create Q waves without any heart attack. A doctor usually confirms or dismisses the finding with an echocardiogram, which shows whether that wall of the heart moves normally. See [inferior infarct, age undetermined](https://www.ecgmeans.com/inferior-infarct-age-undetermined/) and [anterior infarct, age undetermined](https://www.ecgmeans.com/anterior-infarct-age-undetermined/).

The reverse is also true. Q waves can fade over years, and NSTEMIs often leave no Q waves at all, so a normal EKG does not prove you never had one.

## Silent heart attacks

A silent heart attack is one that happened without classic symptoms, or with symptoms mistaken for indigestion, tiredness or a pulled muscle. It is more common in people with diabetes, older adults and women. The American Heart Association notes that reduced blood flow can occur without any obvious warning.

Silent heart attacks are often first discovered when an EKG done for another reason shows Q waves. If that happens, your doctor will usually:

- Order an echocardiogram to look for an area of weak or scarred muscle
- Check cholesterol, blood sugar and blood pressure
- Consider tests for narrowed arteries
- Discuss medicines that lower the risk of a future heart attack

A silent heart attack is still a real heart attack, and the follow-up is the same as for one that caused symptoms.

## Panic attack or heart attack: what a normal EKG means

Panic attacks can cause chest tightness, a pounding heart, breathlessness, sweating and tingling. These overlap with heart attack symptoms, so many people end up in the emergency room, have a normal EKG and normal troponin, and are told it was anxiety.

If your EKG and repeat troponin tests were normal, that is genuinely reassuring for that episode. It does not mean every future chest pain is anxiety. New, different or exertion-related chest pain still needs to be checked. For more on this, see [heart palpitations but normal ECG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/heart-palpitations-normal-ecg/).

## Can other things look like a heart attack on an EKG?

Yes. Several conditions cause ST elevation without a blocked artery. These "mimics" include:

- [Early repolarization](https://www.ecgmeans.com/early-repolarization/), a common harmless pattern in young, fit people
- [Pericarditis](https://www.ecgmeans.com/pericarditis-ecg/), inflammation of the sac around the heart
- Left bundle branch block and left ventricular hypertrophy
- High potassium, Brugada pattern, and some other rare causes

This is why EKG findings are always read alongside symptoms, older tracings and blood tests.

## What usually happens next

If your EKG was done because of chest pain:

1. **ST elevation or a STEMI equivalent:** emergency treatment to open the artery, usually by catheter (angioplasty and stent), as fast as possible.
2. **ST depression, T wave changes, or a rise in troponin:** admission for NSTEMI or unstable angina care. Many people have a coronary angiogram within a day or two.
3. **Normal EKG and normal repeat troponin:** heart attack is unlikely for that episode. You may be sent home with advice and a plan for further outpatient testing.

If an old heart attack was found on a routine EKG, expect an echocardiogram and a review of your heart risk factors.

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Did my EKG show signs of a current heart attack, an old one, or neither?
- Were my troponin levels checked more than once?
- Has my EKG been compared with an older one?
- If my report says "infarct, age undetermined", do I need an echocardiogram to check?
- Do I need a stress test or a coronary CT scan?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Will an EKG show a heart attack right away?
A: Sometimes. A STEMI usually shows within minutes, but changes from an NSTEMI may be subtle or appear only on a repeat EKG. That is why troponin blood tests are always checked as well.
Q: Can you have a normal EKG and still be having a heart attack?
A: Yes. A normal first EKG is common in NSTEMI. Doctors repeat the EKG and check troponin over a few hours before ruling a heart attack out.
Q: How long after a heart attack does it show on an EKG?
A: Acute changes such as hyperacute T waves and ST elevation can appear within minutes. Q waves from dead muscle usually develop over hours to days and may last for life.
Q: Can an EKG show a heart attack from years ago?
A: Often, through pathological Q waves. However, some old heart attacks leave no trace, and some "old infarct" statements on reports turn out to be false alarms.
Q: Can a panic attack cause a heart attack pattern on an EKG?
A: A panic attack can speed up the heart and cause minor nonspecific ST or T wave changes, but it does not cause the ST elevation and Q waves of a true heart attack.
Q: Is a blood test better than an EKG for a heart attack?
A: They answer different questions. The EKG shows a STEMI fastest, while troponin confirms heart muscle injury. Both are needed.
Q: Can a smartwatch detect a heart attack?
A: No. Smartwatch EKGs record a single lead, which is designed mainly to spot atrial fibrillation. They cannot rule a heart attack in or out. Call 911 for chest pain.
