@@LEDE@@
Left anterior fascicular block (LAFB, or left anterior hemiblock) means one of the two small branches of the left bundle — the anterior fascicle — is delayed. The result is a marked leftward shift of the QRS axis with only slight widening of the QRS. It is a common finding, particularly with age, and on its own is generally harmless.
@@BODY@@
## What "Left anterior fascicular block" means

The left bundle divides into an anterior and a posterior fascicle. If the anterior one is blocked, the impulse enters the left ventricle via the posterior fascicle and spreads upward and leftward, pulling the axis to between −45° and −90°. The QRS stays under 120 ms because the rest of the wiring is intact.

When LAFB coexists with right bundle branch block the combination is called bifascicular block. That is more significant, because only one of the three fascicles is left conducting.

## What it looks like on the ECG

Left axis deviation beyond −45°, a small q in lead I and a small r in lead III (qR in I, rS in III), a normal or slightly widened QRS.

## Common causes

- Age-related fibrosis of the conduction system
- High blood pressure and left ventricular hypertrophy
- Coronary artery disease
- Cardiomyopathy
- Aortic valve disease
- Sometimes no cause is found

## Should you worry?

Usually harmless

Isolated LAFB carries a very low risk and needs no treatment. It becomes more important when it is combined with right bundle branch block, a long PR interval, or symptoms such as fainting, since these suggest wider conduction disease.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

## What usually happens next

1. Usually nothing beyond blood pressure control
2. Echocardiogram if other findings suggest structural disease
3. Monitoring if bifascicular block is present with symptoms

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Is this isolated, or combined with another block?
- Is my blood pressure well controlled?
- Do I need any follow-up?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Is LAFB dangerous?
A: Not on its own. It is one of the most common and least concerning conduction findings.
Q: What causes left anterior fascicular block?
A: Most often age-related change in the conduction tissue, or the effects of long-standing high blood pressure.
Q: Does LAFB need a pacemaker?
A: No. A pacemaker is considered only for bifascicular block with symptoms or documented higher-grade block.
