@@LEDE@@
Left anterior fascicular block (LAFB) — often printed as "left anterior hemiblock" — is one of the most common conduction findings on an adult ECG (EKG). It changes the direction of the heart's electrical signal rather than how well the heart pumps. This page explains what LAFB looks like, why it happens, when doctors pay closer attention (mainly when it is combined with another block or with symptoms), and which tests, if any, usually follow.
@@BODY@@
## What "left anterior fascicular block" means

The heart's ventricular wiring has three main branches, often called **fascicles**:

- The **right bundle branch**, to the right ventricle
- The **left anterior fascicle**, to the front and upper part of the left ventricle
- The **left posterior fascicle**, to the back and lower part of the left ventricle

The left anterior fascicle is thin and has a single blood supply, so it is the branch most easily affected by age-related scarring or strain. When it is delayed or blocked, the impulse enters the left ventricle through the posterior fascicle first and then spreads **upward and to the left**. That changes the overall direction (axis) of the QRS complex to strongly leftward — typically between −45° and −90°.

The rest of the wiring is still working, so the ventricles are activated almost on time and the **QRS remains narrow (under 120 ms)**. That is why LAFB is called a "hemiblock": only half of the left bundle is affected.

## What LAFB looks like on the ECG

The AHA/ACCF/HRS ECG recommendations define LAFB in adults by these features:

| Feature | LAFB criterion |
| --- | --- |
| Frontal QRS axis | Between −45° and −90° (marked [left axis deviation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-axis-deviation/)) |
| Lead aVL | qR pattern (small q, tall R) |
| R-peak time in aVL | 45 ms or longer |
| Leads II, III and aVF | rS pattern (small r, deep S) |
| QRS duration | Under 120 ms (may be slightly longer than your usual QRS) |
| Other cause of left axis | Absent — for example no inferior heart attack or other explanation |

You can see where your own axis falls using our [ECG axis calculator](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-axis-calculator/). Some reports print "left axis deviation" when the axis is between −30° and −45°; that range on its own does not meet the criteria for LAFB.

LAFB can also cause some side effects on the tracing that confuse automated reports:

- **Taller R waves in aVL and I**, which can trigger a false "possible [left ventricular hypertrophy](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-ventricular-hypertrophy/)" message
- **Small R waves in the early chest leads**, which can produce [poor R wave progression](https://www.ecgmeans.com/poor-r-wave-progression/)
- **Small q waves in V2–V3** if the chest electrodes are placed a little high, which can look like an old [septal infarct](https://www.ecgmeans.com/septal-infarct/)
- **Masking or mimicking an old inferior heart attack**, because both change the shape of the QRS in II, III and aVF

## Causes of LAFB

**Common causes**

- **Age-related fibrosis** of the conduction system — the most frequent cause in older adults
- **Long-standing high blood pressure** and left ventricular hypertrophy
- **Coronary artery disease**, including a previous heart attack involving the front wall
- **No identifiable cause** — LAFB is often an isolated finding

**Less common causes**

- **Aortic valve disease**, or after aortic valve surgery or TAVI/TAVR
- **Cardiomyopathy** (including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy) and myocarditis
- **Congenital heart disease**, such as some forms of atrial septal defect
- **High potassium** — [hyperkalemia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/hyperkalemia-ecg/) can cause a temporary fascicular block
- **Infiltrative or infectious disease** — for example sarcoidosis, or Chagas disease in people from Latin America
- **Inherited or degenerative conduction disease** (sometimes called Lev or Lenègre disease)

## Is LAFB dangerous?

**Isolated LAFB carries a very low risk and needs no treatment.** It rarely progresses to complete heart block on its own. Some large population studies found that people with LAFB had a modestly higher long-term rate of atrial fibrillation and heart failure, but this is thought to reflect the blood pressure, ageing and heart disease that often cause LAFB, rather than the block itself.

LAFB becomes more significant when it comes with any of these **risk features**:

- **Right bundle branch block as well** — the combination is called [bifascicular block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/bifascicular-block/), because two of the three branches are affected
- **A long PR interval** ([first-degree AV block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/first-degree-av-block/)) alongside bifascicular block — loosely labelled [trifascicular block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/trifascicular-block/)
- **Fainting, near-fainting or a very slow pulse**
- **Signs of heart muscle or valve disease** — breathlessness, swelling, a murmur or an abnormal echocardiogram
- **It is new**, especially if it appeared during chest pain

## Symptoms to watch for

LAFB itself does not cause symptoms. If you have it, report:

- Blackouts, fainting or sudden dizziness
- A pulse that is unusually slow or irregular
- Breathlessness on mild effort, or ankle swelling
- Chest pain or pressure on exertion

## What tests usually come next

1. **Nothing further** is needed for many people with isolated LAFB and no symptoms.
2. **Comparison with old ECGs** to see whether it is new.
3. **Blood pressure check** and cardiovascular risk review, because high blood pressure is a common driver.
4. **Echocardiogram** if there is a murmur, breathlessness, high blood pressure with LVH voltage, or other findings that suggest structural heart disease. See [EKG vs echocardiogram](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ekg-vs-echocardiogram/).
5. **Holter or event monitoring** if there are blackouts or palpitations, to look for intermittent higher-grade heart block.
6. **Blood tests** such as potassium if the LAFB is new and unexplained.

## Treatment and management

- **No specific treatment** is needed for LAFB.
- **Treat the cause:** good blood pressure control, cholesterol and diabetes management, and treatment of any valve or heart muscle disease.
- **Pacemaker:** not needed for LAFB. A pacemaker is considered only when there is documented advanced heart block or, in some cases, bifascicular block with unexplained fainting.
- **Medicines:** your doctor may review drugs that slow conduction if you also have other conduction delays.

## Special groups

- **Older adults:** LAFB becomes more common with age and is usually a sign of age-related fibrosis rather than a new problem.
- **Athletes and young people:** LAFB is not a typical training effect. International athlete ECG criteria treat isolated left axis deviation as a "borderline" finding that usually needs no further tests in a symptom-free athlete; an echocardiogram is considered if there are symptoms, a relevant family history or other ECG findings.
- **Children:** a leftward axis in a child is uncommon and may prompt an echocardiogram, because some congenital heart conditions (such as an ostium primum atrial septal defect) cause it.
- **Before surgery:** isolated LAFB does not usually change anaesthetic plans.

## LAFB vs look-alike findings

| Finding | How it differs |
| --- | --- |
| [Left posterior fascicular block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-posterior-fascicular-block/) | The opposite: right axis deviation with qR in III; much rarer and more often linked to heart disease |
| [Left bundle branch block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-bundle-branch-block/) | Both left fascicles affected: QRS 120 ms or more with a broad notched R in I, V5–V6 |
| [Incomplete left bundle branch block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/incomplete-left-bundle-branch-block/) | QRS 110–119 ms with LBBB-like shape, rather than a pure axis shift |
| [Inferior infarct](https://www.ecgmeans.com/inferior-infarct-age-undetermined/) | Pathological Q waves (qR or QS) in II, III and aVF rather than rS |
| [Right bundle branch block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/right-bundle-branch-block/) | Wide QRS with rSR' in V1; when combined with LAFB it becomes bifascicular block |
| [Intraventricular conduction delay](https://www.ecgmeans.com/intraventricular-conduction-delay/) | A wide QRS without a typical bundle branch shape |

## Get urgent help

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase. Call 911 in the US, 999 in the UK or 112 in Europe.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- What is my QRS axis in degrees, and does it meet the criteria for LAFB?
- Is the LAFB isolated, or combined with right bundle branch block or a long PR interval?
- Was it present on my earlier ECGs?
- Is my blood pressure well controlled, and do I need an echocardiogram?
- Should I watch for any particular symptoms?
- Do any of my medicines slow the heart's conduction?
@@FAQ@@
Q: What does left anterior fascicular block mean on an ECG?
A: It means the front branch of the left bundle is delayed, so the left ventricle is activated from below and the heart's electrical axis swings sharply left. The QRS stays narrow.
Q: Is LAFB dangerous?
A: Not on its own. Isolated LAFB is one of the most common and least concerning conduction findings; it matters more when combined with right bundle branch block, a long PR interval or fainting.
Q: Is left anterior hemiblock the same as LAFB?
A: Yes. Left anterior hemiblock and left anterior fascicular block are two names for the same finding.
Q: What causes left anterior fascicular block?
A: Most often age-related scarring of the conduction tissue, long-standing high blood pressure or coronary artery disease. Less common causes include valve disease, cardiomyopathy and high potassium.
Q: Does LAFB need a pacemaker?
A: No. A pacemaker is considered only if there is documented advanced heart block, or sometimes bifascicular block with unexplained blackouts.
Q: Can LAFB go away?
A: Usually it is permanent, but LAFB caused by high potassium, a heart procedure or acute ischaemia can sometimes resolve when the cause is treated.
Q: Can LAFB look like a heart attack on an ECG?
A: It can confuse the picture. LAFB may cause poor R wave progression or small q waves in the chest leads and can mimic or mask an old inferior heart attack, so doctors interpret it alongside symptoms and imaging.
