@@LEDE@@
"Tombstoning" is an informal name, but it describes one of the most urgent patterns an ECG can show. It is not a separate disease; it is a shape of ST elevation seen in a heart attack that is usually large and still evolving. Most people will meet the term in a hospital discharge letter, a paramedic's notes, or while studying ECGs. This page explains what the tombstone ECG looks like, which artery is usually involved, why it predicts a rougher course, how it is treated, and what recovery typically involves.
@@BODY@@
## What a tombstone ECG means

On a normal ECG (also called an EKG — they are the same test), the ST segment is a flat line between the end of the QRS complex and the T wave. In a heart attack caused by a completely blocked artery, the injured muscle shifts that segment upward — [ST elevation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/st-elevation/).

In **tombstoning**, the ST elevation is so tall and so convex (dome-shaped, bulging upward) that:

- The R wave is small or barely visible
- The ST segment shoots straight up from the QRS
- The ST segment and T wave fuse into one broad, rounded hump

Seen across several leads, each beat resembles a gravestone — hence "tombstone ECG" or "tombstone EKG." The pattern reflects **a large area of heart muscle under severe, full-thickness injury**.

## What tombstoning looks like on the ECG

| Feature | Typical STEMI | Tombstone STEMI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ST elevation height | Usually 1–4 mm | Often very large — several mm or more |
| ST segment shape | Straight or mildly convex | Strongly convex, dome-shaped |
| R wave | Present | Small, short or lost |
| ST–T relationship | ST and T distinguishable | ST merges with the T wave into one hump |
| Leads involved | A group of neighboring leads | Usually many leads — often V1–V6, plus I and aVL |
| Reciprocal ST depression | Often present | Usually present in the opposite leads (e.g. II, III, aVF) |

The commonest version is in the **chest leads (V1–V6)**, often extending to **I and aVL**. That distribution points to a large **anterior** or **anterolateral** heart attack. Tombstoning can also appear in inferior heart attacks, though less often.

The pattern can evolve rapidly: in the first minutes of a heart attack you may see tall [hyperacute T waves](https://www.ecgmeans.com/hyperacute-t-waves/) that then grow into tombstone ST elevation, and later [pathological Q waves](https://www.ecgmeans.com/pathological-q-waves/) as muscle dies.

## Which artery is usually blocked?

Tombstoning in the chest leads most often means a **proximal left anterior descending (LAD) artery occlusion** — a blockage near the start of the artery that supplies the front wall of the heart, the septum and often part of the side wall. Because the LAD feeds a large territory, a blockage high up affects a lot of muscle.

This is sometimes nicknamed the "widow-maker" pattern of LAD occlusion. Clinicians look for supporting clues of a proximal blockage, such as ST elevation in aVL and lead I, ST elevation in aVR or V1, or a new [right bundle branch block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/right-bundle-branch-block/).

Related high-risk LAD patterns that can appear *before* full ST elevation include [de Winter T waves](https://www.ecgmeans.com/de-winter-t-waves/) and, after the artery reopens, [Wellens syndrome](https://www.ecgmeans.com/wellens-syndrome/).

## Why tombstoning is a poor prognosis marker

Studies comparing people with and without the tombstone shape found that tombstoning goes with:

- **Larger infarcts** — more heart muscle damaged
- **Lower left ventricular ejection fraction** — a weaker pump afterwards
- **More heart failure and cardiogenic shock** (very low blood pressure from the heart failing)
- **More dangerous rhythms**, including [ventricular fibrillation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ventricular-fibrillation/)
- **Higher death rates**

In one Polish study of 207 STEMI patients (Kardiologia Polska, 2006), about a quarter had tombstoning on arrival, and their in-hospital death rate was markedly higher than in those without it. That study was done partly in the era before routine rapid angioplasty; outcomes are better today when the artery is opened quickly — but tombstoning still flags a high-risk heart attack.

For an individual, the most important factor in outcome is **time**: how fast the blocked artery is opened.

## Symptoms that go with a tombstone ECG

People with this pattern are usually very unwell:

- **Severe chest pain or pressure**, often spreading to the arm, jaw, neck or back
- **Sweating, nausea or vomiting**
- **Shortness of breath**
- **Light-headedness, fainting or collapse**
- **Cold, clammy skin and confusion** if blood pressure falls

**Get urgent help:** chest pain or pressure lasting more than a few minutes, especially with sweating, breathlessness or fainting, is a medical emergency. Call 911 (999 in the UK, 112 in Europe) — do not drive yourself. Emergency teams can record an ECG on the way and alert the cath lab before you arrive.

## How it is treated

A tombstone STEMI is treated as a time-critical emergency:

1. **Emergency cardiac catheterization (primary PCI).** The blocked artery is opened with a balloon and usually a stent. Guidelines aim for this within about 90 minutes of first medical contact — faster is better.
2. **Clot-dissolving medicine (thrombolysis)** if a cath lab cannot be reached quickly enough.
3. **Blood thinners and antiplatelet medicines**, given by the emergency team.
4. **Close monitoring** for dangerous rhythms, heart failure and shock, with treatments such as defibrillation, medicines to support blood pressure, or mechanical heart support if needed.

After the artery is opened, the ST elevation usually falls over hours to days. Fast resolution of the ST segments is a good sign that blood flow has returned to the muscle.

## A worked example

*An illustrative case built from typical features — not a real patient.*

> **Paramedic ECG:** *"Massive ST elevation V1–V6, I, aVL with reciprocal ST depression II, III, aVF. Tombstone morphology. STEMI alert."*
> **Patient:** 61-year-old man, smoker, with 40 minutes of crushing central chest pain, sweating and vomiting.

The paramedics sent the ECG ahead and took him straight to the cath lab, bypassing the emergency department. Angiography showed a complete blockage at the start of the left anterior descending artery. It was opened and stented about 70 minutes after the first ECG.

The next morning, his ST segments had fallen by more than half, a good sign that blood was reaching the muscle again. His echocardiogram showed a moderately weakened front wall, and he started heart-protecting medicines and cardiac rehabilitation. A repeat echo three months later showed partial recovery.

**The lesson:** the tombstone pattern correctly signalled a large, high-risk heart attack. The speed of the pre-hospital ECG and direct transfer to the cath lab is what limited the damage.

## Complications doctors watch for

Because tombstoning signals a large injured area, the care team watches closely for:

- **Dangerous rhythms** — ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation, most common in the first 48 hours
- **Heart failure** — breathlessness and fluid in the lungs from a weakened pump
- **Cardiogenic shock** — very low blood pressure needing medicines or mechanical support
- **Heart block** — slow rhythms when the conduction system is affected
- **A clot inside the left ventricle** — from a weak, poorly moving front wall, which may need blood thinners
- **Pericarditis** — inflammation of the heart's lining days after the attack
- **Rare mechanical problems** — such as a tear in the septum or the heart wall, which need emergency surgery

## Tombstoning vs other causes of ST elevation

Not all ST elevation is a heart attack. The tombstone shape, however, is very rarely anything else.

| Condition | ST elevation shape | Distribution | Clues |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tombstone STEMI** | Huge, convex, merging with T | One coronary territory, with reciprocal depression | Severe chest pain, very unwell |
| [Pericarditis](https://www.ecgmeans.com/pericarditis-ecg/) | Concave (saddle-shaped), modest | Widespread, not one territory; PR depression | Pain worse lying flat, better sitting forward |
| [Early repolarization](https://www.ecgmeans.com/early-repolarization/) | Concave, small, with J-point notch | Mostly chest leads | Young, healthy, no symptoms, stable over time |
| Left ventricular aneurysm | Persistent ST elevation with deep Q waves | Old infarct territory | Previous heart attack weeks or more ago |
| [Left bundle branch block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-bundle-branch-block/) | Discordant ST elevation | Leads with negative QRS | Wide QRS; needs [Sgarbossa criteria](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sgarbossa-criteria/) |

The comparison of [STEMI vs NSTEMI](https://www.ecgmeans.com/stemi-vs-nstemi/) and our page on whether [an EKG can detect a heart attack](https://www.ecgmeans.com/can-an-ekg-detect-a-heart-attack/) give the broader picture.

## What usually happens next

After a tombstone STEMI is treated:

1. **Coronary care unit monitoring** for 1–3 days or longer
2. **An echocardiogram** to measure heart function and look for complications such as a clot in the heart or a weak wall
3. **Long-term medicines** — usually antiplatelets, a statin, a beta-blocker, and often an ACE inhibitor or similar, as decided by your cardiologist
4. **Cardiac rehabilitation** — supervised exercise and education
5. **Repeat heart function check** after several weeks; if the pump stays very weak, your team may discuss an implantable defibrillator (ICD)
6. **Risk factor control** — stopping smoking, blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes and weight

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Which artery was blocked, and how quickly was it opened?
- How much heart muscle was damaged, and what is my ejection fraction?
- Did my ST elevation come down after the procedure?
- Do I need a repeat echo, and could I need an ICD?
- When can I start cardiac rehabilitation, return to work and drive?
@@FAQ@@
Q: What does tombstoning mean on an EKG?
A: It describes very large, dome-shaped ST elevation that merges with the T wave so each beat looks like a tombstone. It indicates a large, acute heart attack.
Q: Which artery causes a tombstone ECG?
A: Most often a blockage near the start of the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, producing a large anterior heart attack. Other arteries can occasionally cause it.
Q: Is a tombstone ECG always a heart attack?
A: Almost always. Very rarely, other conditions can mimic it, but the pattern is treated as a STEMI and an emergency until proven otherwise.
Q: Can you survive a tombstone heart attack?
A: Yes. Many people survive, especially when the blocked artery is opened quickly. Tombstoning signals higher risk, which is why speed of treatment matters so much.
Q: Why is it called a widow-maker?
A: "Widow-maker" is a nickname for a blockage of the proximal LAD, which supplies a large part of the heart. Tombstone ST elevation is one ECG pattern seen with that blockage.
Q: Does tombstone ST elevation go away?
A: After the artery is opened, the ST elevation usually falls over hours to days. Persistent elevation weeks later can suggest a scarred or bulging area of the heart wall.
