@@BODY@@
## What "de Winter T waves" mean

Described by Robert de Winter and colleagues, this pattern points to an acute blockage of the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery. It is important because it does not show the usual ST elevation, so it can be missed — yet it carries the same urgency as a classic ST-elevation heart attack (STEMI).

## What it looks like on the ECG

In the chest leads (V1–V6): upsloping ST depression of 1 mm or more at the J point, continuing into tall, broad, symmetrical T waves. There is often slight ST elevation in lead aVR. The pattern tends to persist rather than evolve into typical ST elevation, and it reflects an actively blocked artery.

## Why it matters

De Winter T waves are treated as a STEMI-equivalent, meaning the artery is blocked now and heart muscle is dying. Prompt recognition and emergency treatment to open the artery can save a large area of heart muscle.

**This is an emergency.** Chest pain or pressure, breathlessness, sweating, nausea, or arm or jaw pain with this pattern needs immediate medical attention — call emergency services.

## Why they count as a STEMI-equivalent
The de Winter pattern does not show the ST elevation that defines a classic STEMI, yet it reflects an artery that is blocked right now. Studies have shown it behaves like a STEMI in terms of the artery involved and the muscle at risk, so guidelines treat it the same way — with emergency angiography — rather than as a lesser "ST-depression" finding that could be managed more slowly.

## What usually happens next

1. Immediate recognition as a STEMI-equivalent, not a minor ST change
2. Urgent cardiology activation and serial troponin blood tests
3. Emergency coronary angiography
4. Opening the blocked LAD artery, usually with a stent

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Was my pattern recognised as a STEMI-equivalent?
- Which artery was blocked, and was it opened in time?
- What treatment and follow-up do I need now?
