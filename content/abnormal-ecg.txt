## What does an abnormal ECG mean?

An abnormal ECG — or abnormal EKG; the two abbreviations mean exactly the same test (see [ECG vs EKG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-vs-ekg/)) — is the machine's way of saying "something here is outside my normal settings, please have a doctor look." It does not say what, how serious, or whether it matters for you. To answer those questions you need the lines printed above it.

## What "Abnormal ECG" means

Every modern ECG machine runs an interpretation program. It measures the tracing, compares the numbers with built-in thresholds, prints the individual findings, and then adds an overall verdict: Normal ECG, Otherwise normal ECG, Borderline ECG or Abnormal ECG. The verdict is driven by the most significant finding on the list above it, so the useful information is in those individual lines, not in the word "abnormal".

Computer reads are deliberately over-sensitive. They are designed not to miss anything, so they flag a lot of things that a doctor then dismisses — a heart rate of 58, a slightly leftward axis, a T wave that is a little flat, or electrodes that were placed a centimetre too high. That is why reports carry the words "unconfirmed" or "physician review required".

## Normal vs abnormal ECG: how the verdict is chosen

| Verdict on the report | What it usually means | Typical example findings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Normal ECG** | All measurements within the program's limits | Normal sinus rhythm, normal intervals |
| **Otherwise normal ECG** | One minor finding the program considers unimportant | Sinus arrhythmia, a single early beat |
| **[Borderline ECG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/borderline-ecg/)** | A finding close to a threshold | Slightly long PR, minor axis shift |
| **Abnormal ECG** | At least one finding outside the limits | Sinus bradycardia, left axis deviation, nonspecific T wave changes, bundle branch block, atrial fibrillation, possible old infarct |

Notice that the "abnormal" row covers everything from a fit person's slow heart rate to a heart attack. That is why the word on its own tells you so little. A reliable reference for the normal numbers is our [normal ECG values](https://www.ecgmeans.com/normal-ecg-values/) page, and [how to read your ECG report](https://www.ecgmeans.com/how-to-read-ecg-report/) walks through each line of the printout.

## What does an abnormal EKG look like?

### What it looks like on the ECG

There is no single appearance. Look at the lines printed above the verdict: each one names a specific finding, such as sinus bradycardia, left axis deviation, nonspecific T wave abnormality or possible septal infarct. Search each phrase separately — that is what the verdict is based on.

On the tracing itself, the change might be in any part of the waveform:

- **Rate:** beats closer together (fast) or further apart (slow) than usual
- **Rhythm:** irregular spacing, early beats, or missing P waves
- **Intervals:** a longer gap from P to QRS, a wider QRS, or a longer QT
- **Shape:** flattened or inverted T waves, raised or lowered ST segments, deep Q waves, unusually tall or small complexes

An "irregular EKG" usually refers to the rhythm part of this list — often harmless [sinus arrhythmia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-arrhythmia/) or occasional extra beats, sometimes atrial fibrillation.

## What can cause an abnormal EKG?

### Common causes

- Normal variants: athletic slow heart rate, early repolarization, sinus arrhythmia in young people
- Technical problems: misplaced chest leads, swapped arm leads, movement or muscle tremor
- Body build, pregnancy, chest-wall shape or lung disease changing the electrical picture
- High blood pressure causing thickening of the heart muscle
- Electrolyte changes (potassium, calcium, magnesium) and some medicines
- Rhythm problems such as atrial fibrillation or frequent extra beats
- Reduced blood supply to the heart, or an old or current heart attack

### Harmless vs more significant findings

| Usually harmless or minor | Usually worth a closer look | Needs prompt or urgent review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Sinus bradycardia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-bradycardia/) in a fit person | [Left ventricular hypertrophy](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-ventricular-hypertrophy/) | ST elevation with chest pain |
| Sinus arrhythmia | Bundle branch block, especially if new | New AFib with a fast rate |
| [Early repolarization](https://www.ecgmeans.com/early-repolarization/) | [Atrial fibrillation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/atrial-fibrillation/) | Very slow rate or high-grade heart block |
| Incomplete right bundle branch block | Pathological Q waves ("possible old infarct") | Markedly prolonged QT (over 500 ms) |
| Mild axis deviation, occasional PACs or PVCs | Persistent ST–T changes | Wide-complex fast rhythm |

## Can anxiety or stress cause an abnormal EKG?

Yes. Anxiety and stress raise adrenaline, which speeds the heart into [sinus tachycardia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-tachycardia/), and fast breathing can flatten T waves for a few minutes. Shivering or tensing muscles adds electrical noise that the machine may misread. These are real changes on the tracing but they are not signs of heart disease, and a repeat ECG when you are calm often looks normal. If you feel anxious during the recording, it is fine to tell the technician and ask to rest for a few minutes first.

## Should I worry about an abnormal EKG?

The word itself should not frighten you; the context should guide you. An abnormal ECG in a person with no symptoms, found at a routine check, is very often a variant or a minor finding. The same word matters much more if the ECG was taken because of chest pain, breathlessness, fainting or palpitations, or if it has changed compared with your previous tracing.

A useful way to think about it:

1. **No symptoms, routine test, minor finding** — usually reassurance or a repeat ECG.
2. **No symptoms, but a significant finding** (for example LVH, a bundle branch block or AFib) — follow-up tests, but rarely urgent.
3. **Symptoms such as chest pain, fainting or breathlessness** — the ECG is one part of an urgent assessment, whatever the verdict says.

Also remember that a normal ECG does not guarantee a healthy heart either — see [if my ECG is normal, is my heart OK?](https://www.ecgmeans.com/if-ecg-is-normal-is-my-heart-ok/).

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

## What usually happens next

1. A clinician reads the actual tracing and either confirms or overrules the computer
2. It is compared with any earlier ECG you have — a change is more meaningful than a long-standing pattern
3. If it fits with symptoms or risk factors you may be offered blood tests, a repeat ECG, an echocardiogram, a Holter monitor or a stress test
4. If nothing fits, the usual outcome is reassurance and no further testing

An [echocardiogram](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ekg-vs-echocardiogram/) is the most common next step when the question is whether an electrical finding reflects a structural problem.

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Which specific finding made the machine call this abnormal?
- Has it changed from my last ECG?
- Does it need any follow-up test, or can it be left alone?
- Could anxiety, a medicine or how the stickers were placed explain it?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Can an abnormal ECG be nothing?
A: Yes. Computer interpretations over-call abnormalities, and lead placement, body shape and normal variants account for a large share of them. A clinician's review of the tracing is what counts.
Q: Can anxiety cause an abnormal ECG?
A: Anxiety can raise the heart rate into the sinus tachycardia range and, with fast breathing, can cause minor T wave changes. These are real findings but not signs of heart disease.
Q: Can I have an abnormal ECG and a healthy heart?
A: Yes. An ECG records electrical activity, not structure or pumping strength. If there is doubt, an echocardiogram is the usual test to show that the heart itself is normal.
Q: What does "abnormal ECG, unconfirmed" mean?
A: It means the computer has interpreted the tracing but a doctor has not yet reviewed and signed it. The final, confirmed report may be different.
Q: Is an abnormal EKG the same as a heart attack?
A: No. A heart attack is only one of many possible causes, and most abnormal EKG results are due to minor or harmless findings. Specific changes such as ST elevation, together with symptoms, are what point to a heart attack.
Q: Can an abnormal EKG go back to normal?
A: Often, yes. Findings caused by anxiety, a fast rate, electrolyte changes, a medicine or poor lead placement frequently disappear on a repeat ECG.
