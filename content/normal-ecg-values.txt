@@ANSWER@@
Normal EKG results in adults: heart rate 60–100 bpm, PR interval 120–200 ms, QRS under 120 ms, QTc under about 440 ms (men) or 460 ms (women), and QRS axis −30° to +90°. A normal EKG shows sinus rhythm, an upright P wave before every narrow QRS, a flat ST segment, and upright T waves in most leads.
@@LEDE@@
Every number printed at the top of an ECG report has a normal range. This chart puts them all in one place for adults, with links to what it means when a value falls outside it.
@@BODY@@
If your report says "normal ECG" or "normal sinus rhythm", the machine found every measurement inside the expected range and saw no abnormal pattern. This page explains what normal EKG results look like, gives a full normal ECG values chart for adults, and shows how to read each EKG interval yourself. (EKG and ECG are the same test — see [ECG vs EKG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-vs-ekg/).)

## Normal ECG values chart

| Measurement | Normal adult range | Outside the range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heart rate | 60–100 bpm | Under 60: [bradycardia](/sinus-bradycardia/); over 100: [tachycardia](/sinus-tachycardia/) |
| P wave | Under 120 ms wide and under 2.5 mm tall in lead II | Wide or notched: [left atrial enlargement](/left-atrial-enlargement/); tall: [right atrial enlargement](/right-atrial-enlargement/) |
| PR interval | 120–200 ms (3–5 small boxes) | Under 120: [short PR](/short-pr-interval/); over 200: [first degree AV block](/first-degree-av-block/) |
| QRS duration | Under 120 ms; ideally under 100–110 ms | Wide: [QRS interval](/qrs-interval/), [bundle branch block](/heart-block-conduction/) |
| QTc | Under about 440 ms (men) or 460 ms (women) | Over 500 ms is clearly prolonged: [QT interval](/qt-interval/) |
| QRS axis | −30° to +90° | [Left](/left-axis-deviation/) or [right axis deviation](/right-axis-deviation/) |
| ST segment | At the baseline (isoelectric) | [ST elevation](/st-elevation/) or [ST depression](/st-depression/) |
| T wave | Upright in I, II and V3–V6; inverted in aVR | [T wave inversion](/t-wave-inversion/), [peaked T waves](/peaked-t-waves/) |

Use the [QTc calculator](/qtc-calculator/), [heart rate calculator](/ecg-heart-rate-calculator/) and [axis calculator](/ecg-axis-calculator/) to check your own numbers.

## What does a normal EKG look like?

A normal EKG is a repeating pattern of the same three shapes, beat after beat, at a steady pace:

1. **A small, rounded P wave** — the upper chambers (atria) firing. It is upright in lead II and comes before every QRS.
2. **A short flat gap (the PR segment)** while the signal pauses in the AV node.
3. **A tall, narrow QRS complex** — the main pumping chambers (ventricles) firing. It is the sharpest, biggest spike on the strip.
4. **A flat ST segment** sitting on the baseline.
5. **A smooth, upright T wave** — the ventricles resetting for the next beat.

On a normal 12-lead printout, the beats are evenly spaced, every P wave is followed by a QRS, and every QRS is preceded by a P wave. The R waves grow taller across the chest leads from V1 to V5. Lead aVR looks "upside down" — that is expected, because it views the heart from the opposite direction.

The phrase you will see at the top for a normal rhythm is **normal sinus rhythm**, meaning the beat starts in the heart's natural pacemaker at 60–100 beats per minute. See [sinus rhythm](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-rhythm/) for what that means.

## EKG intervals explained

ECG intervals are the time gaps between the waves. Each one shows how long a stage of the heartbeat takes.

| ECG interval | What it measures | Normal adult value | Small boxes at 25 mm/s |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [PR interval](https://www.ecgmeans.com/pr-interval/) | Time from the start of atrial firing to the start of ventricular firing | 120–200 ms | 3–5 |
| [QRS interval](https://www.ecgmeans.com/qrs-interval/) | Time for the ventricles to fire | Under 120 ms | Under 3 |
| [QT interval](https://www.ecgmeans.com/qt-interval/) | Time for the ventricles to fire and fully reset | Depends on heart rate — corrected as QTc | — |
| QTc | QT adjusted for heart rate | Under about 440 ms (men) or 460 ms (women) | — |
| RR interval | Time between two beats | 600–1,000 ms (equals 60–100 bpm) | 15–25 |

The QT interval shortens as the heart speeds up, so it is "corrected" to a standard rate (the QTc) before it is judged. A QTc over 500 ms is clearly prolonged and raises the risk of dangerous rhythms — see [QT prolongation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/qt-prolongation/).

## Reading the numbers on paper

Standard ECG paper runs at 25 mm/s with a sensitivity of 10 mm per mV. One small box is 40 ms wide and 0.1 mV tall; one large box is 200 ms wide. A normal PR interval therefore spans 3–5 small boxes, and a normal QRS stays under 3 small boxes.

A quick heart-rate trick: count the large boxes between two R waves and divide 300 by that number. Three large boxes is 100 bpm; five is 60 bpm. Anywhere between three and five large boxes is a normal rate. For a step-by-step method, see [how to read an EKG strip](https://www.ecgmeans.com/how-to-read-an-ekg-strip/) and the [ECG interpretation cheat sheet](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-interpretation-cheat-sheet/).

## Other normal findings

- **Q waves:** small "septal" q waves in leads I, aVL, V5 and V6 are normal when they are narrow (under 40 ms) and shallow.
- **R-wave progression:** R waves grow from V1 to about V4–V5, with the switch from mainly negative to mainly positive (the transition) around V3–V4. See [poor R wave progression](/poor-r-wave-progression/).
- **ST thresholds:** small amounts of J-point elevation are normal. Under the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction, new ST elevation at the J point in two or more neighbouring (contiguous) leads is significant at 1 mm in most leads; in V2–V3 the threshold is 2 mm in men aged 40 or over, 2.5 mm in men under 40 and 1.5 mm in women. New horizontal or downsloping ST depression of 0.5 mm or more in two contiguous leads is also significant.
- **T waves** may be inverted in lead III and V1 in healthy adults, and in V1–V3 in children and some young adults.

## Normal waves, one by one

| Wave or segment | Normal appearance in adults | Common report terms when it is not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P wave | Upright in leads I and II, under 120 ms wide and under 2.5 mm tall; one before each QRS | [Inverted P wave](https://www.ecgmeans.com/inverted-p-wave/), [P mitrale](https://www.ecgmeans.com/p-mitrale/), [P pulmonale](https://www.ecgmeans.com/p-pulmonale/) |
| QRS complex | Narrow and sharp; R waves grow taller from V1 across to V5 | [Wide QRS complex](https://www.ecgmeans.com/wide-qrs-complex/), [low voltage QRS](https://www.ecgmeans.com/low-voltage-qrs/) |
| ST segment | Flat, at the level of the baseline | ST elevation, ST depression |
| T wave | Smooth and upright in most leads, same direction as the QRS | [T wave inversion](https://www.ecgmeans.com/t-wave-inversion/), [biphasic T wave](https://www.ecgmeans.com/biphasic-t-wave/) |
| U wave | Small or absent; if seen, follows the T wave in the same direction | [U wave](https://www.ecgmeans.com/u-wave/) |

## How to check your own EKG results

1. **Find the rhythm line.** "Sinus rhythm" or "normal sinus rhythm" is the normal starting point.
2. **Check the heart rate.** Between 60 and 100 bpm is the standard normal range.
3. **Compare each interval** (PR, QRS, QT/QTc) with the chart above.
4. **Look at the axis.** Between −30° and +90° is normal.
5. **Read the interpretation lines.** Phrases such as "normal ECG" or "within normal limits" mean nothing abnormal was flagged. Anything else can be looked up on this site.
6. **Remember the machine is a first pass.** A clinician confirms every reading, and a computer statement is sometimes revised.

## Normal variants that are not abnormal

Several findings look unusual but are common in healthy people. Reports may mention them, sometimes with the word "borderline".

| Finding | Why it can be normal |
| --- | --- |
| [Sinus arrhythmia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-arrhythmia/) | Heart rate speeds up on breathing in and slows on breathing out, especially in young people |
| [Sinus bradycardia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-bradycardia/) in athletes or during rest | A fit heart pumps more per beat, so it needs fewer beats |
| [Early repolarization](https://www.ecgmeans.com/early-repolarization/) | Slight J-point and ST elevation, common in young, active adults |
| [Incomplete right bundle branch block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/incomplete-right-bundle-branch-block/) | A minor conduction delay seen in many healthy people |
| Isolated T wave inversion in lead III or V1 | A positional variant (see above) |

If the only comment on your report is one of these, see [borderline ECG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/borderline-ecg/) for what it usually means.

## Why normal ranges have limits

These are adult values. Children have faster heart rates, shorter intervals and a more rightward axis. Athletes often have slower rates, longer PR intervals and taller QRS complexes without any disease. A value just outside the range is common in healthy people, so it is always judged alongside symptoms and the rest of the tracing.

## What normal EKG results do and do not rule out

A normal resting EKG is reassuring. It shows no sign of a current heart rhythm problem, no pattern of a recent major heart attack, and no strong evidence of thickened heart muscle at the moment it was recorded.

It cannot see everything. A resting EKG lasts about ten seconds, so palpitations that come and go may be missed, and narrowed coronary arteries often produce a normal tracing at rest. That is why your doctor may still suggest a Holter monitor, a stress test or an echocardiogram if your symptoms point that way. Read more in [if my ECG is normal, is my heart OK?](https://www.ecgmeans.com/if-ecg-is-normal-is-my-heart-ok/) and [what can an EKG detect](https://www.ecgmeans.com/what-can-an-ekg-detect/).

**Get urgent help** if you have chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a racing heartbeat that will not settle — even if an earlier EKG was normal. Call 911 (US), 999 (UK) or 112 (EU).
@@FAQ@@
Q: What are normal ECG values?
A: Heart rate 60–100 bpm, PR 120–200 ms, QRS under 120 ms, QTc under about 440 ms in men or 460 ms in women, and axis −30° to +90°.
Q: What is a normal PR interval?
A: 120–200 ms, which is 3 to 5 small boxes on standard ECG paper.
Q: What is a normal QRS duration?
A: Under 120 ms, and ideally under 100–110 ms.
Q: Are ECG normal values different for children?
A: Yes. Children have faster heart rates, shorter PR and QRS intervals and a more rightward axis, so adult ranges do not apply.
Q: What does "normal ECG" mean on my report?
A: It means every measured value was within the normal range and the machine found no abnormal pattern. A clinician still confirms the reading.
Q: Can a normal EKG miss a heart problem?
A: Yes. A resting EKG is a ten-second snapshot, so intermittent rhythm problems and many cases of narrowed arteries can be missed. Your doctor may suggest further tests if your symptoms suggest them.
Q: Is a heart rate of 55 on an EKG normal?
A: Often, yes. Rates in the 50s are common in fit people and during rest. It matters mainly if you have dizziness, fainting or tiredness.
