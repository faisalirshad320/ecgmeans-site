## What is normal sinus rhythm?

Normal sinus rhythm (often shortened to NSR) is the first line on most healthy ECG reports. It answers one question — where is each heartbeat starting, and is it happening at a normal pace? — and the answer is "in the right place, at the right speed." ECG and EKG are the same test, so "normal sinus rhythm EKG" and "ECG normal sinus rhythm" mean exactly the same thing.

## What "Sinus rhythm" means

The sinus (sinoatrial) node is a cluster of cells in the right upper chamber. It fires, the atria contract — producing the P wave — the impulse pauses at the AV node, then spreads through the ventricles to produce the QRS. If every QRS is preceded by a normal-looking P wave at a regular pace, the rhythm is sinus.

Reports often print "sinus rhythm with …". The part after "with" is the actual finding: sinus rhythm with PACs (occasional early beats), with sinus arrhythmia (normal breathing-related variation), with first degree AV block (a long PR interval), and so on.

## What is sinus rhythm in an ECG? The criteria

### What it looks like on the ECG

An upright P wave before every QRS in lead II, a constant PR interval of 120–200 ms, and evenly spaced beats at 60–100 per minute.

Doctors and ECG software check the following points. All of them need to be true for the rhythm to be called normal sinus rhythm.

| Criterion | Normal value | What it tells you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Heart rate** | 60–100 beats per minute (adults at rest) | Below 60 is sinus bradycardia; above 100 is sinus tachycardia |
| **P wave before every QRS** | One P wave per QRS, every beat | The beat started in the atria and was conducted normally |
| **P wave direction** | Upright in leads I and II, inverted in aVR | The impulse started high in the right atrium — the sinus node |
| **PR interval** | 120–200 ms (3–5 small squares), constant | Normal delay through the AV node |
| **QRS duration** | Less than 120 ms (usually under 100 ms) | The ventricles were activated through the normal wiring |
| **Regularity** | Evenly spaced beats (slight breathing variation is normal) | No extra or dropped beats |

You can learn more about each measurement on our pages on the [P wave](https://www.ecgmeans.com/p-wave/), the [PR interval](https://www.ecgmeans.com/pr-interval/) and the [QRS interval](https://www.ecgmeans.com/qrs-interval/). For a full table of normal numbers, see [normal ECG values](https://www.ecgmeans.com/normal-ecg-values/).

### How the rate is measured

On standard paper at 25 mm/s, divide 300 by the number of large squares between two R waves. Three large squares gives 100; five gives 60. So in normal sinus rhythm the R waves sit roughly three to five large squares apart. Our [ECG heart rate calculator](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-heart-rate-calculator/) does this for you.

## Sinus rhythm vs normal sinus rhythm vs other sinus rhythms

The word "sinus" only describes where the beat starts. The rate and pattern decide the full name:

| Report wording | Rate (adults) | Pattern | Usually means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Normal sinus rhythm** | 60–100 | Regular | Normal |
| **[Sinus bradycardia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-bradycardia/)** | Below 60 | Regular | Often normal in fit people and during sleep |
| **[Sinus tachycardia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-tachycardia/)** | Above 100 | Regular | The heart responding to exercise, stress, fever or illness |
| **[Sinus arrhythmia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-arrhythmia/)** | Any | Speeds up on breathing in, slows on breathing out | Normal, especially in young people |
| **Sinus rhythm with PACs / PVCs** | Any | Occasional early beats | Usually harmless |

Children have faster normal rates, so a child's sinus rhythm is judged against age-specific ranges rather than the adult 60–100.

## Sinus rhythm with sinus arrhythmia

"Sinus rhythm with sinus arrhythmia" (or "normal sinus rhythm with sinus arrhythmia") is one of the most common lines on a healthy young person's ECG. It means every beat still starts in the sinus node with a normal P wave, but the gap between beats changes gently with breathing — shorter when you breathe in, longer when you breathe out. It reflects a healthy, responsive nervous system and needs no treatment. The machine sometimes flags it as "irregular," but a doctor will recognise it at a glance.

## How the heart makes a normal sinus rhythm

It helps to follow one heartbeat from start to finish, because each step produces a part of the ECG:

1. **The sinus node fires.** A small group of pacemaker cells high in the right atrium sends out a signal on its own, roughly 60–100 times a minute at rest. This step is too small to see on the ECG.
2. **The atria are activated (P wave).** The signal spreads across both upper chambers, which squeeze blood down into the ventricles. Because the signal travels from the top right towards the bottom left, the P wave points upward in leads I and II.
3. **The AV node pauses the signal (PR segment).** The AV node deliberately holds the signal back for a fraction of a second so the ventricles can fill.
4. **The ventricles are activated (QRS complex).** The signal races down the bundle of His and the bundle branches, and the ventricles contract to pump blood to the lungs and body.
5. **The ventricles reset (T wave).** The muscle recharges, ready for the next beat.

When this sequence repeats in the same order, at a steady pace, every time, the result is normal sinus rhythm. If any step happens in the wrong place or at the wrong time, the report will name a different rhythm or add a "with …" finding.

## Normal sinus rhythm in athletes, during sleep and in older people

The 60–100 range is a convention, not a hard biological rule. Many healthy people sit outside it some of the time:

- **Athletes** often have resting rates in the 40s or 50s. The rhythm is still sinus; the report will say sinus bradycardia, and that is usually normal for them.
- **During sleep** the rate commonly drops below 60 in everyone.
- **Children** run faster; a 4-year-old's resting rate of 110 can be entirely normal.
- **Older adults** may have a slightly longer PR interval and fewer breathing-related rate changes, while the rhythm itself remains sinus.

Some cardiologists argue that 50–90 is a better "normal" range for adults at rest. You will see both ranges in textbooks; your report will use the machine's built-in 60–100.

## Normal sinus rhythm vs atrial fibrillation

Because atrial fibrillation (AFib) is the most common rhythm problem in adults, people often want to know how it differs from sinus rhythm.

| | Normal sinus rhythm | [Atrial fibrillation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/atrial-fibrillation/) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Where the beat starts** | The sinus node | Chaotic signals across both atria |
| **P waves** | One normal P wave before every QRS | None; a wavy baseline instead |
| **Spacing of beats** | Regular | Irregularly irregular |
| **Stroke risk** | Normal | Raised; doctors assess the need for a blood thinner |

If a report says "sinus rhythm" after a previous diagnosis of AFib, it means the heart was back in its normal rhythm at the time of that ECG.

## Is normal sinus rhythm good?

### Should you worry?

Usually harmless

None. It is the result you want. Note that sinus rhythm describes only the rhythm; other lines on the report describe wave shapes and intervals.

That last point matters. An ECG can show normal sinus rhythm alongside another finding, such as a bundle branch block or T wave changes, and an ECG cannot see everything — for example, it does not show most narrowed arteries between symptoms. See [if my ECG is normal, is my heart OK?](https://www.ecgmeans.com/if-ecg-is-normal-is-my-heart-ok/) for what a normal ECG can and cannot rule out.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

## Common causes

- This is the normal state — no cause is needed

## Normal sinus rhythm with palpitations

It is common to feel palpitations, then have an ECG that shows only normal sinus rhythm. That usually means the rhythm was normal during the ten seconds of recording — not necessarily during your symptoms. If symptoms come and go, a longer monitor such as a Holter or event recorder may be offered. Our page on [heart palpitations with a normal ECG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/heart-palpitations-normal-ecg/) explains the usual next steps.

## What usually happens next

1. Nothing is required for sinus rhythm itself
2. If there is a second line on the report ("with …"), that finding decides whether anything else is needed
3. If you had symptoms that the ECG did not capture, your doctor may suggest a longer recording

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Is there any other finding on my ECG besides sinus rhythm?
- Was my heart rate inside the normal range for my age?
- If my symptoms come and go, do I need a longer heart monitor?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Is sinus rhythm good or bad?
A: Good. It is the normal, healthy rhythm.
Q: What is the difference between sinus rhythm and normal sinus rhythm?
A: Sinus rhythm describes where the beat starts. Normal sinus rhythm also specifies a rate of 60–100; below that it is sinus bradycardia, above it sinus tachycardia.
Q: Can I have sinus rhythm and still have a heart problem?
A: Yes. The rhythm can be normal while other parts of the ECG, or the heart's structure, are not.
Q: What heart rate is normal sinus rhythm?
A: In adults at rest, 60–100 beats per minute. Children have higher normal rates that depend on age.
Q: Does "sinus" mean I have a sinus infection?
A: No. Here "sinus" refers to the sinus node, the heart's natural pacemaker. It has nothing to do with the nose or sinuses.
Q: What does "sinus rhythm, normal ECG" mean?
A: It means the rhythm is normal and the machine found no other abnormal measurements on the tracing. It is the best overall result an ECG can give.
