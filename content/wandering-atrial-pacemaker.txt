## What "wandering atrial pacemaker" means

Normally every beat starts in the **sinus node**, the heart's natural pacemaker at the top of the right atrium. Other cells in the atria, and around the AV node (the junction), can also act as pacemakers, but they usually stay quiet because the sinus node fires first.

In a wandering atrial pacemaker the leadership passes back and forth between the sinus node and other atrial pacemaker cells, often as breathing and vagal tone change. When the sinus node slows slightly — for example during breathing out or at rest — a different site takes over for a few beats, then hands control back. Each site produces a slightly different P wave, so the P waves vary in shape and the PR interval changes a little, while the rate remains normal.

It is essentially the slow-rate cousin of [multifocal atrial tachycardia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/multifocal-atrial-tachycardia/): the same shifting-pacemaker picture, but under 100 beats per minute and usually harmless. Reports may print it as "wandering atrial pacemaker", "wandering pacemaker", "WAP" or "multifocal atrial rhythm".

## What it looks like on the ECG

| Feature | Wandering atrial pacemaker |
| --- | --- |
| Heart rate | Under 100 bpm (often 60–100, sometimes slower) |
| P waves | At least three different shapes in the same lead; some may be [inverted](https://www.ecgmeans.com/inverted-p-wave/) when the beat starts low in the atria or near the junction |
| P before every QRS | Yes — every QRS is still preceded by a P wave, unless a junctional beat takes over |
| [PR interval](https://www.ecgmeans.com/pr-interval/) | Varies from beat to beat with the pacemaker site; may shorten when the site is near the AV node |
| Rhythm | Mildly irregular, with P–P and R–R spacing varying |
| QRS complex | Normal and narrow (under 120 ms) |
| Change pattern | Often gradual, shifting over several beats rather than abruptly |

## Why the P-wave shape changes

The P wave records electrical activity spreading across the atria. When a beat starts in the sinus node, high in the right atrium, the signal travels downwards and to the left, giving an upright, rounded P wave in lead II. When a beat starts lower down, or in the left atrium, the signal spreads in a different direction, so the P wave becomes flatter, more pointed, notched or upside down.

The distance from the starting site to the AV node also changes, which is why the PR interval shortens or lengthens slightly with each shift. None of this means the heart muscle is damaged; it simply shows that a different group of the heart's own pacemaker cells is leading for a few beats. Automated ECG software notices the changing P waves and may print "wandering atrial pacemaker" or "multiform P waves" even when a cardiologist would regard the tracing as normal.

The best place to see the changing P waves is usually a long rhythm strip of lead II. Our guide to the [P wave](https://www.ecgmeans.com/p-wave/) explains what a normal one looks like.

## Causes

### Common — usually a normal variant

- High vagal tone in children, teenagers and young adults
- Athletes and physically fit people
- Rest and sleep
- Older people, in whom it can appear without disease

### Less common — worth considering in context

- Lung disease such as COPD, where it can be a forerunner of multifocal atrial tachycardia
- Heart disease affecting the atria, including atrial enlargement
- Digoxin effect or toxicity (see [digoxin effect on ECG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/digoxin-effect-ecg/))
- Sinus node disease, when the sinus node is slow enough that other sites keep taking over

## Is a wandering atrial pacemaker dangerous?

In a healthy person it needs no treatment and causes no symptoms. It occasionally points to underlying lung or heart disease, particularly if it speeds up into multifocal atrial tachycardia, so the context is what matters rather than the rhythm itself.

Features that make doctors look further:

- The rate creeps **above 100**, turning it into multifocal atrial tachycardia
- The person has **lung disease, breathlessness or low oxygen levels**
- The person takes **digoxin**
- It comes with **very slow rates, pauses or dizziness**, raising the question of [sick sinus syndrome](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sick-sinus-syndrome/)
- Other abnormalities are present on the same ECG

## Symptoms

A wandering atrial pacemaker usually causes no symptoms at all and is found by chance. If you have palpitations, dizziness, fainting or breathlessness, these are more likely to come from something else — another rhythm, or a lung or heart condition — and should be assessed on their own merits.

## What tests come next?

1. **Usually none** — a young, fit, symptom-free person needs only reassurance.
2. **A longer rhythm strip or repeat ECG** to confirm the pattern and the rate.
3. **Holter or patch monitor** if there are symptoms, or if it alternates with a faster atrial rhythm or with pauses.
4. **Medication review and digoxin level** if you take digoxin.
5. **Assessment of lung or heart disease** (oxygen levels, chest examination, sometimes an echocardiogram) if there are symptoms or known disease.

## Treatment

The rhythm itself needs no treatment. When it is linked to an illness, care is directed at that cause — treating a lung condition, adjusting digoxin, or managing sinus node disease if it is causing symptomatic slow rates.

## Special groups

- **Children and young adults:** a common finding on routine and sports ECGs, usually reflecting high vagal tone.
- **Athletes:** often seen with sinus bradycardia and sinus arrhythmia as part of a well-trained heart.
- **Older adults:** can occur without disease, but is more likely to prompt a check for lung disease, medicines such as digoxin, or sinus node problems.
- **Pregnancy:** there is no specific concern; it is interpreted the same way as in anyone else, based on rate and symptoms.

## How it differs from look-alike findings

| Finding | P waves | Rate | Usual significance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wandering atrial pacemaker | Three or more shapes | Under 100 | Usually harmless |
| [Multifocal atrial tachycardia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/multifocal-atrial-tachycardia/) | Three or more shapes | Over 100 | Needs clinician review; usually linked to illness |
| [Sinus arrhythmia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-arrhythmia/) | All the same shape | Varies with breathing | Normal |
| [Ectopic atrial rhythm](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ectopic-atrial-rhythm/) | One abnormal shape, constant | Usually 60–100 | Depends on context |
| [Sinus rhythm with PACs](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-rhythm-with-pac/) | Mostly normal, with occasional early abnormal P waves | Normal | Usually harmless |
| [Junctional rhythm](https://www.ecgmeans.com/junctional-rhythm/) | Absent, or inverted near the QRS | Usually 40–60 | Depends on context |
| [Atrial fibrillation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/atrial-fibrillation/) | No distinct P waves; wavy baseline | Variable | Needs clinician review |

The irregular rhythm of a wandering atrial pacemaker can occasionally be misread by automated software or a smartwatch as atrial fibrillation. The giveaway is that clear, separate P waves are still present before each QRS.

## Get urgent help

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

## Questions to ask your doctor

- Is this just a normal variant for me?
- What was my heart rate — under or over 100?
- Is there any sign of lung or heart disease?
- Could any of my medicines, such as digoxin, be involved?
- Does it need any monitoring, or a repeat ECG?
@@FAQ@@
Q: What does wandering atrial pacemaker mean on an ECG?
A: It means the heartbeat's starting point shifts between the sinus node and other sites in the atria, producing at least three different P-wave shapes at a rate under 100. It is usually a harmless variant.
Q: Is a wandering atrial pacemaker dangerous?
A: Usually not. In a healthy person it is a benign normal variant that needs no treatment.
Q: What is the difference from multifocal atrial tachycardia?
A: They look alike, but a wandering atrial pacemaker runs under 100 beats per minute, while multifocal atrial tachycardia is over 100 and usually signals illness, most often lung disease.
Q: What causes a wandering atrial pacemaker?
A: Most often normal changes in vagal tone, especially in young people, athletes and during sleep. Less often it is linked to lung disease, heart disease, sinus node problems or digoxin.
Q: Does a wandering atrial pacemaker need treatment?
A: No, not in itself. Any treatment is directed at an underlying lung or heart condition, or a medicine effect, if one is present.
Q: Can a wandering atrial pacemaker be mistaken for atrial fibrillation?
A: Yes, occasionally, because the rhythm is slightly irregular. The difference is that a wandering atrial pacemaker still has distinct P waves before each beat, while atrial fibrillation has none.
Q: Is a wandering atrial pacemaker the same as an artificial pacemaker?
A: No. "Pacemaker" here refers to the heart's own natural pacemaker cells, not to an implanted device.
