Torsades de pointes (French for "twisting of the points") is a rhythm most people only meet in two places: a drug leaflet warning about "QT prolongation", or a hospital report after a fainting episode. It is rare, but it is the main reason doctors care so much about the QT interval. The good news is that torsades usually has an identifiable trigger — a medicine, a low blood salt, a very slow heart rate — and once that is found it can often be prevented. This page explains what the rhythm looks like, why it happens, how it is treated and how people at risk are protected.

## What is torsades de pointes?

Torsades de pointes (often shortened to TdP or "torsades") is a type of **polymorphic ventricular tachycardia** — a very fast rhythm starting in the ventricles in which the QRS complexes keep changing shape. What sets torsades apart from other polymorphic VT is that it happens **on a background of a prolonged QT interval**.

The QT interval measures how long the ventricles take to electrically reset after each beat. When that reset is slow and uneven, some heart cells can fire again before the others have recovered. Those extra signals, called early afterdepolarizations, can set off a spiralling wave of electrical activity that produces the twisting pattern.

Episodes are often short and stop on their own, causing palpitations, dizziness or a sudden faint. But a run can keep going, and torsades can degenerate into [ventricular fibrillation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ventricular-fibrillation/) and sudden cardiac death. That is why any suspected torsades is treated as an emergency.

## What torsades de pointes looks like on an ECG

The classic torsades de pointes ECG shows:

- **A fast, wide-complex rhythm**, usually around 160–250 beats per minute
- **QRS complexes that change in height and direction**, swelling and shrinking in a spindle-like way, so the peaks seem to rotate around the baseline
- **A prolonged QT interval** on the normal beats before the episode starts — usually the key clue
- **A typical start**: often a "short–long–short" sequence, where an early beat is followed by a pause and then another early beat that lands on the T wave and launches the run (see [R-on-T phenomenon](https://www.ecgmeans.com/r-on-t-phenomenon/))
- **Short bursts** that may stop by themselves and recur

The twisting is easiest to see in a long rhythm strip; in a single lead the complexes may just look chaotic. Large U waves and bizarre T waves often appear on the beats before an episode.

## Torsades vs other polymorphic ventricular tachycardia

Not every polymorphic VT is torsades. The distinction matters because the cause and treatment differ.

| Feature | Torsades de pointes | Other polymorphic VT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| QT interval between episodes | Prolonged | Normal |
| Most common cause | Medicines, low potassium/magnesium, slow heart rate, inherited long QT | Acute ischemia (heart attack), cardiomyopathy, some inherited conditions |
| Typical trigger pattern | Short–long–short sequence, pauses | Often no pause dependence |
| First-line drug treatment | IV magnesium; stop QT-prolonging drugs | Treat the ischemia; antiarrhythmics such as amiodarone may be used |
| Drugs to avoid | Further QT-prolonging drugs (including some antiarrhythmics) | Depends on cause |

How torsades sits among other dangerous rhythms is covered on our pages on [ventricular tachycardia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ventricular-tachycardia/) and [wide QRS complex](https://www.ecgmeans.com/wide-qrs-complex/).

## What causes torsades de pointes?

Anything that lengthens the QT interval can set the stage. Most cases involve more than one factor at once.

### Medicines (the most common cause)

Hundreds of drugs can lengthen the QT. Commonly mentioned groups include:

- Some antiarrhythmic drugs (for example sotalol, dofetilide, quinidine)
- Some antibiotics (macrolides such as erythromycin and azithromycin, fluoroquinolones)
- Some antifungals
- Some antipsychotic and antidepressant medicines
- Some anti-nausea drugs (such as ondansetron at high doses)
- Methadone and some other opioids

Having a QT-prolonging medicine does not mean torsades will happen. The risk rises when doses are high, kidney or liver problems raise drug levels, two QT-prolonging drugs are combined, or another risk factor is present. Never stop a prescribed medicine on your own — ask your doctor or pharmacist.

### Low blood salts

- **Low potassium** ([hypokalemia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/hypokalemia-ecg/))
- **Low magnesium**
- **Low calcium**

These are often caused by diuretics ("water pills"), vomiting, diarrhea, poor diet or alcohol use disorder.

### Other risk factors

- **Congenital (inherited) long QT syndrome** — a genetic condition affecting the heart's ion channels; it can cause torsades with exercise, emotion, sudden noise or sleep, depending on the type
- **Slow heart rate**, including [complete heart block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/third-degree-av-block/) and long pauses
- **Female sex** and **older age**
- **Heart disease**, especially heart failure and recent heart attack
- **Underactive thyroid**, recent stroke or bleeding in the brain
- **Starvation states** such as anorexia nervosa

## The link with long QT

Torsades de pointes and a long QT interval are closely tied. The QT is measured on the ECG and corrected for heart rate (QTc) — our [QTc calculator](https://www.ecgmeans.com/qtc-calculator/) does the arithmetic. As a rough guide:

| QTc | Interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| Under about 450 ms (men) / 460 ms (women) | Normal |
| About 450–500 ms | Borderline to prolonged — look for causes |
| Over 500 ms | Clearly prolonged; torsades risk rises significantly |
| Rise of more than 60 ms from baseline after a new drug | Concerning even if the absolute value is under 500 ms |

For a full explanation of how the interval is measured and what changes it, see [QT interval](https://www.ecgmeans.com/qt-interval/) and [QT prolongation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/qt-prolongation/).

## Symptoms of torsades de pointes

- Sudden **palpitations** or a racing, fluttering heartbeat
- **Dizziness or light-headedness**
- **Fainting** (syncope), sometimes with jerking movements that can be mistaken for a seizure
- **Collapse and cardiac arrest** if the rhythm continues or turns into VF

Some people have no symptoms because episodes are very brief and only appear on a monitor.

**Get urgent help:** call 911 (999 in the UK, 112 in Europe) for fainting, a racing heartbeat with dizziness, chest pain or breathlessness — especially if you take a QT-prolonging medicine or have been told you have long QT. If someone collapses and is not breathing normally, start CPR and use an AED.

## How torsades de pointes is treated

Treatment depends on whether the person is stable and is always given in hospital or by emergency services.

1. **Unstable or pulseless:** immediate **defibrillation** (an unsynchronized shock), as for ventricular fibrillation, along with CPR if there is no pulse.
2. **IV magnesium sulfate:** the first-line drug for torsades, even when the blood magnesium level is normal. It often stops episodes and prevents recurrences.
3. **Stop the trigger:** any QT-prolonging medicines are stopped, and potassium is replaced, often to the higher end of normal.
4. **Speed the heart up if needed:** because slow rates and pauses make torsades more likely, doctors may use temporary pacing at a faster rate, or occasionally isoproterenol, to shorten the QT.
5. **Continuous monitoring** until the QT has come back toward normal.

For **congenital long QT syndrome**, long-term treatment is guided by a specialist and may include beta-blockers, avoiding QT-prolonging drugs, and sometimes an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD).

## Preventing torsades de pointes

- **ECG checks** before and after starting certain high-risk medicines
- **Checking potassium and magnesium** in people on diuretics or with vomiting or diarrhea
- **Reviewing all medicines together**, including over-the-counter and herbal products, for combinations that lengthen the QT
- **Carrying a list** of medicines to avoid if you have inherited long QT syndrome
- **Telling every prescriber and pharmacist** about any past QT prolongation or torsades

## What usually happens next

After an episode, most people stay on a monitored bed while the cause is corrected. Typical next steps:

1. Repeat ECGs to follow the QTc
2. Blood tests for potassium, magnesium, calcium, kidney and thyroid function
3. A medication review and a clear list of drugs to avoid
4. An echocardiogram to look at heart structure
5. If no acquired cause is found, or the QT stays long, referral for genetic testing and family screening for long QT syndrome

### Questions to ask your doctor

- What do you think triggered the torsades — a medicine, a low blood salt, a slow heart rate?
- What was my QTc, and what is it now?
- Which medicines should I avoid in the future?
- Could this be inherited long QT syndrome? Should my family be tested?
- Do I need a pacemaker or ICD?
@@FAQ@@
Q: What does torsades de pointes mean?
A: It is French for "twisting of the points." It describes a polymorphic ventricular tachycardia in which the QRS peaks seem to twist around the baseline.
Q: Is torsades de pointes the same as polymorphic VT?
A: Torsades is one type of polymorphic VT — the type that occurs with a prolonged QT interval. Polymorphic VT with a normal QT is usually caused by ischemia and is treated differently.
Q: Why is magnesium given for torsades?
A: IV magnesium stabilizes the heart cells and suppresses the extra beats that trigger torsades. It works even when the blood magnesium level is normal.
Q: Can torsades de pointes stop on its own?
A: Yes, many episodes are short and stop by themselves, causing dizziness or fainting. Because it can turn into ventricular fibrillation, every episode needs urgent medical care.
Q: What QTc is dangerous?
A: A QTc over 500 ms is the commonly used threshold for a significantly increased risk of torsades. A rise of more than 60 ms after starting a medicine is also a warning sign.
Q: Which drugs cause torsades de pointes?
A: Examples include some antiarrhythmics, macrolide and fluoroquinolone antibiotics, some antipsychotics and antidepressants, some anti-nausea drugs and methadone. Risk depends on dose and other factors.
Q: Is torsades de pointes shockable?
A: Yes. If the person is unstable or has no pulse, it is treated with an unsynchronized shock (defibrillation), like ventricular fibrillation.
