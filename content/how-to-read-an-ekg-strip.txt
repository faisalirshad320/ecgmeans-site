## How to read an EKG strip: the 8-step method at a glance

ECG and EKG mean the same test, an electrocardiogram. "EKG" comes from the German spelling and is common in the US. Experienced readers do not take in a strip all at once. They go through it in a fixed order so nothing gets missed. If you are learning how to read an EKG strip, this is the order to practise:

1. **Paper speed and boxes.** Confirm the calibration so your measurements mean something.
2. **Heart rate.** Count how fast the ventricles are beating.
3. **Rhythm regularity.** Are the R waves evenly spaced?
4. **P waves.** Is there one upright P wave before every QRS?
5. **PR interval.** How long does the impulse take to get from the atria to the ventricles?
6. **QRS width.** Is the ventricular beat narrow or wide?
7. **QT / QTc.** How long does the ventricle take to recharge, corrected for heart rate?
8. **ST segment and T wave.** Is the baseline flat after the QRS, and are the T waves the expected shape?

On a full 12-lead EKG you would also check the **axis** after step 6. Our [ECG axis calculator](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-axis-calculator/) walks through it. A single rhythm strip (usually lead II) is mainly used for steps 1 to 8.

## Step 1: Paper speed and what the boxes mean

Standard EKG paper runs at **25 mm per second**. The grid has small boxes of 1 mm and large boxes of 5 mm (five small boxes on each side).

| Grid unit | Time (horizontal) | Voltage (vertical) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 small box (1 mm) | **0.04 s** (40 ms) | 0.1 mV |
| 1 large box (5 mm) | **0.20 s** (200 ms) | 0.5 mV |
| 5 large boxes | 1.0 s | — |
| 30 large boxes | **6.0 s** | — |
| 10 mm tall | — | **1 mV** (standard calibration) |

Look for the calibration pulse, a small rectangle usually at the start of the strip. At standard gain it is 10 mm tall (1 mV). If it is 5 mm tall (half gain) or 20 mm tall (double gain), every height on the strip is scaled to match. If the paper speed is 50 mm/s, every interval looks twice as long. Check this before measuring anything.

**How to measure EKG strips accurately:** count boxes from the start of one wave to the start or end of the next. Calipers help, but the edge of a sheet of paper works too. Mark two points, then slide the paper along the grid.

## Step 2: Work out the heart rate

There are three standard methods. Use the one that fits the rhythm.

- **300 method (regular rhythms, quick):** count the large boxes between two R waves and divide 300 by that number. One large box = 300, two = 150, three = 100, four = 75, five = 60, six = 50. Memorise "300, 150, 100, 75, 60, 50."
- **1500 method (regular rhythms, precise):** count the small boxes between two R waves and divide 1500 by that number. 20 small boxes = 75 bpm.
- **6-second method (irregular rhythms):** count the QRS complexes in a 6-second strip (30 large boxes) and multiply by 10. This is the method to use for atrial fibrillation.

A resting adult rate of 60 to 100 bpm is normal. Under 60 is bradycardia and over 100 is tachycardia, though fit people often sit in the 50s. The [ECG heart rate calculator](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-heart-rate-calculator/) does the arithmetic for you.

## Step 3: Is the rhythm regular?

Measure the R-to-R distance across several beats. Small variations of a box or so are normal, and in young people the rate often speeds up as they breathe in ([sinus arrhythmia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-arrhythmia/)). Then sort the strip into one of these patterns:

| Pattern | What it suggests |
| --- | --- |
| Regular | Sinus rhythm, flutter with fixed block, SVT, VT, junctional rhythm |
| Regular with occasional early beats | PACs or PVCs on a sinus background |
| Regularly irregular (a repeating pattern) | Bigeminy, trigeminy, Wenckebach (Mobitz I) |
| Irregularly irregular (no pattern at all) | Atrial fibrillation, multifocal atrial tachycardia, flutter with variable block |

## Step 4: Look at the P waves

The P wave is the small bump before the QRS. It shows the atria firing. Ask four questions:

1. Is there a P wave at all?
2. Is there **one** P wave before **every** QRS, and a QRS after every P wave?
3. Are the P waves upright in lead II and all the same shape?
4. Are there extra P waves that are not followed by a QRS?

Upright, identical P waves, each followed by a QRS, mean the beat started in the sinus node (sinus rhythm). No P waves with a wavy baseline suggests [atrial fibrillation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/atrial-fibrillation/). A sawtooth baseline suggests [atrial flutter](https://www.ecgmeans.com/atrial-flutter/). More P waves than QRS complexes points to heart block. Our [P wave guide](https://www.ecgmeans.com/p-wave/) covers abnormal shapes.

## Step 5: Measure the PR interval

Measure from the **start of the P wave to the start of the QRS**. Normal is **120 to 200 ms**, or 3 to 5 small boxes.

- **Longer than 200 ms (5 small boxes) and constant:** [first-degree AV block](https://www.ecgmeans.com/first-degree-av-block/).
- **Gets longer each beat until a QRS drops:** second-degree block, Mobitz I (Wenckebach).
- **Constant, but some P waves have no QRS:** second-degree block, Mobitz II.
- **No fixed relationship between P and QRS:** third-degree (complete) block.
- **Shorter than 120 ms:** possible pre-excitation (WPW) or a junctional rhythm.

See the [PR interval page](https://www.ecgmeans.com/pr-interval/) for more detail.

## Step 6: Measure the QRS width

Measure from the first deflection of the QRS to where it returns to baseline (the J point). Normal is **under 120 ms (3 small boxes)**, and usually 80 to 100 ms.

- **Narrow QRS (under 120 ms):** the impulse used the normal fast wiring. The beat started above the ventricles.
- **Wide QRS (120 ms or more):** a bundle branch block, a beat that started in the ventricles (PVC, ventricular tachycardia), a paced beat, or a drug or electrolyte effect. See [wide QRS complex](https://www.ecgmeans.com/wide-qrs-complex/).

A fast, regular, wide-complex rhythm is treated as ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise.

## Step 7: Measure the QT and calculate the QTc

The QT runs from the **start of the QRS to the end of the T wave**. Because the QT shortens as the heart speeds up, it is corrected for rate (QTc). The most common correction is Bazett's: QTc = QT ÷ √RR, with RR in seconds.

A quick check: if the QT is less than half the R-to-R interval at a normal rate, it is probably not long. As a rough guide, a QTc above about 450 ms in men or 460 to 470 ms in women is prolonged, and above 500 ms carries a higher risk of a dangerous rhythm. Use the [QTc calculator](https://www.ecgmeans.com/qtc-calculator/) instead of doing the square root by hand.

## Step 8: Check the ST segment and T wave

The ST segment runs from the end of the QRS to the start of the T wave. It should sit level with the baseline (the TP segment). Look for:

- **ST elevation:** raised above baseline. In the right setting this can mean a heart attack. See [ST elevation](https://www.ecgmeans.com/st-elevation/).
- **ST depression:** dipping below baseline, seen with strain, ischemia or digoxin.
- **T waves:** normally upright in lead II and following the direction of the QRS. Tall, peaked T waves suggest high potassium. Inverted T waves have many causes.

Be careful here. A single monitoring lead **cannot rule out a heart attack**. ST changes need a full 12-lead EKG, and they have to be read alongside symptoms.

## Worked example: reading a lead II strip

Here is a 6-second lead II strip, taken at 25 mm/s with a 10 mm calibration pulse.

| Step | What we measured | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Paper and calibration | 25 mm/s, 10 mm pulse | Standard; measurements valid |
| 2. Rate | 4 large boxes (20 small) between R waves; 8 QRS in 6 s | 300 ÷ 4 = 75 bpm; 1500 ÷ 20 = 75; 8 × 10 = 80 (rough) |
| 3. Rhythm | R-R intervals all 20 small boxes | Regular |
| 4. P waves | Upright, identical, one before every QRS | Sinus origin |
| 5. PR interval | 4 small boxes | 160 ms, normal |
| 6. QRS width | 2 small boxes | 80 ms, narrow |
| 7. QT / QTc | QT 9 small boxes = 360 ms; RR = 0.80 s | QTc = 360 ÷ √0.8 ≈ 402 ms, normal |
| 8. ST / T | ST level with baseline; T upright | No obvious ST-T change |

**Interpretation:** normal sinus rhythm at 75 bpm, with normal intervals.

Notice that the 6-second count (80) differs slightly from the box methods (75). That is expected, because the 6-second method is an estimate. For a regular rhythm, trust the box methods. For an irregular rhythm, the 6-second count is the better choice.

## Common mistakes beginners make

- **Skipping the calibration check.** At half gain, a large QRS can look small, and the reverse is also true.
- **Reading artifact as an arrhythmia.** Shivering, a loose electrode or patient movement can mimic VT or VF. Always check the patient, not just the monitor. See [ECG artifact](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-artifact/).
- **Using the 300 method on an irregular rhythm.** It gives a different answer on every beat.
- **Measuring the QT in a lead with a flat T wave.** Use the lead where the end of the T wave is clearest, often lead II or V5.
- **Jumping to a diagnosis first.** Pattern recognition improves with practice, but the steps are what stop you missing a second finding.

## EKG for dummies: why the method matters more than memorising

Most people searching "EKG for dummies" want shortcuts. The best one is the fixed order above. Common rhythms such as sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation, flutter, heart blocks and ventricular tachycardia each fail at a predictable step:

| Rhythm | Step where it shows itself |
| --- | --- |
| Sinus bradycardia / tachycardia | Step 2 (rate), with everything else normal |
| Atrial fibrillation | Step 3 (irregularly irregular) and step 4 (no P waves) |
| Atrial flutter | Step 4 (sawtooth flutter waves) |
| First-degree AV block | Step 5 (PR over 200 ms) |
| Complete heart block | Steps 4–5 (P waves and QRS unrelated) |
| Bundle branch block | Step 6 (QRS 120 ms or more) |
| Ventricular tachycardia | Steps 2 and 6 (fast and wide) |
| Long QT | Step 7 |

To practise, work through our [ECG rhythm strips](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-rhythm-strips/) library, then test yourself with the [EKG practice strips](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ekg-practice-strips/). Keep the [ECG interpretation cheat sheet](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-interpretation-cheat-sheet/) beside you until the numbers stick.

## If this is your own EKG

Reading a strip is a useful skill. If you are looking at a tracing from your own smartwatch or hospital visit, though, the interpretation belongs to your clinician, who can see the whole 12-lead EKG, your symptoms and your history. Our guide on [how to read your ECG report](https://www.ecgmeans.com/how-to-read-ecg-report/) explains the wording that appears on printed reports.

**Get urgent help** if you have chest pain or pressure, fainting, severe breathlessness, or a racing heartbeat that will not settle. Call 911 (US), 999 (UK) or 112 (EU). Do not wait to interpret a strip yourself.
@@FAQ@@
Q: How do you read an ECG strip for beginners?
A: Use the same order every time: calibration, rate, rhythm, P waves, PR interval, QRS width, QT/QTc, then ST segments and T waves. Doing it in order stops you missing a second finding.
Q: How many seconds is one small box on an EKG?
A: At the standard paper speed of 25 mm/s, one small box is 0.04 seconds (40 ms) and one large box is 0.20 seconds (200 ms).
Q: How do you calculate heart rate on an irregular EKG strip?
A: Count the QRS complexes in a 6-second strip (30 large boxes) and multiply by 10. The 300 and 1500 methods only work when the rhythm is regular.
Q: Which lead is used for a rhythm strip?
A: Usually lead II, because the P waves are normally clearest and most upright there. Some monitors also show V1 or MCL1.
Q: Can you read a heart attack from a single rhythm strip?
A: No. A single lead can show suspicious ST changes, but diagnosing or excluding a heart attack needs a full 12-lead EKG, symptoms and often blood tests.
Q: What is a normal PR interval in small boxes?
A: Three to five small boxes, which is 120 to 200 ms at standard paper speed.
Q: How long does it take to learn to read EKGs?
A: Most students can learn the basic method in a few days. Recognizing patterns confidently takes weeks to months of regular practice on real strips.
