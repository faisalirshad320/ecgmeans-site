@@LEDE@@
Atrial tachycardia is a fast heart rhythm — usually 150–250 beats per minute — driven by a single focus in the atria rather than the normal sinus node. On the ECG each QRS is preceded by an abnormally shaped P wave. It is a form of supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) and, while rarely immediately dangerous, it should be assessed because a persistently fast rate can tire the heart.
@@BODY@@
## What "Atrial tachycardia" means

In atrial tachycardia an irritable cluster of atrial cells fires faster than the sinus node and takes over the rhythm. Because the impulse starts away from the sinus node, the P wave looks different from a normal sinus P wave — it may be a different shape or point the wrong way. The ventricles follow normally, so the QRS stays narrow.

It can come in short bursts or run continuously (sometimes "incessant" atrial tachycardia). When several atrial foci fire at once the rhythm becomes multifocal atrial tachycardia instead, which has several P wave shapes.

## What it looks like on the ECG

A regular narrow-complex tachycardia at 150–250 per minute with an abnormal P wave before each QRS. At very fast rates the P wave may hide in the previous T wave. Sometimes not every P wave conducts (atrial tachycardia with block), classically from digoxin toxicity.

## Common causes

- A normal variant, or triggered by caffeine, alcohol or stress
- Underlying heart disease, previous heart surgery (especially in children)
- Digoxin toxicity (atrial tachycardia with block)
- Lung disease and low oxygen
- Overactive thyroid

## Should you worry?

Short runs are often harmless, but a sustained fast rate can cause palpitations, breathlessness, chest discomfort or dizziness, and over months can weaken the heart muscle (a tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy). It should be assessed, and a very fast rate with chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting needs emergency care.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

## What usually happens next

1. A 12-lead ECG and often a Holter monitor to capture it
2. Blood tests (including thyroid) and an echocardiogram
3. Rate or rhythm control with medication
4. Catheter ablation, which can cure a single-focus atrial tachycardia

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Is this atrial tachycardia, SVT or sinus tachycardia?
- Could a medicine such as digoxin be responsible?
- Would ablation be an option for me?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Is atrial tachycardia dangerous?
A: It is rarely immediately life-threatening, but a sustained fast rate can cause symptoms and, over time, weaken the heart, so it needs assessment.
Q: How is it different from sinus tachycardia?
A: Sinus tachycardia has normal P waves and speeds up and slows down gradually; atrial tachycardia has abnormal P waves and tends to start and stop more abruptly.
Q: Can atrial tachycardia be cured?
A: Often. Catheter ablation of a single atrial focus has a high success rate.
