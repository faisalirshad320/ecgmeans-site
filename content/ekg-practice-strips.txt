## How to approach each practice strip

Resist the urge to name the rhythm at a glance. Work through the same five questions on every EKG strip, and the answer usually becomes obvious:

1. **Rate** — count the QRS complexes on a 6-second strip and multiply by 10, or use 300 ÷ the number of large boxes between two R waves. The [ECG heart rate calculator](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-heart-rate-calculator/) does the arithmetic.
2. **Regularity** — are the R-R gaps equal? Regularly irregular (a repeating pattern) and irregularly irregular (no pattern) point to very different rhythms.
3. **P waves** — is there one upright P wave before every QRS? Are there extra P waves, missing P waves, or sawtooth flutter waves instead?
4. **PR interval** — normal is 120–200 ms (3 to 5 small boxes). Long and fixed is first-degree block; lengthening until a beat drops is Wenckebach.
5. **QRS width** — narrow (under 120 ms) means a supraventricular origin; wide means ventricular origin or a bundle branch block.

## Common mistakes on EKG practice tests

| Mistake | How to avoid it |
| --- | --- |
| Calling every fast narrow rhythm "SVT" | Look for P waves first: visible normal P waves at a rate of 100–150 is usually sinus tachycardia |
| Missing atrial flutter | A regular rate of almost exactly 150 should make you hunt for sawtooth waves |
| Confusing Mobitz I and Mobitz II | Measure every PR interval: lengthening before the dropped beat means Mobitz I |
| Mistaking artifact for VT or VF | Check whether normal QRS complexes march through the "chaos" and whether the patient is awake |
| Calling asystole without checking | Confirm in a second lead and check the gain and leads — fine VF can look flat |

## Learn the rhythms, then practise

If a rhythm keeps catching you out, study it side by side with its look-alikes in the [ECG rhythm strip library](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-rhythm-strips/), keep the [ECG interpretation cheat sheet](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-interpretation-cheat-sheet/) open while you practise, or read the step-by-step guide to [how to read an EKG strip](https://www.ecgmeans.com/how-to-read-an-ekg-strip/). @@FAQ@@
Q: Is this EKG practice quiz free?
A: Yes. It runs in your browser, needs no sign-up, and you can repeat it as many times as you like; the order and answer options change every time.
Q: How many EKG rhythms do I need to know for nursing or ACLS?
A: Most courses expect around 15 to 20 core rhythms: sinus rhythms, atrial fibrillation and flutter, SVT, junctional rhythms, PVCs, ventricular tachycardia and fibrillation, asystole, PEA and the heart blocks. All of them are in this quiz.
Q: What is the best way to practise ECG strips?
A: Use the same systematic method on every strip — rate, rhythm, P waves, PR, QRS — and practise little and often with immediate feedback, focusing on rhythms that look alike.
Q: Are these real patient ECGs?
A: No. They are accurate schematic drawings made from textbook criteria, which makes the key features clearer than on real, noisy tracings. Practise on real strips as well once the patterns are familiar.
Q: Can I print EKG practice strips?
A: You can print this page, but for paper practice the rhythm strip library and the cheat sheet are designed to be easier to print.
