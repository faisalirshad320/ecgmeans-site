@@LEDE@@
Atrial flutter is a fast, usually regular rhythm in which the upper chambers beat in a rapid, organised circle — around 300 times a minute — while the AV node lets only a fraction through to the ventricles. On the ECG it produces a distinctive "sawtooth" baseline. It is a close relative of atrial fibrillation and carries a similar stroke risk.
@@BODY@@
## What "Atrial flutter" means

The **atrial flutter ECG** is one of the classic patterns in cardiology, and also one of the most often missed — especially when the heart rate sits at a steady 150. ECG and EKG are the same test; both are used here.

In typical atrial flutter a single electrical wave circles a loop in the right atrium at about 300 beats per minute. The AV node blocks most of these, commonly letting through one in two (2:1), giving a ventricular rate of about 150, or one in three or four. Because the conduction ratio is often fixed, the pulse is usually regular — unlike atrial fibrillation, which is irregular.

Flutter and fibrillation often occur in the same person and share the same risk factors and the same danger of clot formation in the atria, so both are assessed for stroke prevention.

## What it looks like on the ECG

A "sawtooth" or picket-fence baseline (flutter waves) seen best in the inferior leads (II, III, aVF), with regular QRS complexes at a rate that is a simple fraction of 300 — often 150 (2:1), 100 (3:1) or 75 (4:1).

## Atrial flutter EKG: the key features

| Feature | In typical atrial flutter |
| --- | --- |
| **Atrial waves** | Flutter (F) waves instead of P waves — regular, identical, with no flat baseline between them |
| **Atrial rate** | About 300 per minute (range roughly 250–350; can be slower on some medicines) |
| **Shape in II, III, aVF** | Sawtooth: a slow downslope then a sharp upstroke, repeating like the teeth of a saw |
| **Shape in V1** | Often small upright waves that can look like normal P waves |
| **QRS** | Usually narrow, unless there is a bundle branch block or aberrancy |
| **Ventricular rhythm** | Regular with fixed block; irregular with variable block |
| **Ventricular rate** | A fraction of the atrial rate — commonly 150, 100 or 75 |

**Typical vs atypical flutter.** Typical flutter uses a circuit around the tricuspid valve in the right atrium and gives the classic inferior-lead sawtooth. Atypical flutter uses other circuits — often after heart surgery or a previous ablation — and the waves may look different. Atypical flutter is less straightforward to ablate.

## Atrial flutter conduction ratios

The AV node acts like a gate that only lets some flutter waves through. The ratio describes how many flutter waves occur for each QRS.

| Conduction ratio | Ventricular rate (if atrial rate ≈ 300) | Rhythm | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1:1** | About 250–300 | Regular, very fast | Rare and dangerous; can occur with some medicines that slow the atria, or with an accessory pathway |
| **2:1** | About 150 | Regular | Most common in untreated flutter; flutter waves are easily hidden in the QRS and T waves |
| **3:1** | About 100 | Regular | Less common |
| **4:1** | About 75 | Regular | Often with rate-slowing medicines or AV node disease; sawtooth easy to see |
| **Variable (e.g. 2:1 to 4:1)** | Varies | Irregular | R-R gaps are multiples of each other — a clue that separates it from AFib |

### The 150 rule: spotting 2:1 flutter

At 2:1, every second flutter wave falls inside the QRS or the T wave, so the tracing can look like a plain regular [sinus tachycardia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/sinus-tachycardia/) or [SVT](https://www.ecgmeans.com/supraventricular-tachycardia/). Clinicians use a simple rule: **a regular narrow-complex rhythm at about 150 beats per minute is atrial flutter until proven otherwise.** Clues include:

1. A rate that stays fixed at about 150 and barely changes with rest or activity.
2. A "P wave" that looks odd or appears twice per beat — one before the QRS and one hidden in the T wave.
3. Sawtooth waves visible in lead II, III or aVF when you look closely (turning the strip upside down can help).
4. A brief slowing of the AV node — with a vagal manoeuvre or a medicine given under monitoring — temporarily increases the block and unmasks the sawtooth.

## Atrial flutter vs AFib on the ECG

| Feature | Atrial flutter | Atrial fibrillation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Atrial activity** | Organised sawtooth F waves, about 300/min | Chaotic fibrillatory waves, about 350–600/min |
| **Baseline** | Regular repeating waves | Irregular wavy or nearly flat baseline |
| **Ventricular rhythm** | Usually regular | Irregularly irregular |
| **Typical rate untreated** | Often fixed at 150 | Varies beat to beat, often 100–160 |
| **Ablation success** | Very high for typical flutter | Good, but lower and recurrences more common |
| **Stroke risk** | Raised | Raised |

Many people have both rhythms at different times. For the full comparison, see [AFib vs atrial flutter](https://www.ecgmeans.com/afib-vs-aflutter/) and [atrial fibrillation on an ECG](https://www.ecgmeans.com/atrial-fibrillation/).

## Common causes

- High blood pressure and age
- Heart valve disease, heart failure, previous heart surgery
- Chronic lung disease
- Overactive thyroid
- Alcohol and excess caffeine

Others include obstructive sleep apnoea, obesity, pulmonary embolism, recent surgery or acute illness, and previous treatment for atrial fibrillation (some antiarrhythmic medicines can convert AFib to flutter).

## Atrial flutter symptoms

Because the rate is often a steady 150, people commonly notice:

- A fast, regular pounding or racing heartbeat
- Breathlessness, especially on exertion
- Tiredness and reduced exercise tolerance
- Light-headedness
- Chest discomfort

Some people, particularly with 4:1 block or on rate-slowing medicines, feel very little.

## Should you worry?

Atrial flutter is rarely an immediate emergency, but a sustained rate of 150 can cause palpitations, breathlessness and tiredness, and over time can weaken the heart. Like atrial fibrillation it raises stroke risk, so anticoagulation is usually considered. A very fast rate with chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting needs emergency care.

**Get urgent help** if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

Signs of a stroke — face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty — also need an emergency call (911, 999 or 112).

## How atrial flutter is treated

Treatment has three goals, usually pursued together:

1. **Protect against stroke.** Your doctor will use a risk score, much as in atrial fibrillation, to decide whether a blood thinner is recommended.
2. **Control the heart rate.** Rate-slowing medicines widen the AV node block — for example from 2:1 to 4:1 — so the ventricles beat more slowly. Flutter can be harder to rate-control than AFib, because the steady atrial input tends to keep the ventricular rate locked at one fraction of 300.
3. **Restore normal rhythm.** Options include electrical cardioversion, medicines, and catheter ablation. In typical flutter, ablation creates a line of scar across the narrow strip of tissue between the tricuspid valve and the inferior vena cava (the "cavotricuspid isthmus"), breaking the circuit. It has a high success rate, though some people go on to develop atrial fibrillation later.

A very fast flutter needs special care. If the ventricular rate approaches 250–300 (1:1 conduction), or if flutter occurs with [Wolff-Parkinson-White](https://www.ecgmeans.com/wolff-parkinson-white/) pre-excitation, the QRS may widen and the rhythm can look like [ventricular tachycardia](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ventricular-tachycardia/). This is an emergency. A wide QRS in flutter can also simply be a bundle branch block or [aberrant conduction](https://www.ecgmeans.com/aberrant-conduction/).

To work out the heart rate from a strip yourself, see the [ECG heart rate calculator](https://www.ecgmeans.com/ecg-heart-rate-calculator/). Many people with flutter also have an enlarged left or right atrium on echocardiogram; see [left atrial enlargement](https://www.ecgmeans.com/left-atrial-enlargement/).

## What usually happens next

1. Confirmation on a 12-lead ECG
2. Blood tests (including thyroid) and an echocardiogram
3. A stroke-risk assessment and discussion about anticoagulation
4. Rate control, and often catheter ablation, which is very effective for typical flutter

Some people are restored to normal rhythm with an electrical cardioversion, after steps to reduce the risk of clots. Your doctor will explain which approach suits you; do not start or stop medicines yourself.

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Do I need a blood thinner to reduce stroke risk?
- Is my rate controlled, and would ablation help?
- Is there an underlying cause to treat?
- Is my flutter typical or atypical?
- Have I also had atrial fibrillation?

## A worked example

> **Report line:** *"Sinus tachycardia, rate 150. Nonspecific T wave abnormality. Abnormal ECG."*
> **Patient:** 72-year-old with chronic lung disease, three days of breathlessness and a racing heart.

The rate was fixed at exactly 150 and did not change when he rested. On close inspection, leads II, III and aVF showed a regular sawtooth baseline at 300 per minute, with a second flutter wave hidden in each T wave. The machine had misread 2:1 atrial flutter as sinus tachycardia. A rate-slowing medicine increased the block to 4:1, making the sawtooth obvious. A stroke-risk assessment led to a blood thinner, and he was referred for ablation of typical flutter.
@@FAQ@@
Q: What is the difference between atrial flutter and atrial fibrillation?
A: Flutter is organised and usually regular with a sawtooth pattern; fibrillation is chaotic and irregular with no P waves. Both raise stroke risk.
Q: Is atrial flutter dangerous?
A: It is seldom immediately life-threatening, but it can cause symptoms, weaken the heart over time and raise stroke risk, so it needs treatment.
Q: Can atrial flutter be cured?
A: Catheter ablation cures typical atrial flutter in most people, which is why it is often recommended early.
Q: Is atrial flutter regular or irregular?
A: Usually regular, because the AV node often lets through a fixed fraction of flutter waves. With variable block it becomes irregular, but in a patterned way unlike atrial fibrillation.
Q: Why is the heart rate often 150 in atrial flutter?
A: The atria are firing at about 300 per minute and the AV node commonly lets every second wave through, giving a ventricular rate of about 150.
Q: Which leads show atrial flutter best on an ECG?
A: The inferior leads II, III and aVF usually show the sawtooth most clearly. Lead V1 often shows small, regular, P-like waves.
Q: Can a smartwatch detect atrial flutter?
A: Not reliably. Watches look mainly for irregular rhythms, and flutter is often regular, so a 12-lead ECG is needed.
