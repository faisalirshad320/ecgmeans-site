## What "biatrial enlargement" means

Your heart has two upper chambers, called the atria: one on the right and one on the left. When your ECG report says **biatrial enlargement**, it means the tracing shows signs that both atria are enlarged or under strain at the same time.

Doctors describe this as a combination of two separate patterns:

- **Right atrial enlargement**, sometimes called **P pulmonale**
- **Left atrial enlargement**, sometimes called **P mitrale**

When features of both appear together, the finding is called biatrial enlargement. An ECG records the heart's electrical activity rather than taking a direct picture of the heart. So this finding is a suggestion based on the shape of the waves, not a direct measurement of chamber size.

## What it looks like on the ECG

The clue lies in the **P wave**. This is the small bump on the ECG that reflects the electrical activity of the atria. With biatrial enlargement, the P wave shows both the right-sided and left-sided patterns at once.

- **Tall and wide P waves:** the P wave is both tall (**more than 2.5 mm**) and wide (**more than 110–120 ms**).
- **In lead II:** the finding is suggested by a P wave **taller than 2.5 mm** and **wider than about 120 ms**.
- **In lead V1:** the P wave is prominently biphasic, meaning it has an up part and a down part.
    - The first, upward part is tall (**more than 1.5 mm**). This reflects the right atrium.
    - The final, downward part is deep and wide. This reflects the left atrium.
- **In the limb leads:** the P wave can look both peaked and notched at the same time.

These details are mainly for the clinician reading your ECG. You don't need to measure anything yourself. The key point is that the P wave carries signs pointing to both atria.

## Common causes

Biatrial enlargement usually points to a significant underlying condition that is putting strain on both sides of the heart. Common causes include:

- Advanced valvular heart disease affecting more than one valve
- Congenital heart disease (heart conditions present from birth)
- Pulmonary hypertension (high pressure in the lungs' blood vessels) together with left heart disease
- Cardiomyopathy (disease of the heart muscle)
- Long-standing heart failure

The ECG alone can't tell which of these, if any, is responsible. That is why further testing matters.

## Should you worry?

Needs clinician review

Biatrial enlargement is not a disease in itself. It is a clue that both sides of the heart may be under strain, usually from a significant underlying condition. It deserves a proper assessment, but it is not an emergency on its own. The sensible next step is to discuss the result with your doctor so the cause can be looked into.

## What usually happens next

The key test is an **echocardiogram**, an ultrasound scan of the heart. It is painless and gives your doctor much more detail than an ECG can, because it:

- Measures the size of the atria directly, confirming whether they really are enlarged
- Looks for valve disease
- Checks for signs of pulmonary hypertension
- Assesses the heart muscle for cardiomyopathy

Treatment then depends on what the echocardiogram and any other assessments find. The finding itself isn't treated. Care is aimed at the underlying cause.

In the meantime, bring your ECG report to your appointment, along with a list of any symptoms you've noticed and any medicines you take. If you develop new or worsening symptoms before you are seen, contact your doctor or healthcare service for advice.
@@FAQ@@
Q: What does biatrial enlargement on my ECG mean?
A: It means your ECG shows signs that both the right and left atria, the heart's two upper chambers, are enlarged or under strain at the same time. It combines the features of right atrial enlargement (P pulmonale) and left atrial enlargement (P mitrale). It is a clue on the tracing, not a diagnosis in itself.
Q: Is biatrial enlargement dangerous?
A: It is not a disease in itself and not an emergency on its own. It does suggest that both sides of the heart are under strain, usually from a significant underlying condition. That is why it should be reviewed by a clinician.
Q: What causes biatrial enlargement?
A: Common causes include advanced valve disease affecting more than one valve, congenital heart disease, and pulmonary hypertension together with left heart disease. Cardiomyopathy and long-standing heart failure are other causes. An ECG alone can't tell which cause is responsible.
Q: What test will I need next?
A: An echocardiogram is the key test. This ultrasound scan of the heart measures the size of the atria directly. It also looks for valve disease, pulmonary hypertension and cardiomyopathy. Your doctor will then plan treatment based on the cause found.
Q: Can biatrial enlargement be treated or prevented?
A: The ECG finding itself isn't treated directly. Management focuses on the underlying cause, such as a valve problem or a heart muscle condition. Finding and treating that cause is the main goal, which is why follow-up testing matters.
