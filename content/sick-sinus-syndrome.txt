@@BODY@@
## What "Sick sinus syndrome" means

Your heartbeat normally starts in a small area of the heart called the **sinus node**. It acts as the heart's natural pacemaker and sets a steady rate. **Sick sinus syndrome**, also called **sinus node dysfunction**, means this pacemaker no longer reliably sets a normal rate.

It is not a single problem. It is a name for a group of related rhythm issues:

- **Inappropriate sinus bradycardia**: the heart rate stays slower than it should.
- **Sinus pauses or sinus arrest**: the sinus node briefly stops firing, leaving a gap between beats.
- **Sinoatrial (SA) exit block**: the sinus node fires, but the signal does not get out to the rest of the heart, so a beat is dropped.
- **The "tachy-brady" pattern**: fast atrial rhythms alternate with slow ones.

## What it looks like on the ECG

What appears on the ECG depends on which subtype is present:

- A **persistently slow sinus rate**.
- **Long pauses between P waves**, which suggests sinus arrest. (The P wave is the small bump on the ECG that marks the sinus node's signal spreading through the upper chambers.)
- **Dropped P-QRS cycles**, where a whole beat is missing. This suggests SA exit block.
- **Alternating runs of atrial fibrillation or flutter with slow rates and pauses**. There is often a long pause when the fast rhythm stops.

These changes can come and go, so a short routine ECG may miss them. Doctors often need prolonged monitoring to capture the problem, such as a **24–48 hour Holter** monitor or a longer recording. Pauses that cause symptoms are typically **longer than 3 seconds (>3 seconds)**.

## Common causes

- **Age-related fibrosis (scarring) of the sinus node**. This is the commonest cause.
- **Ischaemic heart disease**, meaning reduced blood supply to the heart muscle.
- **Medicines that slow the heart**, such as beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, digoxin and some antiarrhythmics.
- **Hypothyroidism** (an underactive thyroid) or an **electrolyte disturbance**.
- **Prior heart surgery**.

## Should you worry?

Needs clinician review

Sick sinus syndrome matters mainly when the slow rates or pauses cause symptoms such as fatigue, dizziness, breathlessness or fainting (syncope). The tachy-brady form also carries a stroke risk because of its atrial fibrillation component, so it is worth discussing with your doctor even if you feel well. It is not an emergency unless there is collapse. **Get urgent help** if you collapse or faint.

## What usually happens next

The diagnosis rests on matching your symptoms to what your heart rhythm is doing at the same time. This is why monitoring over a longer period is often arranged. It can help to note when you feel dizzy, tired, breathless or faint, so these episodes can be compared with the recording.

Your medicines are usually reviewed first. Some common drugs can slow the heart, and adjusting them may help.

For sick sinus syndrome that is causing symptoms, a **permanent pacemaker** is the definitive treatment. It stops the heart from running too slowly or pausing for too long. If atrial fibrillation is part of the picture, your doctor will also consider **anticoagulation** (blood-thinning treatment) to reduce stroke risk.
@@FAQ@@
Q: What does sick sinus syndrome mean on my ECG?
A: It means the sinus node, your heart's natural pacemaker, is no longer reliably setting a normal heart rate. It can show as a persistently slow rate, pauses between beats, dropped beats, or fast atrial rhythms alternating with slow ones.
Q: Is sick sinus syndrome dangerous?
A: It is not an emergency unless there is collapse. It matters when the slow rates or pauses cause symptoms such as fatigue, dizziness, breathlessness or fainting. The tachy-brady form also carries a stroke risk from its atrial fibrillation component, so it needs a clinician's review.
Q: What causes sick sinus syndrome?
A: The commonest cause is age-related scarring (fibrosis) of the sinus node. Other causes include ischaemic heart disease, medicines that slow the heart such as beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers, digoxin and some antiarrhythmics, hypothyroidism, electrolyte disturbance and prior heart surgery.
Q: Why do I need a Holter monitor if my ECG already showed something?
A: The rhythm changes in sick sinus syndrome can come and go, so a short ECG may not capture them fully. Prolonged monitoring, such as a 24–48 hour Holter or longer, helps match your symptoms to what your heart rhythm is doing at the time. Pauses that cause symptoms are typically longer than 3 seconds.
Q: What happens next, and will I need a pacemaker?
A: Your doctor will usually review any medicines that might be slowing your heart first. If sick sinus syndrome is causing symptoms, a permanent pacemaker is the definitive treatment. If atrial fibrillation is also present, anticoagulation (blood-thinning treatment) will be considered to reduce stroke risk.
