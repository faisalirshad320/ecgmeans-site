@@LEDE@@
Bifascicular block means two of the three main conduction branches that carry the electrical signal to the ventricles are blocked. The usual combination is a right bundle branch block plus one of the two left-sided fascicles. On its own it often causes no symptoms, but it reflects disease in the heart's wiring and sometimes needs monitoring.
@@BODY@@
## What "bifascicular block" means

The signal to the ventricles travels down three branches: the right bundle, and the left bundle's two fascicles (anterior and posterior). Bifascicular block means two of these are not conducting. The common patterns are right bundle branch block (RBBB) with left anterior fascicular block, or RBBB with left posterior fascicular block.

## What it looks like on the ECG

The features of the two individual blocks combine. For example, RBBB with left anterior fascicular block shows a wide QRS with an RBBB shape plus marked left axis deviation. RBBB with left posterior fascicular block shows an RBBB shape plus right axis deviation.

## Why it matters

Only one working branch is left carrying the signal to the ventricles. In most people this is stable for years. The concern is that, occasionally, the remaining branch also fails, which would cause complete (third-degree) heart block and a very slow pulse. Whether this needs action depends on symptoms and the clinical picture.

**Get prompt care** if bifascicular block comes with fainting, near-fainting, very slow pulse, chest pain or severe breathlessness.

## What causes it
Bifascicular block is most often the result of slow, age-related scarring (fibrosis) of the conduction system. Other causes include coronary artery disease and previous heart attacks, long-standing high blood pressure, and diseases of the heart muscle or valves. In many older people it is found incidentally and stays stable for years, which is why the focus is on symptoms and on comparison with any earlier ECGs.

## What usually happens next

1. Comparison with old ECGs to see whether the block is new
2. Assessment for symptoms such as blackouts
3. Monitoring and sometimes further electrical testing of the conduction system
4. A pacemaker only if there are symptoms or evidence of more advanced block

### Questions to ask your doctor

- Is my bifascicular block new or long-standing?
- Have I had any blackouts or near-faints?
- Do I need monitoring or a pacemaker?
@@FAQ@@
Q: Is bifascicular block dangerous?
A: Often it is stable and symptom-free, but because only one conduction branch remains, there is a small risk it can progress to complete heart block, so it is monitored.
Q: What causes bifascicular block?
A: Usually age-related fibrosis of the conduction system, coronary artery disease, high blood pressure, or previous heart damage.
Q: Do I need a pacemaker for bifascicular block?
A: Not usually on its own. A pacemaker is considered if there are blackouts, a very slow pulse, or evidence of more advanced block.
