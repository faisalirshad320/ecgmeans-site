Ventricular Tachycardia on an ECG: What It Means

Seek prompt care

Ventricular tachycardia (VT) is a fast heart rhythm that starts in the ventricles, the heart's lower pumping chambers, instead of following the normal electrical pathway. Because the ventricles beat fast and inefficiently, VT can lower blood pressure and can turn into a more dangerous rhythm that causes cardiac arrest. It is a potentially life-threatening rhythm and doctors treat it as an emergency. With prompt assessment and treatment, it can be stabilised and its underlying cause addressed.

What "ventricular tachycardia" means

Ventricular tachycardia, often shortened to VT, is a fast heart rhythm that begins in the ventricles. These are the heart's two lower chambers, which pump blood out to the lungs and the rest of the body. Normally, each heartbeat starts higher up in the heart and travels down through a dedicated electrical wiring system called the conduction system. In VT, the rhythm starts below that normal system, in the ventricle muscle itself.

When the ventricles beat this fast and this inefficiently, they cannot fill and pump properly. That can cause blood pressure to drop. VT can also deteriorate into ventricular fibrillation, a chaotic rhythm in which the heart stops pumping effectively. This leads to cardiac arrest. For these reasons, VT is considered a potentially life-threatening rhythm.

What it looks like on the ECG

On an ECG, VT appears as a fast, regular rhythm with wide QRS complexes. The QRS complex is the spike on the tracing that shows the ventricles being activated. In VT the QRS is 120 ms or longer (QRS ≥120 ms).

Key features include:

  • Rate: usually faster than 120 beats per minute, typically in the range of 120–250 bpm.
  • QRS width: ≥120 ms, so the beats look broad rather than narrow and sharp.
  • Shape of the beats: in monomorphic VT, every beat looks identical. In polymorphic VT, the shape keeps changing. This includes a type called torsades de pointes.
  • Duration: VT is called sustained when it lasts more than 30 seconds, or when it needs treatment sooner than that.

A fast, wide-QRS rhythm can occasionally come from the upper chambers instead (called SVT with aberrancy). Doctors look for certain clues that strongly point to VT. These include AV dissociation, where the upper and lower chambers beat independently, and capture beats or fusion beats, which are occasional normal-looking or blended beats. When any of these are present, they strongly favour VT.

Common causes

  • A previous heart attack that left scar tissue in the heart muscle (the commonest cause)
  • Cardiomyopathy (disease of the heart muscle) or heart failure
  • Acute ischaemia, meaning reduced blood supply to the heart, or an ongoing heart attack
  • A long QT interval or an inherited electrical condition (channelopathy), which can lead to torsades de pointes
  • Severe electrolyte disturbance, especially low potassium or low magnesium

Should you worry?

Seek prompt care

VT is treated as a medical emergency. Doctors assume that any fast, wide-QRS rhythm is VT until proven otherwise, because it can cause collapse and cardiac arrest. If this finding appears on your report, it needs to be assessed by a medical team without delay. It should not wait for a routine appointment.

Get urgent help: if you have been told your ECG shows ventricular tachycardia and you are not already under medical care, or you feel unwell, faint or collapse, call your local emergency number immediately.

What usually happens next

VT is managed as an emergency, and the first step depends on how the person is doing:

  • If the person is unstable, doctors deliver a controlled electrical shock to restore a normal rhythm. This is called cardioversion.
  • If there is no pulse, the person is treated for cardiac arrest with defibrillation and resuscitation.

Once the rhythm is under control, attention turns to finding and treating the underlying cause. That might be scarring from a past heart attack, a heart muscle problem, an ongoing heart attack, a long QT interval, or low potassium or magnesium.

Many people who have had VT go on to receive an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD). This small device sits under the skin, monitors the heart rhythm, and can deliver a shock if a dangerous rhythm returns. Some people may also be offered ablation, a procedure that targets the area of the heart producing the abnormal rhythm.

Your heart team will explain which of these steps apply to you, based on your ECG, your heart's structure and what caused the VT.

Frequently asked questions

What does ventricular tachycardia mean on my ECG?

It means the ECG shows a fast rhythm starting in the ventricles, the heart's lower chambers, rather than through the normal electrical pathway. On the tracing it appears as a fast, regular rhythm with wide QRS complexes of 120 ms or more, usually at a rate above 120 beats per minute.

Is ventricular tachycardia dangerous?

Yes, VT is considered potentially life-threatening. Because the ventricles beat fast and inefficiently, blood pressure can drop, and VT can deteriorate into ventricular fibrillation and cardiac arrest. That is why it is always treated as an emergency.

What causes ventricular tachycardia?

The commonest cause is scar tissue from a previous heart attack. Other causes include cardiomyopathy or heart failure, an ongoing heart attack or reduced blood supply to the heart, a long QT interval or inherited electrical condition, and severe low potassium or magnesium.

What happens after VT is found?

It needs emergency assessment. An unstable patient is given an electrical shock (cardioversion) to restore a normal rhythm, and a patient without a pulse is defibrillated and resuscitated. After stabilisation, doctors treat the underlying cause, and many people receive an implantable defibrillator (ICD) and sometimes ablation.

Can ventricular tachycardia be prevented from coming back?

Prevention focuses on treating the underlying cause, such as correcting low potassium or magnesium or managing heart muscle disease or blocked arteries. Many people are fitted with an ICD, which can detect and treat a dangerous rhythm if it returns, and some benefit from ablation. Your heart team will advise on the best approach for you.
