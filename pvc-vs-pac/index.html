PAC vs PVC: What's the Difference on an ECG?

Usually harmless

A PAC (premature atrial contraction) is an early beat that starts in the upper chambers and travels the normal route, so its QRS looks normal. A PVC (premature ventricular contraction) starts in the lower chambers and spreads abnormally, giving a wide, odd-shaped QRS. Both are common and usually harmless; PVCs are the ones more often counted and followed up.

Quick answer

PAC vs PVC comes down to where the early beat starts. A PAC (premature atrial contraction) starts in the upper chambers, so on the ECG it has an early, odd-looking P wave and a narrow QRS. A PVC (premature ventricular contraction) starts in the lower chambers, so it has no P wave and a wide, oddly shaped QRS. Both are common and usually harmless.

What "PVC vs PAC" means

When people search PAC vs PVC, they usually have a report, a Holter result or a smartwatch strip that mentions one of these extra beats and want to know which one they have and whether it matters. ECG and EKG mean the same test; both spellings are used here.

Both are ectopic beats — impulses from a spot other than the sinus node. The difference is location. PACs come from atrial tissue, use the AV node and bundle branches, and produce a narrow QRS preceded by an abnormal P wave. PVCs bypass the wiring, produce a wide QRS with no preceding P wave, and are usually followed by a longer (compensatory) pause.

Both can feel identical: a flutter, a thump or a skipped beat. The symptom cannot tell them apart; only the ECG can.

You may also see these beats called premature atrial complexes, atrial ectopics or supraventricular ectopics (for PACs), and premature ventricular complexes or ventricular ectopics (for PVCs). The names differ; the beats are the same. For the full story on each beat individually, see PVCs on an ECG and sinus rhythm with PACs.

PAC vs PVC on ECG: side-by-side comparison

This is the table most people are looking for. Read across each row to see how a PAC and a PVC differ on the tracing.

Where it starts Atria (upper chambers) Ventricles (lower chambers)
Timing Early Early
P wave before the beat Present, but early and a different shape from normal; may hide in the previous T wave Absent — no related P wave in front of the QRS
QRS width Narrow (under 120 ms), same shape as normal beats Wide (usually 120 ms or more), bizarre shape

A useful way to remember it: narrow with a P wave = atrial; wide without a P wave = ventricular.

What it looks like on the ECG

PAC: early beat, narrow QRS, odd-looking P wave, short pause. PVC: early beat, wide and tall or deep QRS, no P wave, T wave pointing the opposite way, longer pause.

How to tell PAC vs PVC on an ECG strip, step by step

  1. Find the early beat. Look for the QRS that arrives sooner than the steady rhythm predicts.
  2. Measure the QRS width. Narrow (under three small squares, or 120 ms) points to a PAC. Wide points to a PVC.
  3. Look just before it for a P wave. Check the end of the previous T wave carefully — an early P wave often hides there and makes the T wave look peaked or notched. A P wave means PAC.
  4. Check the pause. Measure from the beat before the extra beat to the beat after it. If that span equals two normal beat-to-beat gaps, the pause is fully compensatory, which favours a PVC. A shorter span favours a PAC.
  5. Look at the T wave of the early beat. A T wave that points the opposite way to a wide QRS is typical of a PVC.

The tricky cases

  • A PAC with aberrant conduction. A very early PAC can arrive while one bundle branch is still recovering, so its QRS comes out wide — usually with a right bundle branch block shape. It can look like a PVC. The clue is the early P wave in front of it. See aberrant conduction and the Ashman phenomenon.
  • A blocked PAC. A PAC so early that it cannot get through the AV node produces a P wave with no QRS, followed by a pause. It can be mistaken for a sinus pause or heart block.
  • An interpolated PVC. Sometimes a PVC slips between two normal beats with no pause at all.
  • A fusion beat. When a sinus beat and a PVC arrive at the same moment, the QRS looks part-normal, part-wide. See fusion beat.
  • A junctional beat. An early beat from the AV junction has a narrow QRS like a PAC, but the P wave is absent or inverted and very close to the QRS. See premature junctional contraction.

These are the cases where a single-lead strip can mislead, and a 12-lead ECG or Holter recording settles the question.

Common causes

  • Shared triggers: caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, stress, poor sleep, low potassium or magnesium, thyroid overactivity
  • PACs are linked to atrial stretch (high blood pressure, sleep apnoea, enlarged left atrium)
  • PVCs are linked to scar, cardiomyopathy and coronary disease when frequent

Other everyday contributors include dehydration, a recent illness or fever, decongestants and stimulant medicines, and heavy exercise followed by sudden rest. In many people no single cause is ever found, and that is normal.

PAC vs PVC symptoms: can you feel the difference?

Usually not. Many people with either type of extra beat feel nothing at all, and the beats are found by chance on an ECG or a watch. When they are felt, people describe:

  • A skipped or missing beat — this is actually the pause after the extra beat
  • A thump or a "flip-flop" in the chest — often the stronger beat after the pause
  • A brief flutter in the chest or throat
  • A short cough or catch of breath

PVCs are sometimes felt more strongly because the pause after them tends to be longer, so the next beat is more forceful. But this is not reliable enough to tell them apart at home. If your palpitations feel different from usual, last longer, or come with other symptoms, record an ECG and speak to your doctor.

Should you worry?

Usually harmless

Occasional PACs or PVCs need no treatment. Very frequent PACs raise the long-term risk of atrial fibrillation; a high PVC burden (over roughly 10–15% of beats) can weaken the heart and is treatable. Symptoms such as fainting or chest pain with either should be assessed.

Which is worse, a PAC or a PVC?

Neither is dangerous on its own in a structurally normal heart. PVCs attract more attention for two reasons: they come from the ventricles, where serious rhythms such as ventricular tachycardia also arise, and a very high PVC burden can weaken the heart muscle over time. PACs mainly matter because frequent PACs are linked to later atrial fibrillation.

Situation PACs PVCs
Occasional beats, healthy heart No action needed No action needed
Frequent beats, no symptoms Watch for signs of atrial fibrillation Holter to measure burden; echocardiogram if burden is high
Bothersome symptoms Trigger control; medicine if needed Trigger control; medicine or ablation if needed
Known heart disease Usual heart care Closer review; burden and pattern matter more
Fainting or chest pain Urgent assessment Urgent assessment

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

PACs and PVCs in patterns: bigeminy, trigeminy and couplets

Extra beats often fall into rhythms of their own, and the report will name the pattern:

  • Bigeminy: every second beat is early. Atrial bigeminy uses PACs; ventricular bigeminy uses PVCs.
  • Trigeminy: every third beat is early. See trigeminy.
  • Couplet: two extra beats in a row. A ventricular couplet is two PVCs back to back.
  • Runs: three or more in a row. Three or more PACs form a short run of atrial tachycardia; three or more PVCs form nonsustained ventricular tachycardia.
  • Multifocal: extra beats of more than one shape, meaning more than one starting point.

Patterns change how often the beats appear, not what they are. Bigeminy can also make a pulse oximeter or blood pressure machine read a falsely low heart rate, because the early beats are weak.

What usually happens next

  1. Holter monitor to count them if they are frequent or troublesome
  2. Echocardiogram for frequent PVCs
  3. Lifestyle triggers addressed first
  4. Beta-blockers or ablation only for symptoms or a high burden

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Are my extra beats atrial or ventricular?
  • What percentage of my beats are they?
  • Do I need an echocardiogram?

What PVC burden percentage is too high?

A PVC burden above about 10% of all heartbeats is considered high, and once PVCs make up more than a quarter of beats the risk of them weakening the heart muscle rises clearly. Burden is simply the share of your heartbeats that are PVCs, worked out from a Holter monitor worn for 24 to 48 hours. For example, 8,000 PVCs out of roughly 100,000 beats in a day is a burden of 8%.

  • Over 10% (or more than 10,000 PVCs in 24 hours): a high burden. An echocardiogram is usually considered to check how well the heart is pumping.
  • Over 15%: the level at which the 2017 AHA/ACC/HRS guideline says catheter ablation may help if the heart's pumping is reduced and medicines have not worked or were not tolerated.
  • Over 25%: PVC-induced cardiomyopathy (heart muscle weakening caused by the extra beats) becomes more likely.

PVC-induced cardiomyopathy is generally reversible once the PVCs are brought under control, most often by ablation. PACs are not usually graded by percentage in the same way: with very frequent PACs, the main long-term concern is a higher chance of atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter rather than a weak heart muscle.

When should you see a cardiologist for PVCs or PACs?

Ask for a cardiology review if your extra beats are frequent, come in runs, are brought on by exercise, or happen alongside heart disease or fainting; occasional PACs or PVCs in an otherwise healthy heart usually need no specialist care. Reasons for referral include:

  • A Holter result showing a PVC burden over 10%, or runs of three or more PVCs in a row (nonsustained ventricular tachycardia).
  • Extra beats that appear or become more frequent during exercise.
  • Known heart disease, such as a previous heart attack, heart failure, cardiomyopathy or valve disease.
  • Dizziness, near-fainting or fainting with the palpitations.
  • Frequent PACs together with bouts of a fast, irregular heartbeat, which can be a sign of atrial fibrillation.

A typical work-up includes a 12-lead ECG, a 24–48-hour Holter monitor, an echocardiogram, and blood tests for potassium, magnesium and thyroid function. Call emergency services if extra beats come with chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting or sudden confusion.

Can anxiety cause PVCs and PACs, and do they go away?

Yes. Stress and anxiety raise adrenaline, which can set off both PVCs and PACs, and the extra beats often settle when triggers such as stress, caffeine, alcohol, nicotine and poor sleep are reduced. Noticing the beats can itself be unsettling, and that worry adds more adrenaline, so a cycle can build. The number of extra beats also tends to vary with the time of day.

For occasional beats in a healthy heart, reassurance and trigger control are usually enough. If symptoms persist:

  • Beta-blockers are the usual first medicine for both PACs and PVCs. Calcium-channel blockers are sometimes used instead, mainly for PVCs.
  • Catheter ablation is used more often for PVCs, mainly for a high burden, a weakened heart or symptoms that medicines do not control. For PACs it is kept for stubborn symptoms or PACs that trigger atrial fibrillation.

Even when stress seems the obvious trigger, it is worth having an ECG or Holter recording to confirm which type of extra beat you have.

A worked example

Holter summary: "Sinus rhythm, average rate 74. Supraventricular ectopics 1,240 (1.2%). Ventricular ectopics 310 (0.3%), unifocal. No pauses. No sustained arrhythmia." Patient: 38-year-old with evening palpitations, two or three coffees a day, poor sleep with a new baby.

Both kinds of extra beat were present — PACs (listed here as supraventricular ectopics) and PVCs (ventricular ectopics) — which is very common. Both burdens were low, well under 10%, and the PVCs all had one shape (unifocal). The echocardiogram was not needed. The doctor checked potassium, magnesium and thyroid function, which were normal, and suggested cutting back on caffeine and protecting sleep where possible. The palpitations faded over the next few months without medicine.

This is the typical story: two scary-sounding abbreviations, one ordinary explanation.

Frequently asked questions

Which is worse, PAC or PVC?

Neither is dangerous in isolation. Frequent PVCs are more likely to lead to further tests because of their link with heart muscle disease.

Can PACs and PVCs occur together?

Yes, commonly.

Can a smartwatch tell PACs from PVCs?

A single-lead watch ECG can often show the early beat and whether the QRS is wide, but a 12-lead ECG or Holter is more reliable.

How do you tell a PAC from a PVC on an ECG?

Look at the QRS width and for a P wave. A PAC has an early P wave and a narrow QRS; a PVC has no P wave in front of it and a wide, oddly shaped QRS, usually followed by a longer pause.

Is a PAC with a wide QRS a PVC?

Not necessarily. A very early PAC can be conducted with aberrancy and look wide. An early P wave in front of the beat, often hidden in the previous T wave, shows it is still a PAC.

Is a compensatory pause seen with PACs or PVCs?

A full compensatory pause is typical of a PVC. A PAC usually resets the sinus node, so the pause after it is shorter than a full compensatory pause.

Can a PAC or PVC be a sign of a heart attack?

An isolated extra beat is not a sign of a heart attack. Extra beats that come with chest pain, breathlessness or sweating need urgent assessment because of the symptoms, not the beats.
