What Is an EKG (ECG), and How Long Does an EKG Take?

Usually harmless

An EKG — also written ECG — is a quick, painless test that records the electrical signals your heart makes with each beat. Small sticky pads on your chest, arms and legs pick up the signals, and the machine prints them as a set of waves. It takes only a few minutes, uses no needles and no radiation, and shows your heart's rhythm, rate and electrical health at that moment.

Quick answer

An EKG (electrocardiogram, also written ECG) is a quick, painless test that records your heart's electrical activity. A standard resting EKG takes about 5 to 10 minutes from start to finish, but the recording itself lasts only about 10 seconds. It measures your heart rate and rhythm and the timing of electrical signals through the heart, using sticky pads on the chest, arms and legs.

If you have been booked for an EKG, you probably want to know three things: what the test is, how long an EKG takes, and what it can show. The short version: it is one of the simplest tests in medicine, you will usually be in and out of the room within 10 minutes, and it gives your doctor a snapshot of your heart's rhythm and electrical health. The sections below cover each question in plain English.

The short answer

EKG and ECG are the same test. "ECG" comes from the English electrocardiogram; "EKG" from the German elektrokardiogramm. American clinics tend to say EKG, while the UK, Canada, Australia and most of the world say ECG. There is no difference in the test itself. (More on the naming in ECG vs EKG.)

During the test, ten electrodes are placed on the skin — six across the chest and one on each arm and leg. They do not send anything into you; they only listen. The machine combines their signals into twelve "leads", each a different electrical view of the heart, and prints a short strip for each. The whole recording lasts about ten seconds.

What does EKG stand for?

EKG stands for electrocardiogram: electro (electrical), cardio (heart) and gram (a recording). The "K" comes from the German spelling, Elektrokardiogramm, which is why the abbreviation EKG is common in the United States even though the English word starts with "C". A "12-lead EKG" is the standard resting test described on this page.

How long does an EKG take?

A standard resting EKG takes about 5 to 10 minutes in total. The actual recording takes only about 10 seconds; the rest of the time is spent getting ready, placing the stickers and removing them.

Type of EKG How long it takes What it is for
Resting 12-lead EKG About 5–10 minutes in the room; about 10 seconds of recording The standard test at a clinic, hospital or pre-op visit
Rhythm strip A minute or two A longer single-lead recording to look at the rhythm
Exercise (stress) EKG Usually 40–60 minutes in total, including preparation and recovery Shows how the heart copes with exertion
Holter monitor Worn for 24–48 hours, sometimes up to 7 days Catches symptoms or rhythms that come and go
Event monitor or patch Days to weeks For infrequent palpitations or fainting

If the first tracing has interference — for example from movement, shivering or a loose sticker — the technician may simply repeat it, which adds a minute or two. See ECG artifact for why that happens.

What does an EKG measure?

An EKG measures the electrical activity of the heart — not its pumping or the blood flow through it. From that electrical signal it shows:

  • Heart rate — how many beats per minute
  • Heart rhythm — whether beats are regular and start in the right place
  • Timing of the signal through each part of the heart — the PR, QRS and QT intervals
  • Electrical axis — the overall direction the signal travels
  • Signs of strain or damage, such as patterns suggesting a heart attack, thickened heart muscle or enlarged chambers
  • Clues to blood chemistry, such as high or low potassium

What it looks like on the ECG

The printout shows a repeating pattern of waves — a small P wave, a tall QRS spike and a rounded T wave — for each heartbeat, plus a block of measurements (heart rate, PR, QRS, QT intervals and the electrical axis) and the machine's automated interpretation.

For the normal range of each number, see normal ECG values. For what an EKG can and cannot pick up — including heart attacks and blocked arteries — see what can an EKG detect.

What is an EKG used for?

  • Reasons an EKG is done: a routine or pre-operation check
  • Chest pain, pressure or tightness
  • Palpitations or a feeling of skipped or racing beats
  • Breathlessness, dizziness or fainting
  • Monitoring a known heart condition or the effect of a medicine
  • Screening before some sports or jobs

How is an EKG performed?

  1. You undress to the waist behind a screen or curtain and may be given a gown. Women can usually keep a bra on, though an underwire may need to be moved.
  2. You lie on your back on an examination couch, propped slightly if that is more comfortable.
  3. The skin is prepared. The technician may wipe small areas with alcohol, lightly rub them, or shave a little chest hair so the stickers grip well.
  4. Ten sticky electrodes are placed — six across the left side of the chest and one on each arm and leg (often at the wrists and ankles). See 12-lead ECG placement for exactly where each goes.
  5. You lie still and breathe normally for about 10 seconds while the machine records. Talking, moving or tensing your muscles can blur the tracing.
  6. The stickers are peeled off, and you can dress and go about your day straight away. There is no recovery time.

What to wear for an ECG test

  • A two-piece outfit — a top you can easily take off, with trousers or a skirt — is easiest.
  • Avoid one-piece dresses or jumpsuits, as you will need to uncover your chest.
  • Skip full-length tights or pantyhose if possible, because electrodes go on your lower legs or ankles.
  • Do not put lotion, oil or talcum powder on your chest, arms or legs on the day, as they stop the stickers sticking.
  • Jewelry can usually stay on, but a long necklace may need to be removed.

There is no need to fast for a resting EKG. For an exercise EKG, you may be asked to avoid a heavy meal and caffeine beforehand and to wear comfortable shoes. Tell the team about every medicine you take, because some can affect the tracing.

Should you worry?

The test itself is completely safe — there is nothing to worry about in having one. What matters is the result, and even there the automated interpretation is only a first pass that a clinician confirms. If your report carries a phrase you do not understand, look it up on this site and ask the clinician who ordered the test.

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

How long do EKG results take?

The machine prints its measurements and an automated interpretation immediately. How quickly a clinician explains the result depends on the setting:

Setting When you usually hear
Emergency department or urgent care Within minutes — the tracing is read straight away
Hospital ward or pre-operation clinic Same day, often during the same visit
Routine test ordered by your doctor Often a few days
Holter or other ambulatory monitor Usually one to a few weeks, because hours of data must be analyzed

If you have not heard anything after a couple of weeks, contact the clinic that ordered the test. Ask for a copy of the printout — our guide to how to read your ECG report explains each line.

Where can you get an EKG?

EKGs are done in doctors' offices, hospitals, urgent care centers, cardiology clinics and some pharmacies or workplace health services. Many smartwatches can record a single-lead tracing, which helps with rhythm questions but is not a full 12-lead EKG. See where to get an EKG for options and typical costs.

What usually happens next

  1. The clinician reads the tracing and compares it with any earlier EKG
  2. A normal result is reassuring for rhythm and acute damage at that moment
  3. If a finding needs following up you may be offered a repeat EKG, blood tests, an echocardiogram, a Holter monitor or a stress test
  4. Keeping a copy of your EKG is useful — future tracings are easier to judge against it

An EKG records electrical activity; an echocardiogram is an ultrasound that shows the heart's structure and pumping. See EKG vs echocardiogram for how the two tests fit together.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What did my EKG show?
  • Is there anything on it that needs following up?
  • Can I have a copy for my records?

Frequently asked questions

What does EKG stand for?

Electrocardiogram — "electro" for electrical, "cardio" for heart, "gram" for a recording. EKG uses the German spelling; ECG the English one. They mean the same test.

How long does an EKG take?

The recording takes about ten seconds. With attaching and removing the electrodes, the whole visit is usually five to ten minutes.

Does an EKG hurt?

No. The electrodes only stick to the skin and listen; there are no needles and no electric shock. Removing the sticky pads may tug at hair slightly.

How much does an EKG cost?

It varies widely by country and setting, from little or nothing where care is publicly funded to a typical self-pay price of roughly $40–$150 for the test itself in the United States, based on published price-transparency data. Visit fees and a cardiologist's interpretation may be billed separately — see where to get an EKG and what it costs.

Is an EKG the same as an ECG?

Yes — identical test, two spellings.

Do I need to do anything to prepare for an EKG?

Usually not. Wear a top that is easy to remove, avoid lotions on your skin that day, and bring a list of your medicines.

Can an EKG show a heart attack?

It can show signs of a current or previous heart attack, but a normal EKG does not always rule one out. Blood tests and other investigations are often used alongside it.

Is an EKG safe during pregnancy?

Yes. An EKG only records the heart's own electrical signals and uses no radiation, so it is safe at any stage of pregnancy.
