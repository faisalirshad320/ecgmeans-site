Posterior MI: What It Means on an ECG

Seek prompt care

A posterior MI is a heart attack affecting the back wall of the heart. Standard ECG leads do not look directly at this area, so it shows up as mirror-image changes — ST depression and tall R waves in the front chest leads (V1–V3). It is easy to miss, which is why it is important, and it needs urgent treatment like any heart attack.

What "posterior MI" means

A heart attack (myocardial infarction, MI) happens when a coronary artery is blocked and part of the heart muscle is starved of blood. A posterior MI affects the back wall of the heart. Because the standard 12 leads do not sit over the back, the usual sign of a heart attack — ST elevation — is not seen directly. Instead, the front leads show a mirror image.

What it looks like on the ECG

In leads V1–V3 the ECG shows the reverse of a heart attack: ST depression (instead of elevation), tall and broad R waves (instead of deep Q waves), and upright T waves. Placing extra leads on the back (V7–V9) can reveal the true ST elevation and confirm the diagnosis. Posterior MI often accompanies an inferior or lateral heart attack.

Why it matters

Because it hides in the standard leads, a posterior MI can be missed and treatment delayed. It is a genuine heart attack and, when recognised, is treated as urgently as any ST-elevation heart attack.

This is an emergency. Chest pain or pressure, breathlessness, sweating, nausea, or arm or jaw pain needs immediate medical attention — call emergency services.

Which artery is usually involved

The back wall of the heart is most often supplied by the right coronary artery or the circumflex branch of the left coronary artery, so a posterior MI usually reflects a blockage in one of these. This is also why it so often occurs together with an inferior or lateral heart attack, which share the same blood supply — recognising the mirror-image changes can reveal a larger area at risk than the standard leads suggest.

What usually happens next

  1. Recognising the mirror-image changes in V1–V3
  2. Recording posterior leads (V7–V9) to confirm ST elevation
  3. Serial troponin blood tests and urgent cardiology review
  4. Emergency angiography and opening of the blocked artery, usually with a stent

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Were posterior leads (V7–V9) recorded?
  • Which artery was blocked?
  • What treatment do I need right now?

Frequently asked questions

Why is a posterior MI easy to miss?

Because the standard ECG leads do not look directly at the back of the heart, so it shows only as mirror-image changes (ST depression and tall R waves) in V1–V3.

How is a posterior MI confirmed?

By recording extra leads placed on the back (V7–V9), which reveal the ST elevation that is hidden on the standard 12-lead ECG.

Is a posterior MI as serious as other heart attacks?

Yes — it is a true heart attack and is treated as an emergency, usually with urgent angiography and a stent.
