Left Axis Deviation: What It Means on an ECG

Usually harmless

Left axis deviation means the average direction of electrical flow through your ventricles points further up and to the left than usual — between −30° and −90°, where normal is −30° to +90°. It is a measurement rather than a disease, and as an isolated finding it usually needs no action.

What "left axis deviation" means

The ECG machine calculates the "QRS axis" from how tall the QRS is in the different limb leads, and prints it as a number in degrees. The axis drifts leftward naturally with age and with a horizontal heart position (for example in shorter, heavier people or late pregnancy). A value between 0° and −30° may be labelled borderline left axis deviation.

A clearly leftward axis has a short list of electrical and structural explanations, the commonest being left anterior fascicular block — a minor delay in one branch of the heart's wiring.

What it looks like on the ECG

The QRS is upright in lead I and mainly downward in leads II and aVF. The printed axis figure is more negative than −30°.

Common causes

  • Left anterior fascicular block
  • Left ventricular hypertrophy from high blood pressure
  • Old inferior heart attack
  • Left bundle branch block
  • Normal age-related shift or horizontal heart position
  • Paced rhythm or certain congenital conditions

Should you worry?

Usually harmless

Alone, left axis deviation has little significance. It is interpreted together with everything else on the tracing: with a wide QRS it suggests bundle branch block; with tall voltages, hypertension-related thickening; with inferior Q waves, a previous heart attack.

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

What usually happens next

  1. Usually nothing beyond blood-pressure checks
  2. Echocardiogram only if other findings or symptoms point to structural disease

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What is my axis in degrees?
  • Is there anything else on the ECG alongside it?
  • Is my blood pressure well enough controlled?

Frequently asked questions

Is left axis deviation serious?

Not by itself. It is one of the most common minor findings on adult ECGs.

Can left axis deviation be normal?

Yes, particularly a borderline value in older adults or people with a high diaphragm.

What is the most common cause?

Left anterior fascicular block, followed by changes related to long-standing high blood pressure.
