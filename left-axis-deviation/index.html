Left Axis Deviation: What It Means on an ECG

Usually harmless

"Left axis deviation" (sometimes abbreviated LAD — not to be confused with the left anterior descending artery) is a common line on ECG (EKG) reports, particularly in middle-aged and older adults. It describes the direction of the heart's electrical signal, worked out from the limb leads. This page explains how the axis is measured, what pushes it leftward, which combinations make doctors look further, and what usually happens next.

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBS · Medical DoctorPMDC 54440-SUpdated 2026-10-29
Quick answer

Left axis deviation means the average direction of electrical flow through the ventricles points further up and to the left than usual — between −30° and −90°, where the normal range is about −30° to +90°. It is a measurement, not a disease. The commonest cause is left anterior fascicular block, and as an isolated finding in a well person it usually needs no action.

What "left axis deviation" means

Every heartbeat sends a wave of electrical activation through the ventricles. The ECG machine adds up the direction of that wave and expresses it as an angle on a circle — the QRS axis, printed in degrees on most reports (often labelled "QRS axis" or "Axes: P, QRS, T").

  • Normal axis: about −30° to +90° (some references extend the upper limit to +100° or +105°)
  • Left axis deviation: −30° to −90°
  • Right axis deviation: +90° to +180° — see right axis deviation
  • Extreme (northwest) axis: −90° to ±180°, which is unusual and more often linked to significant problems

The axis drifts leftward naturally with age, and with a horizontal heart position — for example in shorter, heavier people, in late pregnancy or with fluid in the abdomen, all of which push the diaphragm up. A value just beyond −30° is sometimes labelled "borderline left axis deviation."

A clearly leftward axis has a short list of electrical and structural explanations. The commonest is left anterior fascicular block — a minor delay in one branch of the heart's wiring.

What it looks like on the ECG

You can estimate the axis from three limb leads:

Lead I Lead II Lead aVF Likely axis
Positive Positive Positive Normal (0° to +90°)
Positive Positive or flat Negative Normal-leftward (0° to −30°), still considered normal
Positive Negative Negative Left axis deviation (−30° to −90°)
Negative — Positive Right axis deviation
Negative — Negative Extreme axis

"Positive" means the QRS is mostly above the baseline; "negative" means mostly below. Lead II is the tie-breaker: if lead I is upright and aVF is negative, a negative lead II confirms true left axis deviation. Our ECG axis calculator works this out for you.

When the axis is between −45° and −90° with a narrow QRS and a qR pattern in aVL, the usual explanation is left anterior fascicular block.

Causes of left axis deviation

Common causes

  • Left anterior fascicular block (LAFB) — the most frequent cause of a clearly leftward axis
  • Left ventricular hypertrophy from long-standing high blood pressure — see left ventricular hypertrophy
  • Age-related shift and a horizontal heart position
  • Old inferior heart attack, which removes the downward-pointing electrical forces seen by the inferior leads — see inferior infarct, age undetermined
  • Left bundle branch block, which often (though not always) shifts the axis left

Less common causes

  • Paced rhythm from a pacemaker lead in the lower right ventricle
  • Wolff-Parkinson-White pattern, depending on where the extra pathway sits
  • High potassium
  • Congenital heart disease — classically an ostium primum atrial septal defect or tricuspid atresia, often noticed in childhood
  • Some ventricular rhythms, such as ventricular tachycardia arising from certain areas
  • Lead misplacement, especially swapped arm leads — see 12-lead ECG placement

Is left axis deviation dangerous?

On its own, rarely. Left axis deviation is a direction, not a diagnosis. Its importance comes entirely from what is causing it and what else is on the tracing. Doctors look further when it comes with these risk features:

Combined with What it may suggest
A wide QRS (120 ms or more) A bundle branch block, or an RBBB plus LAFB combination (bifascicular block)
RBBB and a long PR interval So-called trifascicular block — more extensive conduction disease
Tall voltages and strain-type ST–T changes Thickened heart muscle from high blood pressure or valve disease
Q waves in II, III and aVF An old inferior heart attack — see pathological Q waves
A short PR and a slurred QRS start Wolff-Parkinson-White pattern
Symptoms such as fainting or breathlessness Possible conduction or structural heart disease needing assessment
A sudden change from your previous ECG A new process, or lead misplacement

Some large population studies have linked a leftward axis with slightly higher long-term cardiovascular risk, but this mostly reflects the high blood pressure and heart disease behind it.

Symptoms to watch for

Left axis deviation itself causes no symptoms. Report these if they occur:

  • Fainting, blackouts or near-fainting
  • A very slow or irregular pulse
  • Breathlessness on exertion, or ankle swelling
  • Chest pain or pressure, particularly with exertion
  • Palpitations

What tests usually come next

  1. Often nothing, if the axis is only mildly leftward, the rest of the ECG is normal and you feel well.
  2. Check the electrodes and repeat the ECG if the change is sudden or unexpected.
  3. Compare with earlier ECGs to see whether the axis has shifted.
  4. Blood pressure measurement and a risk factor review.
  5. Echocardiogram if there are signs of thickened heart muscle, a murmur, symptoms, or a wide QRS. See EKG vs echocardiogram.
  6. Ambulatory monitoring if there are blackouts and the ECG shows conduction disease.

Treatment and management

Left axis deviation itself is not treated. Management is aimed at the cause: controlling blood pressure, managing coronary risk factors, treating valve or heart muscle disease, correcting potassium, or adjusting a pacemaker. A conduction combination such as bifascicular block with unexplained blackouts may lead to further monitoring and, sometimes, a pacemaker.

Special groups

  • Older adults: a mild leftward axis is common and often reflects age and a horizontal heart position.
  • Pregnancy: the growing uterus pushes the diaphragm and heart upward, so the axis often shifts left in later pregnancy and returns afterwards.
  • People with obesity or abdominal swelling: a raised diaphragm can tilt the axis leftward without any heart disease.
  • Children: babies normally have a rightward axis, so left axis deviation in a child is unusual and may prompt an echocardiogram to look for congenital heart disease.
  • Athletes: left axis deviation is not a typical training effect; sports cardiology guidelines treat isolated axis deviation as a "borderline" finding that matters mainly when combined with others.

Left vs right axis deviation and other look-alikes

Finding Key difference
Right axis deviation Lead I negative, aVF positive; more often linked to the right heart or lungs, or normal in young, thin people
Left anterior fascicular block A specific cause of left axis deviation, with an axis beyond −45° and qR in aVL
Left posterior fascicular block Causes right axis deviation, the opposite direction
Borderline ECG A report label often triggered by a mildly leftward axis

Get urgent help

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase. Call 911 in the US, 999 in the UK or 112 in Europe.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What is my QRS axis in degrees?
  • Does it meet the criteria for left anterior fascicular block?
  • Is there anything else on the ECG alongside it, such as a wide QRS or Q waves?
  • Has my axis changed compared with earlier ECGs?
  • Is my blood pressure well enough controlled, and do I need an echocardiogram?

Frequently asked questions

What does left axis deviation mean on an ECG?

It means the overall direction of electrical flow through the ventricles points further left and upward than usual, between −30° and −90°. It is a measurement rather than a disease.

Is left axis deviation serious?

Not by itself. It is one of the most common minor findings on adult ECGs and matters mainly when combined with other findings or symptoms.

What is the most common cause of left axis deviation?

Left anterior fascicular block, a minor delay in one branch of the left bundle. Long-standing high blood pressure with left ventricular hypertrophy is another frequent cause.

Can left axis deviation be normal?

Yes. A mildly leftward axis is common in older adults, in people with a horizontal heart position, and in late pregnancy.

What is borderline left axis deviation?

It describes an axis just beyond −30°. It is usually of no significance if the rest of the ECG is normal.

Can left axis deviation be caused by the ECG leads?

Yes. Misplaced or swapped limb electrodes can change the calculated axis, so a sudden unexpected axis change is often rechecked with a repeat ECG.

Does left axis deviation need treatment?

No. Treatment, if any, is aimed at the underlying cause, such as high blood pressure or valve disease.
