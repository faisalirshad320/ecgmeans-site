Sinoatrial (SA) Block on an ECG: What It Means

Depends on context

Sinoatrial (SA) block means your heart's natural pacemaker, the sinus node, fires normally, but its signal is delayed or blocked on its way to the upper chambers (the atria). The result is a missing or delayed heartbeat. Brief, occasional SA block, especially in young, fit people at rest or asleep, is usually harmless and causes no symptoms. It matters more if the pauses are long or frequent enough to make you dizzy, tired or faint.

What "Sinoatrial block" means

Your heartbeat normally starts in a small cluster of cells called the sinoatrial (SA) node, or sinus node. It acts as the heart's built-in pacemaker, sending out regular electrical signals that spread across the upper chambers (the atria) and then down to the lower chambers.

In sinoatrial block, the sinus node is still firing normally, but the signal is delayed or blocked as it leaves the node to reach the atria. Because the signal never gets out, the heart misses a beat, or a beat arrives late. You may also see this called SA exit block, because the problem is with the signal exiting the node.

This is different from sinus arrest, where the sinus node itself fails to fire. In SA block, the node keeps working and keeping time; only the outgoing signal is affected. The difference can matter when your doctor works out the cause and decides on next steps.

What it looks like on the ECG

On an ECG, each normal heartbeat produces a repeating pattern: a small P wave (the atria activating), followed by the QRS complex (the main chambers activating) and the T wave (recovery). Together these make up one P-QRS-T cycle.

In SA block, the ECG shows a dropped P-QRS-T cycle, meaning a whole beat is simply missing from the trace.

In the common form, called type II, the pause is a whole multiple of the underlying P-P interval (the normal gap between beats). For example, the pause may be exactly double the usual gap. This happens because the sinus node carries on keeping its own time in the background. One expected beat fails to get out, and the next one arrives right on schedule.

Apart from the dropped beat, the surrounding rhythm is otherwise regular. This neat whole-multiple pattern helps doctors tell SA exit block apart from sinus arrest.

Common causes

  • High vagal tone. The vagus nerve slows the heart. This is common in young, fit people, especially at rest or asleep.
  • Medicines, such as beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers and digoxin.
  • Sick sinus syndrome, a condition in which the sinus node doesn't work properly.
  • Ischaemia (reduced blood supply) affecting the sinus node.
  • Electrolyte disturbance, meaning an imbalance in the body's salts and minerals.

Should you worry?

Depends on context

Brief, occasional SA block caused by high vagal tone is usually benign and causes no symptoms. This is often what is seen in healthy, active people, particularly during rest or sleep. It matters more when the pauses are long or frequent enough to cause dizziness, fatigue or fainting, or when SA block is part of sick sinus syndrome. If you have any of these symptoms, tell your doctor so they can look at the finding in the context of your health.

What usually happens next

What happens next depends on whether you have symptoms and what might be causing the block.

  • Reviewing reversible causes. Your doctor will usually check whether a medicine, such as a beta-blocker, calcium-channel blocker or digoxin, could be contributing. They may adjust it if appropriate. Other factors, such as electrolyte levels, may also be checked.
  • Monitoring. Heart rhythm monitoring over a longer period may be used to match any symptoms you have to what your heart rhythm is doing at that moment. This helps show whether the pauses are actually causing how you feel.
  • Pacemaker, in some cases. If pauses are causing symptoms and no reversible cause is found, a pacemaker may be considered, much as it would be for sick sinus syndrome.

If the SA block was an occasional finding and you feel well, especially if you are young and fit, it may simply be noted without needing treatment. Always go through your own report with your doctor, who can interpret it alongside your symptoms, medicines and overall health.

Frequently asked questions

What does sinoatrial block mean on my ECG?

It means the sinus node, your heart's natural pacemaker, fired normally, but its signal was delayed or blocked before reaching the upper chambers. This shows up as a missing or delayed heartbeat on the ECG. It is different from sinus arrest, where the node itself fails to fire.

Is sinoatrial block dangerous?

It depends on the context. Brief, occasional SA block from high vagal tone is usually benign and causes no symptoms. It matters more if the pauses are long or frequent enough to cause dizziness, fatigue or fainting, or if it is part of sick sinus syndrome.

What causes sinoatrial block?

A common cause is high vagal tone, which is often seen in young, fit people, especially at rest or asleep. Other causes include medicines such as beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers and digoxin. Sick sinus syndrome, reduced blood supply to the sinus node and electrolyte disturbances can also cause it.

How do doctors tell SA block apart from sinus arrest?

In the common type II form of SA block, the pause is a whole multiple of the normal gap between beats, for example exactly double. This happens because the sinus node keeps its own time and only one expected beat is missing. That pattern helps distinguish SA exit block from sinus arrest.

What happens next if I have sinoatrial block?

Your doctor will usually review possible reversible causes, such as medicines. Heart rhythm monitoring may be used to see whether your symptoms match the pauses. If pauses cause symptoms and no reversible cause is found, a pacemaker may be considered, as with sick sinus syndrome.
