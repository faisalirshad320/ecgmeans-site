Low Voltage QRS: What It Means

"Low voltage QRS" means the main spikes of your ECG are unusually small: under 5 mm in all six limb leads, or under 10 mm in all six chest leads. Either the heart is generating less signal, or — more commonly — something between the heart and the skin electrodes, such as fat, air or fluid, is dampening it.

Quick answer

Low voltage QRS means the main spikes on your ECG are smaller than normal: under 5 mm in every limb lead, or under 10 mm in every chest lead. Most often the signal is dampened by body build, lung disease such as COPD, or fluid around the heart or lungs. Less often the heart muscle itself is affected. Its importance depends on the cause, so doctors look at symptoms, old ECGs and often an echocardiogram.

What "low voltage QRS" means

QRS height depends on how much working heart muscle there is and how well its signal conducts to the skin. Anything that adds insulation reduces the height: a large body, over-inflated lungs, or fluid around the heart or lungs. Less often the muscle itself is the issue — replaced by scar after several heart attacks, or infiltrated by abnormal protein in amyloidosis.

Doctors pay attention to the mismatch: low voltage on the ECG together with thick heart walls on an echocardiogram is a recognised clue to cardiac amyloidosis.

Your ECG or EKG report may say "low voltage QRS", "low QRS voltage", "low voltage in limb leads", "low voltage in precordial leads" or simply "low voltage". ECG and EKG are the same test. The phrase is a description of size only; it does not by itself say anything about your heart rhythm.

It is often a computer-generated statement. A doctor reading the ECG will check whether it truly meets the criteria and whether it is new.

What it looks like on the ECG

All complexes look short. The rhythm, intervals and wave shapes may otherwise be completely normal.

QRS height is measured from the lowest point (the bottom of the Q or S wave) to the highest point (the top of the R wave) in each lead. On standard ECG paper, 10 mm equals 1 millivolt (mV).

Criterion Threshold Notes
Low voltage in the limb leads QRS under 5 mm (0.5 mV) in all of leads I, II, III, aVR, aVL and aVF The most commonly reported form
Low voltage in the chest (precordial) leads QRS under 10 mm (1.0 mV) in all of leads V1–V6 Less common on its own
Generalised low voltage Both of the above More likely to reflect a significant cause
Calibration The calibration box should be 10 mm tall A 5 mm box (half-standard) makes every complex look half size

Other findings that may appear alongside it, and point towards a cause:

  • Sinus tachycardia plus electrical alternans — suggests a large pericardial effusion
  • Tall, peaked P waves (P pulmonale) and a rightward axis — suggest COPD/emphysema
  • Poor R wave progression — can occur in amyloidosis, previous heart attacks or COPD
  • Fragmented QRS or Q waves — can suggest scar in the heart muscle
  • Slow heart rate — can accompany an underactive thyroid

Low voltage QRS causes

Common causes (signal dampened on its way to the skin):

  • Obesity or a large chest
  • Emphysema / COPD
  • Fluid around the heart (pericardial effusion) or lungs (pleural effusion)
  • Generalised swelling (severe oedema)
  • Machine gain set to half-standard (5 mm/mV) — check the calibration mark
  • Electrode placement problems or poor skin contact

Less common causes (less signal generated by the heart, or a specific disease):

  • Underactive thyroid
  • Extensive previous heart attacks or cardiomyopathy
  • Infiltrative disease such as amyloidosis
  • Constrictive pericarditis (a stiff, thickened pericardium)
  • Air around the heart or lungs, such as a large left-sided pneumothorax
  • After heart surgery, when fluid or air may collect for a while

Is low voltage QRS serious?

Often it simply reflects body build or lung disease already known about. It needs a clearer explanation when it is new, when it comes with breathlessness, ankle swelling, light-headedness or a fast heart rate, or when no obvious reason exists.

Situation Usually means Typical next step
Long-standing, larger body build or known COPD, no symptoms Signal dampening Compare with old ECGs; often no further tests
Calibration box only 5 mm tall Recording setting, not the heart Repeat at standard calibration
New low voltage compared with previous ECGs Something has changed Echocardiogram
Low voltage with a fast heart rate or breathlessness Possible pericardial effusion Prompt echocardiogram
Low voltage with thick heart walls on echo Possible cardiac amyloidosis Specialist tests
Low voltage with tiredness, weight gain, feeling cold Possible underactive thyroid Thyroid blood tests

The direction of change matters. A fall in voltage compared with an older tracing is more meaningful than a single low reading, because it suggests something new between the heart and the electrodes or a change in the heart muscle.

Symptoms to watch for

Low voltage itself causes no symptoms. Symptoms come from the underlying cause:

  • Breathlessness, especially when lying flat, or a fast heart rate — fluid around the heart
  • Ankle and leg swelling, tiredness — heart failure, kidney or liver problems, amyloidosis
  • Tiredness, weight gain, constipation, feeling cold — underactive thyroid
  • Chronic cough and breathlessness — COPD
  • Light-headedness or fainting — needs prompt assessment

Tests that usually come next

  1. Check the calibration box on the printout is 10 mm tall.
  2. Comparison with old ECGs to see whether voltage has fallen.
  3. Echocardiogram to look for fluid around the heart and at wall thickness. See EKG vs echocardiogram.
  4. Thyroid blood tests where appropriate.
  5. Chest X-ray to look for lung disease, pleural effusion or an enlarged heart shadow.
  6. Further tests for amyloidosis if the echo shows unexplained thick walls, such as specialised blood and urine tests, a cardiac MRI or a nuclear scan.

Low voltage QRS treatment

There is no treatment for low voltage as such. Management is aimed at the cause:

  • Body build or COPD: no specific action; COPD is managed in its own right.
  • Pericardial effusion: treating the cause, and draining the fluid if it is large or pressing on the heart. See cardiac tamponade on the ECG and pericarditis on the ECG.
  • Underactive thyroid: thyroid hormone replacement, after which voltage may improve.
  • Cardiac amyloidosis: specialist treatment, which has improved considerably in recent years, so early recognition matters.
  • Heart failure or cardiomyopathy: standard heart failure care.

Special groups

  • People with obesity: low voltage is common and usually reflects tissue between heart and electrodes. A new fall in voltage is still worth checking.
  • Older adults: cardiac amyloidosis is more common with age and may be under-recognised. Low voltage with unexplained heart thickening, heart failure or carpal tunnel syndrome raises suspicion.
  • Athletes: athletes usually have higher QRS voltages, so low voltage in an athlete is unexpected and deserves review.
  • Pregnancy: voltage can vary as the heart shifts position, and small effusions can occur; marked low voltage with breathlessness is assessed with an echocardiogram.
  • After heart surgery: temporary low voltage from fluid or air is common and is monitored.

How it differs from look-alike findings

  • Left ventricular hypertrophy: the opposite — tall voltages. A thick heart on echo with low ECG voltage is the clue to amyloidosis rather than ordinary hypertrophy.
  • Electrical alternans: QRS height alternates every beat, rather than being consistently small.
  • Nonspecific ST abnormality: often printed alongside low voltage, but describes the ST segment, not QRS size.
  • Borderline ECG: many reports with low voltage are labelled borderline because the finding is common and often benign.
  • Half-standard recording: everything looks small, but the calibration box gives it away.

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Was the ECG recorded at standard calibration?
  • Is this new compared with before?
  • Do I need an echocardiogram?
  • Could my thyroid be involved?
  • Is there any sign of fluid around my heart or lungs?
  • If the echo shows thick heart walls, should amyloidosis be considered?

Frequently asked questions

What does low voltage QRS mean on an ECG?

It means the QRS complexes are smaller than normal: under 5 mm in all limb leads or under 10 mm in all chest leads. It usually reflects something dampening the signal, such as body build, lung disease or fluid.

Is low voltage QRS serious?

It depends entirely on the cause. Body build is harmless; a large pericardial effusion is not. New low voltage with symptoms should be assessed promptly.

Can low voltage QRS be normal?

Yes. A small percentage of healthy people meet the criteria with no underlying condition.

Does being overweight cause low voltage?

Yes. Extra tissue between the heart and the electrodes weakens the recorded signal.

What are the most common causes of low voltage QRS?

Obesity, COPD or emphysema, and fluid around the heart or lungs are the most common. Less common causes include an underactive thyroid, previous heart attacks, and cardiac amyloidosis.

Can low voltage on an ECG be a recording error?

Yes. If the machine was set to half-standard (5 mm/mV), every complex looks half size. The calibration box on the printout should be 10 mm tall.

What test is done after a low voltage ECG?

An echocardiogram is the most common next step, because it shows fluid around the heart, wall thickness and pumping strength. Comparison with old ECGs and thyroid tests are also common.

Is low voltage QRS a sign of a heart attack?

Not usually. Extensive scarring from previous heart attacks can reduce voltage, but low voltage on its own is not a sign of a current heart attack.
