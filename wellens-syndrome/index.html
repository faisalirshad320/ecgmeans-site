Wellens Syndrome: What It Means on an ECG

Seek prompt care

Wellens syndrome is a specific pattern of T-wave changes in the chest leads (V2–V3) that warns of a critically narrowed left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery. It often appears when chest pain has settled, which can be falsely reassuring. It is a red-flag pattern that needs urgent cardiology assessment because a large heart attack may be close.

What "Wellens syndrome" means

Wellens syndrome (also called LAD coronary T-wave syndrome) is named after the doctors who described it. It points to a tight narrowing in the left anterior descending artery, which supplies a large part of the heart. The warning is important because people can look and feel well between episodes of pain, yet be at high risk of a major heart attack within days if untreated.

What it looks like on the ECG

There are two types, both seen in leads V2–V3 (sometimes wider):

  • Type A (Type 1): biphasic T waves that go up then down (the earlier, less common form)
  • Type B (Type 2): deeply inverted, symmetrical T waves (the more common form)

Importantly, the ST segment is only minimally raised or normal, and there are no pathological Q waves, so the ECG can look deceptively unalarming at a glance. The changes are typically seen when pain has eased.

Why it is urgent

The pattern reflects a reperfused but critically narrowed artery. A stress test in this situation can be dangerous and is avoided. The right path is to assume a high-grade LAD lesion and arrange prompt angiography.

This is an emergency pattern. Any return of chest pain, pressure, breathlessness, sweating, or arm or jaw discomfort needs immediate medical attention — call emergency services.

Why the timing is deceptive

The Wellens pattern classically appears after an episode of chest pain has settled, during a pain-free window, which is deceptive because the person may look and feel well while a critically narrowed artery remains. The T-wave changes can also evolve — the biphasic (Type A) form often appears first and deepens into the inverted (Type B) form over hours to days as the situation progresses.

What usually happens next

  1. Urgent cardiology review and serial troponin blood tests
  2. Avoiding stress testing, which can provoke a heart attack
  3. Coronary angiography to find and treat the LAD narrowing
  4. Treatment of the blockage, often with a stent

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Do my ECG changes fit the Wellens pattern?
  • Do I need an angiogram rather than a stress test?
  • What medicines should I start now to protect my heart?

Frequently asked questions

Why is Wellens syndrome so important?

Because the T-wave pattern signals a critically narrowed LAD artery and a high risk of a large heart attack within days, even when the person currently feels well.

What are the two types of Wellens T waves?

Type A shows biphasic T waves that go up then down; Type B shows deep, symmetrical T-wave inversion in V2–V3.

Should I have a stress test for Wellens syndrome?

No — stress testing can be dangerous in this situation. The recommended step is prompt coronary angiography.
