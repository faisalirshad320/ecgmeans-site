Accelerated Junctional Rhythm: What It Means

Needs clinician review

Accelerated junctional rhythm is a rhythm arising from the AV junction at 60–100 beats per minute — faster than the junction's natural 40–60, so it outpaces the sinus node. The QRS is narrow and normal-looking but the P waves are missing or upside-down. It usually signals irritation of the junction by a drug, recent surgery or reduced blood supply.

Quick answer

Accelerated junctional rhythm (AJR) means the heartbeat is being set by the AV junction, the relay station between the upper and lower chambers, at 60–100 beats per minute instead of by the sinus node. The QRS is narrow, but P waves are missing or upside down. It is rarely dangerous in itself; it matters because it often points to a cause such as digoxin toxicity, recent heart surgery or a heart attack.

What "accelerated junctional rhythm" means

Your heartbeat normally starts in the sinus node at the top of the right atrium. The AV junction — the AV node and the bundle of His just below it — is a back-up pacemaker. If the sinus node fails, the junction steps in at about 40–60 beats per minute.

In an ordinary junctional rhythm the junction takes over because the sinus node is too slow. In the accelerated form the junction itself is firing faster than it should (enhanced automaticity) and captures the heart even though the sinus node is working. Above 100 per minute the same process is called junctional tachycardia.

Your report may say "accelerated junctional rhythm", "AJR" or "accelerated junctional rhythm with AV dissociation". ECG and EKG are the same test; both spellings appear on reports.

Because the rate is in the normal range, most people feel nothing. It is usually discovered on a monitor or routine ECG.

The junctional rhythm family by rate

The same pacemaker site produces different names depending on how fast it fires:

Name on the report Rate (beats per minute) What it usually means
Junctional bradycardia Under 40 A slow back-up rhythm; the sinus node and junction are both sluggish
Junctional escape rhythm 40–60 A protective back-up when the sinus node is too slow
Accelerated junctional rhythm 60–100 An irritable junction overtaking a working sinus node
Junctional tachycardia Over 100 A very irritable junction; same causes, faster rate

What it looks like on the ECG

A regular rhythm at 60–100 with narrow QRS complexes and no upright P wave before them in lead II. Sometimes unrelated P waves can be seen marching through at a different rate.

ECG feature Typical finding in accelerated junctional rhythm
Rate 60–100 beats per minute
Regularity Regular
QRS width Narrow (under 120 ms), unless there is a pre-existing bundle branch block
P waves Absent (hidden in the QRS), or inverted in leads II, III and aVF
P wave position Just before the QRS with a short PR (under 120 ms), or just after the QRS
Relation to sinus P waves Sometimes independent sinus P waves at a similar rate (AV dissociation)
Onset Often gradual "warm-up" rather than sudden

The upside-down P wave happens because the impulse travels backwards up into the atria from the junction, the opposite direction to normal. You may see this described as a retrograde or inverted P wave.

When the sinus node and the junction fire at almost the same rate, the two can drift in and out of control, so sinus P waves appear to slide towards and into the QRS. This is called isorhythmic AV dissociation and is common with AJR. It is a description of timing, not a sign of complete heart block.

Accelerated junctional rhythm causes

Common causes:

  • Digoxin toxicity (the classic cause)
  • After heart valve or congenital heart surgery
  • Inferior heart attack
  • Myocarditis or rheumatic fever
  • Beta-agonist or theophylline excess
  • Low potassium

Less common causes:

  • Catecholamine infusions in intensive care (such as isoprenaline, dopamine or adrenaline)
  • Catheter ablation for AVNRT, where a short junctional rhythm during the procedure is expected and usually settles
  • Low oxygen levels, severe lung disease or acute illness
  • Occasionally in otherwise healthy people, especially at rest, without a clear cause

For more on the drug-related changes, see digoxin effect on the ECG. Digoxin toxicity is especially likely with kidney problems, low potassium, or new medicines that raise digoxin levels.

Is accelerated junctional rhythm dangerous?

Needs clinician review

The rhythm is generally well tolerated and often temporary. Its significance lies in the cause, so it should prompt a check of medicines, blood tests and, where relevant, assessment for heart inflammation or ischaemia.

Doctors weigh it up using these features:

Feature More reassuring Needs prompt attention
Setting Day or two after heart surgery, closely monitored New, unexplained, outside hospital
Medicines Not on digoxin Taking digoxin, especially with nausea or visual changes
Blood pressure Normal Low, with dizziness or confusion
Chest pain None Present — possible heart attack
Potassium and kidney function Normal Low potassium or worsening kidney function
Heart pumping Normal Weak or stiff heart that relies on the atria's "kick"

One practical point: because the atria and ventricles are no longer working in step, the small boost the atria normally give to filling (the "atrial kick") is lost. Most people don't notice, but someone with heart failure or a stiff, thickened heart may feel tired or light-headed, and blood pressure can drop.

Symptoms to watch for

Often none. When symptoms occur, they include:

  • A sense of pounding or fluttering in the neck (the atria contracting against closed valves)
  • Mild tiredness or reduced exercise tolerance
  • Light-headedness, especially on standing
  • Symptoms of the cause — for digoxin toxicity: nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, confusion or blurred and yellow-tinged vision

Tests that usually come next

  1. Digoxin level and electrolytes (potassium, magnesium) and kidney function.
  2. 12-lead ECG to confirm the rhythm and look for signs of a heart attack, especially in the inferior leads.
  3. Troponin blood test if there is chest pain or a possible heart attack.
  4. Review of recent surgery, illness and all medicines, including inhalers and over-the-counter products.
  5. Continuous monitoring or a Holter to see whether the rhythm comes and goes.
  6. Echocardiogram if myocarditis, heart failure or a valve problem is suspected.

Accelerated junctional rhythm treatment

Treatment is aimed at the cause, and that usually restores normal sinus rhythm:

  • Digoxin toxicity: digoxin is stopped and potassium corrected. Severe toxicity is treated in hospital, sometimes with a digoxin antidote (digoxin-specific antibody fragments).
  • After heart surgery: usually observed; it tends to settle as inflammation subsides. Temporary pacing wires can be used to pace the atria slightly faster if blood pressure suffers.
  • Heart attack: treated as the heart attack; the rhythm usually resolves with it.
  • Drug excess: the responsible medicine is reduced or stopped.

Specific rhythm treatment is seldom needed. Medicines that slow the junction, such as beta-blockers, are occasionally used if the rhythm persists and causes symptoms, but not in digoxin toxicity without specialist advice.

Special groups

  • Children: after congenital heart surgery, a fast junctional rhythm (junctional ectopic tachycardia) is a recognised complication and is managed in paediatric intensive care. Slower accelerated junctional rhythms in children are usually monitored.
  • Older adults: more likely to be taking digoxin and to have kidney problems that raise digoxin levels, so a drug cause is checked first.
  • Athletes: well-trained people with a slow sinus rate may show brief junctional rhythms at rest, usually escape rhythms rather than accelerated ones. A true accelerated junctional rhythm in an athlete still warrants review.
  • Pregnancy: junctional rhythms are uncommon; any new rhythm finding in pregnancy is reviewed with attention to electrolytes, thyroid and medicines.

How it differs from look-alike findings

  • Junctional escape rhythm: same appearance, but slower (40–60) and protective, filling in for a slow sinus node.
  • Accelerated idioventricular rhythm: a similar rate range but from the ventricles, so the QRS is wide. It is often seen after reopening a blocked artery.
  • Ectopic atrial rhythm: a low-atrial focus can also produce inverted P waves in the inferior leads, but usually with a normal PR interval.
  • Lown-Ganong-Levine pattern and other short PR intervals: these have a short PR with a normal upright P wave, whereas a junctional rhythm has an inverted or absent P wave.
  • AVNRT: a re-entry circuit around the AV node causing a sudden-onset, sudden-offset fast rhythm, usually 140–250, rather than a gradual "warm-up" at a normal rate.

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Is a medicine causing this?
  • Should my digoxin level be checked, and do any of my other medicines interact with it?
  • Is my potassium normal?
  • Is there any sign of a heart attack or heart inflammation?
  • Will it settle by itself, and how will we know?
  • Do I need a repeat ECG or Holter monitor?

Frequently asked questions

What does accelerated junctional rhythm mean on an ECG?

It means the AV junction, not the sinus node, is setting the heartbeat at 60–100 beats per minute. The QRS is narrow and the P waves are absent or inverted.

How is it different from a junctional escape rhythm?

Only by rate and mechanism: escape is 40–60 and protective; accelerated is 60–100 and due to an irritable junction.

Is accelerated junctional rhythm serious?

It is rarely dangerous in itself, but causes such as digoxin toxicity need prompt treatment.

Does it cause symptoms?

Often none. Some people notice pounding in the neck or mild tiredness because the atria and ventricles are not contracting in sequence.

What is the most common cause of accelerated junctional rhythm?

Digoxin toxicity is the classic cause. Others include recent heart surgery, an inferior heart attack, myocarditis and low potassium.

Does accelerated junctional rhythm need a pacemaker?

Almost never. It usually settles once the cause is treated, and a permanent pacemaker is not part of standard treatment for it.

Can accelerated junctional rhythm go away on its own?

Yes. It is often temporary, particularly after heart surgery or once an offending medicine is stopped or electrolytes are corrected.
