U Wave: What It Means on an ECG

Depends on context

A U wave is a small, rounded bump that sometimes follows the T wave on an ECG. Small U waves are a normal finding, most visible at slow heart rates and in the chest leads. Prominent U waves are the ones that matter — they are a classic sign of a low blood potassium level and of certain medicines.

What "U wave" means

The U wave represents the final part of the heart's electrical recovery. In most people it is tiny or not seen at all. It becomes taller when recovery is prolonged — most importantly when blood potassium is low, but also with low magnesium or calcium, a slow heart rate, and drugs that lengthen repolarization.

Very large U waves can merge with the T wave and lengthen the apparent QT interval, which is why low potassium also raises the risk of a dangerous rhythm. Inverted U waves are less common and can be a sign of heart disease.

What it looks like on the ECG

A small, rounded deflection after the T wave, in the same direction, best seen in leads V2–V3 and at slow heart rates. "Prominent" U waves are taller than about 1–2 mm or taller than the T wave before them.

Common causes

  • Low potassium (hypokalaemia) — the classic cause of prominent U waves
  • Low magnesium or calcium
  • Slow heart rate (bradycardia)
  • Medicines, including some antiarrhythmics and digoxin
  • A normal variant (small U waves)
  • Inverted U waves: ischaemia, high blood pressure

Should you worry?

Depends on context

A small U wave is normal and needs nothing. Prominent U waves prompt a check of potassium and magnesium, because correcting a low level removes the risk. New inverted U waves deserve review, as they can accompany heart disease.

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

What usually happens next

  1. A blood test for potassium, magnesium and calcium
  2. Review of medicines
  3. Correcting a low electrolyte, which resolves the U waves
  4. Further assessment if U waves are inverted or there are other changes

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Could my potassium or magnesium be low?
  • Are any of my medicines responsible?
  • Are these prominent or inverted U waves?

Frequently asked questions

Are U waves normal?

Small U waves are a normal finding. Prominent or inverted U waves are the ones that may need attention.

What causes prominent U waves?

Most often a low blood potassium level; also low magnesium, a slow heart rate and certain medicines.

Are U waves dangerous?

Not in themselves, but prominent U waves can point to low potassium, which raises the risk of a dangerous rhythm and is worth correcting.
