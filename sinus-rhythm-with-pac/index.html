Sinus Rhythm With PACs: What It Means

Usually harmless

"Sinus rhythm with PACs" means your underlying rhythm is normal, with occasional early beats arising from the upper chambers (premature atrial complexes, also called contractions). Almost everyone has some. They may be felt as a skipped beat or flutter and are harmless in the great majority of people. This page explains how PACs look on an ECG (EKG), what triggers them, when frequent PACs deserve follow-up, and how they differ from PVCs and other early beats.

Quick answer

"Sinus rhythm with PACs" means your main heart rhythm is normal, but there are occasional early beats starting in the upper chambers (premature atrial complexes or contractions). On the ECG each PAC is an early beat with a differently shaped P wave, a normal narrow QRS and a short pause after it. PACs are very common, often felt as a skipped beat, and usually harmless.

What "sinus rhythm with PACs" means

Your report has two parts. Sinus rhythm means most beats start normally in the sinus node. PACs — premature atrial complexes, also called premature atrial contractions or atrial ectopic beats — are extra beats that start somewhere else in the atria (the upper chambers) before the next normal beat is due.

A PAC happens when a spot in the atria fires before the sinus node's next scheduled beat. The early impulse travels down the normal wiring, so the QRS looks normal; the sinus node then resets, giving a short pause before the next beat. That pause, and the stronger beat after it, is what people notice.

24-hour monitoring finds PACs in most healthy adults, and they become more frequent with age. A single PAC on a 10-second ECG is therefore an everyday finding. Variants on reports include "sinus rhythm with premature atrial complexes", "with occasional PACs", "with frequent PACs" and "with aberrantly conducted PACs". ECG and EKG are the same test.

What a PAC looks like on the ECG

A beat arriving earlier than expected, with a P wave that looks slightly different from the others, a normal narrow QRS, and then a brief pause.

Feature Normal sinus beat PAC
Timing On schedule Early
P wave Normal shape, upright in lead II Different shape; may be flat, pointed, inverted or hidden in the previous T wave
PR interval 120–200 ms Often different from the sinus beats; can be short or long
QRS complex Narrow (under 120 ms) Usually narrow; occasionally wide if conducted with aberrancy
Pause afterwards — Usually less than fully compensatory, because the sinus node is reset

Some special forms appear on reports:

  • Blocked (non-conducted) PAC: the early P wave arrives so soon that the AV node is not ready, so no QRS follows. It shows as an unexpected pause and can be mistaken for a dropped beat or heart block.
  • Aberrantly conducted PAC: a very early PAC reaches the ventricles while one bundle branch is still recovering, so the QRS is wide and can look like a PVC. See aberrant conduction and the Ashman phenomenon.
  • Patterns: a PAC after every normal beat is atrial bigeminy; after every two normal beats it is trigeminy. Two in a row are a couplet; three or more in a row form a short run of atrial tachycardia.

Causes and triggers

Common

  • A normal finding with no particular trigger
  • Caffeine, alcohol and nicotine
  • Stress, anxiety, tiredness and poor sleep
  • Decongestants (such as pseudoephedrine) and some asthma inhalers
  • Exercise recovery and hormonal changes, including pregnancy

Less common or underlying conditions

  • Low potassium or magnesium
  • Overactive thyroid
  • High blood pressure or an enlarged left atrium (left atrial enlargement)
  • Heart valve disease, heart failure or lung disease such as COPD
  • Obstructive sleep apnoea

Are PACs dangerous?

Occasional PACs need no treatment and do not damage the heart. The questions doctors ask are how many, whether they cause symptoms, and what else is going on.

Very frequent PACs — many hundreds or thousands a day — are associated with a higher chance of developing atrial fibrillation later, and in some studies with a higher stroke risk. So they may prompt monitoring and attention to blood pressure, sleep apnoea and alcohol. Even then, most people with frequent PACs never develop atrial fibrillation.

Features that lead to further review:

  • Frequent PACs on a 10-second ECG, or many on a Holter
  • Runs of PACs (short bursts of atrial tachycardia)
  • Palpitations that last minutes rather than seconds, or come with dizziness or breathlessness
  • An enlarged atrium, valve disease or other abnormalities on the ECG or echo

Symptoms

Many people feel nothing. Others notice:

  • A skipped, missed or "dropped" beat
  • A thump or flip-flop in the chest (the stronger beat after the pause)
  • A brief flutter, often at rest or when lying down

PACs do not cause chest pain or collapse on their own. If those occur, they need separate assessment.

What tests come next?

  1. Usually nothing — isolated PACs in a well person just need reassurance.
  2. Holter or patch monitor (24 hours to 2 weeks) if PACs are frequent or symptomatic, to count them and look for runs or atrial fibrillation.
  3. Blood tests — thyroid function, potassium and magnesium where relevant.
  4. Echocardiogram if there is a murmur, high blood pressure, breathlessness or a large atrium on the ECG.
  5. Sleep assessment if snoring and daytime sleepiness suggest sleep apnoea.

Treatment

  1. Usually reassurance — no treatment is needed for occasional PACs.
  2. Lifestyle — cutting back on caffeine and alcohol if they are troublesome, stopping smoking, improving sleep and managing stress.
  3. Treat underlying causes — blood pressure, thyroid, electrolytes, sleep apnoea.
  4. Medicines — beta-blockers or certain calcium-channel blockers occasionally, purely for symptom relief.
  5. Catheter ablation — rarely, for very frequent, highly symptomatic PACs from a single focus, or PACs that repeatedly trigger atrial fibrillation.

Special groups

  • Pregnancy: PACs are common and usually harmless. Tell your midwife or doctor if palpitations are frequent, prolonged or come with breathlessness or fainting.
  • Athletes: isolated PACs are common and do not usually limit sport.
  • Children: PACs are seen in healthy children and newborns and usually settle without treatment.
  • Older adults: PACs become more frequent with age; very frequent PACs are more often linked to atrial enlargement and later atrial fibrillation, so monitoring is more likely.

How PACs differ from look-alike findings

Finding Key difference Usual significance
PAC Early, abnormal P wave; narrow QRS Usually harmless
PVC No P wave in front; wide, bizarre QRS; full compensatory pause Usually harmless; burden matters
Premature junctional contraction No P wave, or an inverted P wave just before or after a narrow QRS Usually harmless
Sinus arrhythmia Gradual speeding and slowing; all P waves identical Normal
Multifocal atrial tachycardia Three or more P-wave shapes at a rate over 100 Needs clinician review
Atrial fibrillation No P waves; irregularly irregular throughout Needs clinician review

For a side-by-side guide, see PVC vs PAC. PACs can also act as the trigger for sudden fast rhythms such as AVNRT in people who have the necessary extra pathway.

Get urgent help

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • How frequent were the PACs on my tracing?
  • Would a 24-hour or longer monitor be useful?
  • Is there any sign of atrial fibrillation or runs of fast beats?
  • Is my left atrium enlarged, or my blood pressure a factor?
  • Could caffeine, alcohol, sleep apnoea or a medicine be triggering them?

Frequently asked questions

What does sinus rhythm with PACs mean?

It means your heart rhythm is normal, with occasional early beats starting in the upper chambers. These premature atrial complexes are very common and usually harmless.

Are PACs dangerous?

Not in themselves. Occasional PACs do not harm the heart; very frequent PACs are a marker for a higher chance of atrial fibrillation later and may prompt monitoring.

What is the difference between a PAC and a PVC?

A PAC starts in the upper chambers and has a narrow QRS; a PVC starts in the lower chambers and has a wide, odd-looking QRS.

Can PACs turn into atrial fibrillation?

Very frequent PACs are a risk marker for it, but most people with PACs never develop atrial fibrillation.

What causes PACs?

Often nothing specific. Common triggers are caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, stress, poor sleep and decongestants; less common causes include thyroid problems, low potassium or magnesium, high blood pressure and sleep apnoea.

What is a blocked or non-conducted PAC?

It is a PAC that arrives so early that it cannot pass through the AV node, so no QRS follows. It appears as a pause and can look like a dropped beat.

How are PACs treated?

Most need no treatment. Reducing triggers helps many people, beta-blockers can ease troublesome symptoms, and ablation is reserved for rare, severe cases.

How many PACs a day is normal?

Most healthy adults have some PACs on 24-hour monitoring, and the number rises with age. Doctors pay more attention when there are many hundreds or thousands a day, or runs of fast beats.
