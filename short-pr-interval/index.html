Short PR Interval on an ECG: Causes, Meaning and When It Matters

Depends on context

"Short PR interval" is one of the more unsettling lines to find on an ECG report, partly because it sounds precise and partly because the internet links it to Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome. The reality is more reassuring: a short PR on its own, with a normal-looking QRS and no symptoms, is common and usually means nothing more than fast conduction through the AV node. This page explains what the measurement is, every cause worth knowing about, how to tell the harmless version from the one that needs attention, and what your doctor will do next.

Quick answer

A short PR interval (under 120 ms) means the electrical signal reached your ventricles a little faster than usual. In most people it is a harmless variant of normal conduction. It matters when the QRS complex shows a delta wave (the Wolff-Parkinson-White pattern) or when you get sudden episodes of a racing heart — then it needs a cardiology review.

What a short PR interval means on your ECG

The PR interval is the time from the start of the P wave (the atria firing) to the start of the QRS complex (the ventricles firing). Most of that time is spent in the AV node, which deliberately slows the signal so the atria finish contracting before the ventricles start.

Measurement Value What it means
Normal PR interval 120–200 ms (3–5 small squares) The AV node is doing its usual job
Short PR interval Under 120 ms (fewer than 3 small squares) The signal reached the ventricles early
Long PR interval Over 200 ms Conduction through the node is delayed (first-degree AV block)

So "short PR interval" is a timing measurement, not a diagnosis. The ECG machine flags it automatically whenever the PR falls below 120 ms. What that means for you depends entirely on why the signal was fast — and the rest of the ECG usually answers that question.

What causes a short PR interval

There are only a handful of causes, and they fall into two groups: the signal bypassed the AV node, or the node simply conducted quickly.

Cause How common How the ECG looks What it usually means
Normal fast AV conduction Common — a few percent of healthy people Short PR, normal narrow QRS, no delta wave A normal variant. Needs no treatment
Faster heart rate (exercise, anxiety, fever, caffeine) Common Short PR that lengthens again when the rate settles Physiological; adrenaline speeds up the AV node
Junctional or low atrial rhythm Fairly common, often transient Short PR with an inverted or absent P wave in leads II, III, aVF The beat started near the AV node rather than in the sinus node
Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) pattern Roughly 1–3 in every 1,000 people Short PR plus a delta wave (slurred QRS upstroke) and a slightly wide QRS An accessory pathway bypasses the AV node. Needs cardiology assessment
Enhanced AV nodal conduction (historically "Lown-Ganong-Levine syndrome") Uncommon Short PR, normal QRS, episodes of fast heart rate Treated as a description rather than a distinct disease by most cardiologists today
Rare metabolic or genetic conditions (Pompe disease, Fabry disease, some cardiomyopathies) Rare Short PR alongside other abnormalities such as marked voltage changes Usually already known about, or suspected from other findings

The single most useful question is whether there is a delta wave. Without it, a short PR is almost always one of the first three causes. With it, it is WPW pattern until proven otherwise.

Is a short PR interval dangerous?

For most people, no.

A short PR interval with a normal, narrow QRS and no symptoms is not dangerous. It does not damage the heart, it does not progress to anything, and it does not need treatment or follow-up beyond your doctor noting it on your record.

The situation changes when either of two things is also present:

  • A delta wave on the ECG. This means an accessory pathway exists. The concern is not the pathway itself but what happens if atrial fibrillation ever develops: the pathway can conduct the chaotic atrial signals to the ventricles far faster than the AV node would allow, and in rare cases that can trigger a dangerous ventricular rhythm. This is why WPW pattern — even without symptoms — is assessed by a cardiologist, who may recommend an exercise test or an electrophysiology study to judge how fast the pathway can conduct.
  • Episodes of sudden racing heart, fainting, or a family history of sudden cardiac death at a young age. These suggest the pathway is already causing rhythm problems (WPW syndrome, as opposed to the silent pattern).

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

Short PR interval symptoms: what to watch for

A short PR interval on its own causes no symptoms at all. It is an electrical timing measurement, not a condition you can feel.

What some people do feel is the rhythm problem that an accessory pathway can cause. If you have a short PR and any of the following, mention them to your doctor:

  • Palpitations that start and stop suddenly, like a switch — often described as the heart "taking off" for minutes to hours and then abruptly returning to normal
  • Lightheadedness or near-fainting during those episodes
  • Chest discomfort or breathlessness that comes only with the fast heartbeat
  • Fainting (syncope), especially during exertion

If you have never had any of these, a short PR is very unlikely to be causing problems.

Short PR interval vs WPW vs "short PR syndrome"

These three phrases get used interchangeably online, which causes a lot of unnecessary worry. They are not the same thing.

Phrase What it actually means Delta wave? Typical next step
Short PR interval A measurement: PR under 120 ms. Says nothing about the cause Not necessarily Check the QRS for a delta wave and ask about symptoms
WPW pattern Short PR and a delta wave on a routine ECG, in someone with no symptoms Yes Cardiology review; risk assessment of the pathway
WPW syndrome WPW pattern plus documented episodes of fast heart rhythm Yes Cardiology referral; often an electrophysiology study and ablation
"Short PR syndrome" / Lown-Ganong-Levine Short PR with a normal QRS and episodes of palpitations. An older term now regarded as a description, not a distinct disease No Holter monitor to capture the rhythm during symptoms

If your report says "short PR interval" and nothing about a delta wave or pre-excitation, you are in the first row. That is the common, usually harmless situation.

A worked example

Report line: "Sinus rhythm. Short PR interval (104 ms). Otherwise normal ECG." Patient: 29-year-old, ECG done before starting a new job. No palpitations, no fainting, no family history.

The doctor looked at the QRS in several leads: no slurred upstroke, QRS width 86 ms (normal). The P waves were upright in lead II, so this was a sinus beat, not a junctional one. The resting heart rate was 92 — slightly fast, as the patient was nervous.

Conclusion: normal fast AV conduction in an anxious young adult. No referral, no follow-up ECG needed. The patient was told to come back only if they ever developed sudden racing-heart episodes.

This is how the overwhelming majority of short PR findings resolve.

What usually happens next

  1. Your doctor examines the QRS for a delta wave and measures its width. This takes seconds and settles most cases.
  2. You are asked about symptoms — specifically sudden-onset palpitations, fainting, and family history of early sudden death.
  3. If there is a delta wave, you are referred to cardiology. Expect an echocardiogram (to check the heart's structure) and possibly an exercise test or an electrophysiology study to assess the pathway. Many people with WPW pattern are monitored only; those with symptoms or a high-risk pathway are offered catheter ablation, which is curative in the large majority.
  4. If there is no delta wave but you have palpitations, a Holter or event monitor is used to catch the rhythm during an episode.
  5. If there is no delta wave and no symptoms, nothing further is needed.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Is there a delta wave on my ECG, or is the QRS completely normal?
  • Was this a sinus beat or a junctional beat?
  • Do I need any further tests, or can this simply be noted?
  • What symptoms should prompt me to come back?

Frequently asked questions

Is a short PR interval dangerous?

On its own, with a normal QRS and no symptoms, no. It becomes significant only when there is also a delta wave (WPW pattern) or episodes of sudden racing heart, fainting, or a family history of early sudden cardiac death.

What causes a short PR interval?

Most often, normal fast conduction through the AV node, a faster heart rate, or a beat that started near the AV node (junctional rhythm). Less commonly, an accessory pathway bypassing the node (Wolff-Parkinson-White). Rarely, a metabolic or genetic condition.

What does a short PR interval mean on an ECG?

The PR interval measures how long the signal takes to travel from the atria to the ventricles. "Short" means under 120 ms — the signal arrived early. It is a measurement, not a diagnosis; the rest of the ECG shows why.

What is a normal PR interval?

120 to 200 milliseconds (3 to 5 small squares). Below 120 ms is short; above 200 ms is prolonged.

Can a short PR interval be normal?

Yes. A few percent of healthy people have a short PR with a completely normal QRS, and it has no consequences. It is also common at faster heart rates.

Does a short PR interval cause symptoms?

No. The short PR itself cannot be felt. Symptoms such as sudden palpitations or fainting come from a rhythm problem that an accessory pathway may cause — and most people with a short PR never experience them.

What is short PR syndrome?

An older name (Lown-Ganong-Levine syndrome) for a short PR interval with a normal QRS and episodes of fast heart rate. Most cardiologists now treat it as a description rather than a distinct disease.

Is a short PR interval the same as WPW?

No. WPW pattern requires a short PR and a delta wave. A short PR without a delta wave is not WPW.
