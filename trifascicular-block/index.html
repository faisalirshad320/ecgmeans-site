Trifascicular Block: What It Means on an ECG

Needs clinician review

Trifascicular block refers to disease affecting all three of the branches that carry the electrical signal to the ventricles. In practice the term is most often used for a bifascicular block (two branches) together with a delay in the third, shown as a long PR interval. It reflects widespread conduction-system disease and needs careful assessment.

What "trifascicular block" means

The ventricles are supplied by three conduction branches: the right bundle and the two fascicles of the left bundle. True simultaneous block of all three would stop conduction entirely (complete heart block). In everyday ECG reporting, "trifascicular block" usually means a bifascicular block — for example right bundle branch block with a fascicular block — plus evidence that the third pathway is also delayed, seen as a prolonged PR interval (first-degree AV block).

What it looks like on the ECG

The combined features of bifascicular block (an RBBB pattern with left or right axis deviation) plus a long PR interval. The exact site of the extra delay cannot always be known from the surface ECG alone, which is why the term is used cautiously.

Why it matters

Trifascicular block indicates that the conduction system is diseased in more than one place, leaving limited reserve. There is a risk — though often small and unpredictable from the ECG alone — of progression to complete heart block with a dangerously slow pulse. Whether action is needed depends heavily on symptoms.

Seek prompt care if it comes with fainting, near-fainting, a very slow pulse, chest pain or severe breathlessness.

Why the term is used loosely

True block of all three fascicles at once would stop conduction completely, so in practice "trifascicular block" is a convenient label for a bifascicular block plus a prolonged PR interval. The surface ECG cannot always pinpoint exactly where the extra delay lies, which is why the finding is interpreted with caution and judged mainly by whether it is causing symptoms such as blackouts.

What usually happens next

  1. Comparison with old ECGs and a careful symptom history, especially blackouts
  2. Monitoring to look for higher-grade block
  3. Sometimes an electrophysiology study to assess the conduction system
  4. A pacemaker if there are symptoms or evidence of advanced block

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Have I had any blackouts or near-faints?
  • Is my block stable or progressing?
  • Do I need monitoring or a pacemaker?

Frequently asked questions

Is trifascicular block an emergency?

Not usually by itself, but it signals widespread conduction disease and can progress to complete heart block, so symptoms such as fainting make it urgent.

What does trifascicular block look like on an ECG?

Most often a bifascicular block pattern (such as RBBB with a fascicular block) together with a prolonged PR interval.

Does trifascicular block need a pacemaker?

Not automatically. A pacemaker is considered when there are symptoms such as blackouts or when monitoring shows more advanced block.
