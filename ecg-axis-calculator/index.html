ECG Axis Calculator

The QRS axis is the average direction of electrical flow through the ventricles. Enter the net size of the QRS in leads I and aVF (upward deflection minus downward deflection, in small boxes or mm), and the calculator estimates the axis in degrees.

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBS · Medical DoctorPMDC 54440-SUpdated 2026-10-05

Net QRS = height of the upward deflection minus depth of the downward deflection. Use a minus sign if the QRS is mainly negative.

Quick answer

The normal QRS axis is −30° to +90°. If the QRS is mainly upright in both lead I and aVF, the axis is normal. Upright in I but negative in aVF suggests left axis deviation unless lead II is also upright (0° to −30°, still normal); negative in I but upright in aVF suggests right axis deviation.

The quadrant method

Lead I looks at the heart from the left (0°) and lead aVF looks from below (+90°). Whether the QRS is mainly positive or negative in each tells you which quarter of the circle the axis points into.

Lead I Lead aVF Axis
Positive Positive Normal (0° to +90°)
Positive Negative Left axis, or normal if lead II is positive (0° to −30°)
Negative Positive Right axis deviation (+90° to +180°)
Negative Negative Extreme axis (−90° to ±180°)

When lead I is positive and aVF is negative, check lead II. If lead II is mainly positive, the axis is between 0° and −30° and still normal. If lead II is mainly negative, it is left axis deviation.

How the calculator works

Because leads I and aVF sit at right angles, the net QRS in each acts like x and y coordinates. Augmented leads such as aVF record about √3/2 of the signal a limb lead would, so aVF is scaled up by 2/√3 first. The axis is then the angle of that point: axis = arctan((2/√3 × aVF) ÷ lead I), adjusted for the quarter it falls in. This is the same estimate clinicians make by eye, and it is accurate to within roughly 10–15°.

What the result means

  • Normal (−30° to +90°): the expected direction in most adults.
  • Left axis deviation (−30° to −90°): often due to left anterior fascicular block, and usually harmless on its own.
  • Right axis deviation (+90° to +180°): normal in children and some tall, thin adults, but can reflect strain on the right side of the heart.
  • Extreme axis (−90° to ±180°): uncommon. It can be caused by swapped arm electrodes, and otherwise needs a clinician's review.

Frequently asked questions

What is a normal ECG axis?

−30° to +90°. Some references use −30° to +100°.

How do you calculate the axis on an ECG?

Look at leads I and aVF. Mainly positive in both means a normal axis. For a number, use the net QRS in each lead as x and y and take the angle.

Is left axis deviation serious?

Usually not on its own. It is most often due to a minor conduction pattern called left anterior fascicular block, but it is worth a review alongside the rest of the ECG.
