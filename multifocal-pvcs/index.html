Multifocal PVCs: What They Mean on Your ECG

Needs clinician review

A PVC — an early beat from the lower chambers — is one of the most common things an ECG picks up, and in most people it is harmless. When the report adds the word "multifocal" (or "multiform"), it is describing something a little more specific: the extra beats are not all coming from the same place. That detail does not automatically mean danger, but it shifts the question doctors ask from "Is this normal?" to "Is something irritating the heart muscle in more than one area?" This page explains what multifocal PVCs look like, why they happen, and what checks usually follow.

Quick answer

Multifocal PVCs are premature ventricular contractions that come in two or more different shapes on the ECG, meaning they start from more than one spot in the ventricles. Unifocal PVCs all look the same. Multifocal PVCs can occur in healthy people, but they are more often linked to heart disease, low potassium or magnesium, or medicines such as digoxin, so they usually deserve a clinician's review.

What are multifocal PVCs?

A premature ventricular contraction (PVC) is a heartbeat that fires early from the ventricles instead of following the normal signal from the sinus node. Because it spreads through the heart muscle by a slower, unusual route, it produces a wide, oddly shaped QRS complex on the ECG. Our page on premature ventricular contractions covers PVCs in general.

The shape of a PVC depends on where it starts. Every PVC from the same spot (focus) looks the same. So:

  • Unifocal PVCs — all the PVCs on the tracing have the same shape. They come from one focus.
  • Multifocal PVCs — the PVCs have two or more different shapes. They come from two or more different foci in the ventricles.

You may also see the terms multiform or polymorphic PVCs. Strictly, "multiform" only describes the different shapes, because one focus can occasionally produce slightly different-looking beats if they travel by different routes. In everyday reports the terms are used almost interchangeably.

What multifocal PVCs look like on an ECG

Feature Unifocal PVCs Multifocal PVCs
QRS shape All PVCs identical Two or more different PVC shapes
Origin One spot in the ventricles Two or more spots
Coupling interval (time from the normal beat to the PVC) Usually fixed Often varies between the different PVC types
Typical setting Often healthy hearts; common outflow-tract PVCs More often heart disease, electrolyte or drug effects — but also seen in healthy people
Usual next step Reassurance or Holter if frequent Look for an underlying cause

On the strip, each PVC:

  • Arrives early, before the next expected normal beat
  • Has a wide QRS (usually 120 ms or more) with no P wave in front of it
  • Has a T wave pointing the opposite way to the QRS
  • Is usually followed by a full compensatory pause — the gap before the next normal beat makes up for the early beat

In multifocal PVCs, you will see this pattern with at least two distinct QRS shapes — for example one PVC that points upward in lead V1 and another that points downward.

Multifocal PVCs in patterns

Multifocal PVCs can appear on their own or in patterns, which reports describe separately:

  • Bigeminy — every other beat is a PVC
  • Trigeminy — every third beat is a PVC
  • Ventricular couplet — two PVCs in a row
  • Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia — three or more PVCs in a row

When multifocal PVCs come in runs or pairs, doctors look more closely, because that combination suggests more widespread irritability of the heart muscle.

Are multifocal PVCs dangerous?

Usually not on their own — but they are taken more seriously than occasional single-shape PVCs.

Older teaching ranked multifocal PVCs as a "warning arrhythmia" that could herald ventricular fibrillation, particularly in the setting of a heart attack. Modern practice no longer treats them as a direct predictor of sudden death. Instead, they are seen as a marker: they suggest that something may be affecting the heart muscle in more than one place. What matters is finding out whether that "something" exists.

Factors that make multifocal PVCs more concerning:

  • Symptoms such as fainting, chest pain or breathlessness
  • Known heart disease — a previous heart attack, heart failure or cardiomyopathy
  • A high PVC burden — many thousands a day on a Holter monitor
  • Runs or couplets, especially if fast
  • PVCs landing on the T wave of the previous beat (see R-on-T phenomenon)
  • Appearance during a heart attack or right after one
  • A family history of sudden death or inherited heart rhythm conditions

In a young, fit person with no symptoms, a normal echocardiogram and only occasional PVCs, multifocal PVCs are generally benign.

Get urgent help: call 911 (999 in the UK, 112 in Europe) if extra beats come with chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness or a sustained racing heartbeat. These symptoms need same-day assessment regardless of the ECG wording.

What causes multifocal PVCs?

Cause Why it matters Clues
Coronary artery disease / heart attack Damaged or starved muscle in several areas can fire early Chest pain, risk factors, Q waves or ST changes on ECG
Cardiomyopathy or heart failure Stretched and scarred muscle is electrically unstable Breathlessness, swelling, reduced ejection fraction on echo
Low potassium or low magnesium Makes heart cells more excitable Diuretics, vomiting, diarrhea; see hypokalemia
Digoxin toxicity Classically causes multifocal PVCs and bigeminy Nausea, visual changes, kidney problems; see digoxin effect
Low oxygen Lung disease, sleep apnea, acute illness Breathlessness, COPD, low oxygen saturation
Stimulants Caffeine, nicotine, alcohol, decongestants, cocaine, amphetamines Timing with use
Thyroid overactivity Speeds and excites the heart Weight loss, tremor, heat intolerance
Valve disease, myocarditis, sarcoidosis Inflammation or strain in the muscle Murmur, recent viral illness, other organ involvement
No cause found (idiopathic) Common in otherwise healthy people Normal tests

Symptoms

Many people feel nothing. Others notice:

  • A skipped or "missed" beat — the pause after a PVC
  • A thump or flip-flop in the chest — the strong beat after the pause
  • Fluttering or palpitations, especially at rest or lying down
  • Tiredness or breathlessness if PVCs are very frequent

Our page on palpitations with a normal ECG covers what to do when you feel skipped beats but the tracing looks fine.

A worked example

An illustrative case built from typical features — not a real patient.

Report line: "Sinus rhythm with multifocal PVCs. Otherwise normal ECG." Patient: 58-year-old man with high blood pressure, taking a thiazide diuretic, who noticed skipped beats for two weeks.

His doctor ordered blood tests, which showed a potassium of 3.2 mmol/L (slightly low) and low-normal magnesium. A 24-hour Holter found PVCs of two shapes making up about 2% of all beats, with no runs. An echocardiogram showed mildly thickened heart muscle from high blood pressure but normal pumping.

What happened: his blood pressure medicine was adjusted, potassium was replaced and he cut back from five coffees a day. A repeat Holter three months later showed far fewer PVCs, and his symptoms settled.

The lesson: multifocal PVCs prompted a search for causes, and in this case several small, fixable factors were found. That is a common outcome. A different result — for example a weak heart muscle on the echo — would have led to a cardiology referral and treatment for the underlying condition.

Multifocal PVCs vs other irregular rhythms

Several rhythms can look like "irregular extra beats" on a monitor or smartwatch:

Finding What it is Key difference
Multifocal PVCs Early wide beats of several shapes from the ventricles Underlying rhythm is regular sinus rhythm between the extra beats
Multifocal atrial tachycardia Fast rhythm with 3+ different P wave shapes from the atria Narrow QRS; rate over 100; often lung disease
Atrial fibrillation Chaotic atrial activity, irregularly irregular rhythm No P waves; narrow QRS unless there is a conduction problem
PACs with aberrant conduction Early beats from the atria that conduct with a wide QRS A premature P wave precedes the wide beat

If the extra beats are narrow and preceded by a P wave, they are more likely atrial — see PVC vs PAC and multifocal atrial tachycardia.

Tests your doctor may order

  1. Blood tests — potassium, magnesium, calcium, thyroid function, kidney function, and digoxin level if you take it
  2. 12-lead ECG — to compare PVC shapes and look for signs of past heart attack, thickened muscle or long QT
  3. Holter monitor (24–48 hours or longer) — to count PVCs, measure the PVC burden (the percentage of all beats that are PVCs) and look for runs
  4. Echocardiogram — to check heart muscle strength, valves and size; this is usually the most important test for multifocal PVCs
  5. Exercise stress test — to see whether PVCs increase with effort and to look for blocked arteries
  6. Cardiac MRI — sometimes, to look for scarring or inflammation when the echo is unclear

A high PVC burden — often quoted as over about 10–15% of all beats — can weaken the heart over time (PVC-induced cardiomyopathy), so the Holter count matters.

Treatment

Treatment targets the cause rather than the PVCs themselves:

  • Correcting electrolytes and reviewing medicines (including digoxin)
  • Treating heart disease — for example blood flow to the heart, heart failure medicines, blood pressure control
  • Reducing triggers — caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, poor sleep, untreated sleep apnea
  • Beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers for bothersome symptoms
  • Other antiarrhythmic medicines in selected cases, prescribed by a cardiologist
  • Catheter ablation — very effective for a single dominant PVC focus, but less straightforward when PVCs come from several sites

What usually happens next

For most people, the next steps are a blood test, a Holter monitor and an echocardiogram. If those are normal and you feel well, reassurance and lifestyle changes are usually enough. If heart disease is found, treatment focuses on that condition, and the PVCs often settle as it improves.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • How many different PVC shapes were seen, and how often did they occur?
  • Were there any pairs or runs?
  • Could my medicines or electrolyte levels be causing this?
  • Do I need an echocardiogram and a Holter monitor?
  • What PVC burden would worry you?

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between unifocal and multifocal PVCs?

Unifocal PVCs all have the same shape and come from one spot in the ventricles. Multifocal PVCs have two or more shapes and come from different spots.

Are multifocal PVCs worse than unifocal PVCs?

They are more often linked to heart disease, electrolyte problems or drug effects, so they usually lead to more testing. In a healthy heart they can still be harmless.

Can multifocal PVCs be benign?

Yes. If tests show a normal heart, normal electrolytes and a low PVC burden, multifocal PVCs are usually benign.

Is multiform the same as multifocal?

The terms are often used interchangeably. Strictly, multiform describes different shapes, while multifocal means the beats come from different places.

Can anxiety cause multifocal PVCs?

Stress and adrenaline can increase PVCs of any type. Anxiety alone is not usually assumed to explain multifocal PVCs until other causes are checked.

Do multifocal PVCs need treatment?

Treatment focuses on any underlying cause. Medicines or ablation are used only if PVCs cause symptoms, are very frequent or affect heart function.

Can digoxin cause multifocal PVCs?

Yes. Multifocal PVCs and bigeminy are classic signs of digoxin toxicity, which is why digoxin levels are often checked.
