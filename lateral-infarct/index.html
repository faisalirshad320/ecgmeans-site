Lateral Infarct: What It Means on an ECG

Needs clinician review

A "lateral infarct" on an ECG report means the software saw a pattern — usually Q waves — in the leads that look at the side wall of the heart (I, aVL, V5 and V6), which a past heart attack can leave. As with the other "age undetermined" infarct labels, it is a suggestion that needs a clinician's review and often an echocardiogram, because lead factors and normal variants can mimic it.

What "Lateral infarct" means

The side (lateral) wall of the left ventricle is viewed by leads I and aVL (high lateral) and V5–V6 (low lateral), and is supplied by branches of the left-sided coronary arteries. When muscle there has been damaged by a heart attack, Q waves or lost R waves appear in these leads. The report may say lateral infarct, high lateral infarct, or combine territories (for example anterolateral).

"Age undetermined" means the machine saw a fixed pattern that could be old, not the ST changes of an attack in progress. Small lateral Q waves can also be a normal septal variant, so the finding is confirmed rather than assumed.

What it looks like on the ECG

Q waves, or small/absent R waves, in leads I, aVL, V5 and V6, sometimes with T wave changes in the same leads. A genuine old infarct usually involves more than one of these neighbouring leads.

Common causes

  • A genuine previous lateral heart attack
  • Normal septal Q waves (a common mimic)
  • Left ventricular hypertrophy
  • Left bundle branch block
  • Lead placement and body build

Should you worry?

Needs clinician review

A real lateral infarct reflects previous heart muscle damage, so the line should be reviewed by a clinician rather than ignored. But because normal variants and lead factors can produce the same picture, you should not assume you have had a heart attack from the ECG alone. Comparison with old ECGs, your risk factors and an echocardiogram decide it.

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

What usually happens next

  1. Clinician over-read and comparison with previous ECGs
  2. An echocardiogram to look at the side wall's movement
  3. Risk-factor assessment and treatment
  4. Further cardiac testing if a genuine infarct is likely

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Is the change in more than one lateral lead?
  • Is this new compared with my old ECGs?
  • Should I have an echocardiogram?

Frequently asked questions

Is a lateral infarct serious?

A real one reflects previous heart damage and matters, but the ECG label alone is only a suspicion that needs confirming.

Can a lateral infarct on ECG be a false alarm?

Yes. Normal septal Q waves, a thick ventricle and lead placement can all mimic it.

How is it confirmed?

With an echocardiogram and by comparing the ECG with earlier tracings.
