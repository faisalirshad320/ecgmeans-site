Hyperkalemia on an ECG: What the Changes Mean

Seek prompt care

Hyperkalemia means a high level of potassium in your blood. An ECG cannot measure potassium directly, but certain patterns strongly suggest it and can show up before blood results are back. The key point is that hyperkalemia with ECG changes is treated as a medical emergency, so the blood level is checked urgently and treatment does not wait.

Quick answer

Hyperkalemia on an ECG means the tracing shows changes that suggest a high blood potassium level. The usual sequence is tall, peaked T waves, then a longer PR interval with flattening P waves, then a widening QRS, and finally a "sine wave" pattern that can precede cardiac arrest. The ECG cannot measure potassium, so an urgent blood test confirms it, and ECG changes are treated as an emergency.

A hyperkalemia ECG is one of the few tracings that can point to a blood chemistry problem before the lab result is back. This page walks through the hyperkalemia ECG changes in the order they usually appear, explains the sine wave ECG, gives a guide table by potassium level, and explains why a normal-looking ECG does not rule high potassium out.

What "hyperkalemia" means

Hyperkalemia (spelled hyperkalaemia in UK reports) means a high blood potassium level. Potassium is a mineral the heart depends on to keep its electrical signals steady. Normal potassium is about 3.5-5.0 mmol/L.

When potassium rises, it changes the ECG in a fairly predictable order. An ECG cannot measure potassium. It only records the heart's electrical activity. But certain patterns strongly suggest a high level. That is why a report may say "hyperkalemia" or "consider hyperkalemia" even before any blood test results are available.

If you see this phrase on your report, it is usually a prompt for your care team to check your potassium with a blood test straight away.

Why high potassium affects the heart

Heart muscle cells rely on a careful balance of potassium inside and outside the cell to "reset" after each beat. When blood potassium rises, that balance shifts. At first the heart resets faster, which makes the T wave tall and sharp. As potassium climbs further, the cells become harder to fire and signals travel more slowly — so the P wave fades, the PR interval lengthens and the QRS widens. Eventually the electrical system can fail altogether.

Hyperkalemia ECG changes: what it looks like

The changes tend to appear in stages as potassium climbs:

  • First: peaked T waves. The T wave is the part of each heartbeat that shows the heart muscle resetting. With high potassium, it becomes tall, narrow and pointed, often described as "peaked" or "tented." These often appear above about 5.5-6.5 mmol/L.
  • Next: changes to the P wave and PR interval. The PR interval becomes longer, and the P waves (which reflect the upper chambers) flatten and can disappear.
  • Then: a wider QRS. The QRS complex, the main spike of each beat, becomes progressively wider.
  • Severe hyperkalemia: a "sine-wave" pattern. The QRS and T wave blend together into a smooth, wave-like shape. This can come before cardiac arrest. QRS widening and the sine wave tend to appear at higher levels, often above 7-8 mmol/L.

These levels are only a guide. Individuals vary, and some people have quite high potassium with few ECG changes, while others show changes at lower levels.

Hyperkalemia ECG progression by potassium level

The table below shows the classic textbook order. Treat the potassium figures as rough guides only.

Approximate potassium (mmol/L) Typical ECG changes What it signals
3.5–5.0 Normal ECG Normal potassium
About 5.5–6.5 Tall, narrow, peaked ("tented") T waves Early effect on the heart's reset phase
About 6.5–7.5 Longer PR interval; flatter, wider P waves; P waves may vanish Slower conduction through the atria and AV node
About 7–8 and above Widening QRS; slow heart rhythms; heart blocks; bundle branch block patterns Conduction through the ventricles is failing
Very high (often above 8–9) Sine wave — QRS and T merge into a smooth wave Pre-arrest rhythm
Any level when severe Ventricular fibrillation, asystole, or pulseless electrical activity Cardiac arrest

Important caveat: the ECG correlates poorly with the actual potassium level. Studies of patients with confirmed hyperkalemia have found that many have none of the classic changes, and some people have dangerously high potassium with a near-normal tracing until shortly before an arrest. Others, particularly people on long-term dialysis, tolerate high levels with few changes. That is why treatment decisions are based on the blood level, the clinical picture and how fast the potassium is rising — not on the ECG alone. A normal ECG never rules hyperkalemia out.

Other ECG signs of high potassium

Beyond the classic sequence, hyperkalemia can also cause:

  • Slow heart rhythms — sinus bradycardia, slow junctional rhythms, or slow atrial fibrillation
  • Heart block — first degree up to complete heart block
  • Bundle branch and fascicular blocks, which may disappear once potassium is corrected
  • Wide, bizarre complexes that can look like ventricular tachycardia

Doctors are taught to suspect hyperkalemia whenever a new slow rhythm or heart block appears in someone with kidney disease, on dialysis, or taking medicines that raise potassium.

Sine wave ECG

The sine wave ECG is the most severe hyperkalemia pattern. The P waves have gone, the QRS is so wide that it runs straight into the T wave, and each beat looks like a smooth, rolling wave — like the sine curve from school maths. The rate is often slow.

A sine wave pattern means the heart's electrical system is close to failing. It is a pre-arrest rhythm, and in hospital it is treated immediately — often with intravenous calcium given before the blood result is back. Although hyperkalemia is by far the most common cause, a similar pattern can occasionally be seen with severe poisoning by sodium-channel-blocking drugs, so the clinical context matters.

Peaked T waves: hyperkalemia or something else?

Tall T waves are not always caused by potassium. The table shows the main look-alikes.

Finding Typical T wave shape Key clue
Hyperkalemia Tall, narrow, pointed, symmetrical, with a narrow base Kidney disease or potassium-raising medicines; may have wide QRS or flat P waves
Hyperacute T waves (early heart attack) Tall but broad-based, in one region of the heart Chest pain; changes limited to one group of leads
Early repolarization Tall with a notched or slurred J point Young, healthy person; stable over time
Normal variant Tall T waves in V2–V4 No symptoms, normal potassium

Common causes

  • Kidney failure. This is the most common cause, because the kidneys normally remove extra potassium.
  • Medicines. These include ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone and potassium supplements.
  • Acidosis. This includes diabetic ketoacidosis.
  • Major tissue breakdown. Examples are crush injury or hemolysis (breakdown of red blood cells).
  • A falsely high reading (pseudohyperkalemia). A difficult blood draw can make the potassium look higher than it really is.

The last cause is one reason doctors confirm the result carefully. A worrying ECG pattern together with a high blood level carries more weight than a single number on its own.

Other causes include missed dialysis sessions, salt substitutes that contain potassium, adrenal problems such as Addison's disease, and severe muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis).

Symptoms of high potassium

Mild hyperkalemia often causes no symptoms at all, which is why it is commonly found on routine blood tests. As levels rise, people may notice:

  • Muscle weakness or heaviness, sometimes severe
  • Tingling or numbness
  • Nausea
  • Palpitations or an irregular heartbeat
  • Breathlessness or chest discomfort
  • Fainting

Should you worry?

Hyperkalemia with ECG changes is a medical emergency, because high potassium can cause dangerous heart rhythms and cardiac arrest. ECG changes are a sign that the heart is already being affected, so treatment should not wait. The good news is that this is a well-recognised problem with established, effective treatments, and your team will act quickly once it is suspected.

Get urgent help: if your report mentions hyperkalemia and you are not already being looked after by a medical team, contact emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department now. Do this especially if you feel unwell, weak, or notice palpitations.

What usually happens next

Your blood potassium will be checked urgently to confirm the level. Depending on the result and your ECG, treatment can include:

  • Calcium, given to protect the heart from the effects of high potassium.
  • Insulin with glucose, and albuterol (salbutamol), which help drive potassium from the blood into the body's cells.
  • Measures to remove potassium from the body.

Alongside this, your doctors will look for the underlying cause. They may check your kidney function, review any medicines that can raise potassium, and look for conditions such as acidosis. Any culprit drugs are addressed, and the cause is treated so the problem is less likely to come back. Repeat blood tests and ECGs are often used to check that treatment is working.

Measures that remove potassium from the body include potassium-binding medicines taken by mouth, diuretics in people whose kidneys still work, and dialysis when kidney function is poor or the level is very high. Calcium works within minutes but does not lower potassium — it buys time while the other treatments take effect, and the ECG changes often improve quickly once it is given.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What was my potassium level, and was the sample checked for a false high reading?
  • Which of my medicines could be raising my potassium?
  • How is my kidney function?
  • Do I need to change my diet or avoid salt substitutes?
  • How often should my potassium be rechecked?

Frequently asked questions

What does hyperkalemia on my ECG mean?

It means the ECG shows patterns that strongly suggest a high blood potassium level. The ECG cannot measure potassium itself, so a blood test is needed to confirm it. These patterns can appear before blood results are back, which helps doctors act quickly.

Is hyperkalemia dangerous?

It can be. Hyperkalemia with ECG changes is treated as a medical emergency because it can cause dangerous heart rhythms and cardiac arrest. ECG changes signal that the heart is being affected, so treatment should start promptly.

What causes high potassium?

Kidney failure is the most common cause. Other causes include medicines such as ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone and potassium supplements, as well as acidosis (including diabetic ketoacidosis) and major tissue breakdown such as crush injury or hemolysis. Sometimes a difficult blood draw gives a falsely high reading, called pseudohyperkalemia.

What potassium level causes ECG changes?

Normal potassium is about 3.5-5.0 mmol/L. Peaked T waves often appear above about 5.5-6.5 mmol/L, while QRS widening and the sine-wave pattern tend to appear at higher levels, often above 7-8 mmol/L. Individuals vary, so these figures are only a guide.

What happens next, and can it be prevented from coming back?

Your blood potassium is checked urgently. Treatment can include calcium to protect the heart, insulin with glucose and albuterol (salbutamol) to move potassium into cells, and measures to remove it from the body. Your doctors will also treat the underlying cause and review any medicines that may be raising your potassium, which helps reduce the chance of it happening again.

Can you have high potassium with a normal ECG?

Yes. The ECG is an insensitive test for hyperkalemia, and many people with a high level have a normal or near-normal tracing. The blood test is what confirms or rules it out.

What is the first ECG sign of hyperkalemia?

Usually tall, narrow, peaked T waves, best seen in the chest leads.

What does a sine wave on an ECG mean?

It means the QRS and T wave have merged into a smooth, rolling wave. It is most often caused by severe hyperkalemia and is a pre-arrest rhythm that needs immediate treatment.
