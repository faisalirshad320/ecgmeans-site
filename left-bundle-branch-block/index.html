LBBB on an ECG: What Left Bundle Branch Block Means

Left bundle branch block (LBBB) means the electrical impulse cannot travel down the left branch of the heart's wiring, so the left ventricle is activated late and indirectly. On the ECG the QRS becomes wide (120 ms or more) with a characteristic shape. Unlike its right-sided counterpart, LBBB is usually associated with underlying heart disease and prompts an echocardiogram.

What "Left bundle branch block" means

An LBBB ECG reflects a wiring delay, not a blocked artery. Below the AV node the conduction system splits into a right and a left bundle. When the left one is blocked, the right ventricle fires first and the impulse then spreads slowly through muscle to the left. The delay widens the QRS and also changes the ST segments and T waves, which is why many other ECG findings cannot be read reliably in the presence of LBBB.

A QRS of 110–119 ms with the same shape is called incomplete LBBB. New LBBB during chest pain is treated as a possible heart attack until proven otherwise.

The left bundle is a thicker cable than the right, and it divides into two smaller branches called fascicles — the left anterior fascicle and the left posterior fascicle. Because the left bundle is broad, it usually takes a fairly widespread problem in the heart muscle or conduction system to block it completely. That is the main reason LBBB is more often linked to heart disease than right bundle branch block.

ECG and EKG mean the same test, so "LBBB EKG" and "LBBB ECG" describe the same finding.

What it looks like on the ECG

A wide QRS (≥120 ms), broad notched R waves in leads I, aVL, V5 and V6, deep S waves in V1–V2, and T waves that point the opposite way to the QRS.

LBBB ECG criteria at a glance

Feature What to look for Why it happens
QRS duration 120 ms (3 small boxes) or more The left ventricle is activated slowly, muscle cell to muscle cell
Leads V5–V6, I, aVL Broad, often notched or slurred R wave ("M"-shaped or flat-topped); no small Q wave The septum is activated from right to left, the reverse of normal
Leads V1–V2 Deep, broad S wave (QS or rS pattern) Electrical activity moves away from the right-sided chest leads
ST segment and T wave Point the opposite way to the main QRS deflection ("appropriate discordance") Abnormal activation leads to abnormal recovery
Axis Often normal or leftward Depends on the underlying disease

A simple memory aid used by students is "WiLLiaM": a W-like shape in V1 and an M-like shape in V6. Real tracings do not always look this neat, so the measurements above are more reliable than the shape alone.

Incomplete LBBB and nonspecific conduction delay

When the QRS has the LBBB shape but measures 110–119 ms, the report may say incomplete left bundle branch block. A wide QRS that does not fit either bundle branch pattern is called an intraventricular conduction delay. For a broader overview of why the QRS widens, see QRS interval and wide QRS complex.

LBBB vs RBBB

LBBB RBBB
Lead V1 Deep, broad S wave (mostly negative) rSR' — "rabbit ears" (mostly positive)
Lead V6 Broad, notched R wave Wide, slurred S wave
Link to heart disease Common Often seen in healthy people
Usual next step Echocardiogram Often none if you are well

Common causes

  • High blood pressure with left ventricular thickening
  • Coronary artery disease and previous heart attack
  • Dilated cardiomyopathy
  • Aortic valve disease
  • Age-related degeneration of the conduction system
  • Occasionally no cause is found

LBBB can also appear after procedures on the aortic valve, such as valve replacement (including TAVR), because the left bundle runs very close to the valve. Some people develop it only when the heart rate rises — a rate-related LBBB — which disappears when the heart slows.

Is LBBB serious? LBBB and heart attack

LBBB matters in two separate ways.

First, as a clue to underlying heart disease. It is often the first sign of high blood pressure damage, a weakened heart muscle or valve disease, which is why an echocardiogram is the standard next step.

Second, because it hides heart attacks. LBBB already changes the ST segments, so the usual ST-elevation rules for diagnosing a heart attack do not work well. Emergency teams use special rules — the Sgarbossa criteria and their modified versions — to look for a heart attack hidden behind LBBB, alongside symptoms, troponin blood tests and comparison with older ECGs. Current guidance places more weight on these features and on the person's condition than on the LBBB alone. See STEMI vs NSTEMI for how heart attacks are classified.

Should you worry?

LBBB should always be explained. Some people have it for years with a structurally normal heart and do well, but it is more often a marker of an underlying condition and slightly raises the long-term risk of heart failure and higher-grade block. New LBBB with symptoms is an emergency.

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

Left bundle branch block life expectancy

This is one of the most searched questions about LBBB, and the honest answer is that life expectancy depends far more on the heart behind the LBBB than on the LBBB itself. There is no single number that applies to everyone.

Situation What the evidence generally shows
Isolated LBBB with a normal echocardiogram and no coronary disease Long-term follow-up studies have found survival broadly similar to people of the same age without LBBB. A small number later develop a weakened heart muscle, so periodic review is sensible
LBBB with high blood pressure or coronary artery disease Outlook is mainly set by how well these conditions are controlled
LBBB with heart failure or a reduced pumping function LBBB is associated with a worse outlook than heart failure without it, partly because the two sides of the heart squeeze out of step. This is also the group that benefits most from cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT)
LBBB that progresses to higher-grade heart block Treated with a pacemaker, after which the outlook again depends on the underlying heart

Several points are worth knowing:

  • LBBB is a marker, not a disease. It tells your doctor to look carefully at the heart muscle, valves and arteries.
  • The echocardiogram result is the key piece of information. A normal echo is reassuring.
  • Treatment can change the outlook. Controlling blood pressure, treating coronary disease, heart-failure medicines and, where appropriate, CRT all improve symptoms and survival in the groups who need them.
  • Your own outlook is a conversation for your cardiologist, who can weigh your age, echo, symptoms and other conditions.

Symptoms and when LBBB is found

LBBB does not cause symptoms by itself. It is most often found by chance on a routine ECG, a pre-operative check or an insurance medical. When symptoms are present, they usually come from the underlying condition:

  • breathlessness, ankle swelling or tiredness — possible heart failure or valve disease
  • chest pain or pressure on exertion — possible coronary artery disease
  • dizziness or fainting — possible progression to a higher-grade heart block with slow heart rates

If LBBB is found during a pre-operative check, the anesthetist will usually want to know whether it is old or new and whether an echocardiogram has been done. Many people with known, stable LBBB go through surgery without problems.

LBBB treatment and CRT

LBBB itself is not treated; the cause is. In people with heart failure, a reduced ejection fraction and a wide LBBB, a CRT device (a "biventricular pacemaker") can re-synchronize the two ventricles. Guidelines favor CRT most strongly when the QRS is very wide, around 150 ms or more, with a typical LBBB shape. In some people with a structurally normal heart, a pacemaker is needed only if a further block develops and causes slow heart rates.

What usually happens next

  1. Echocardiogram to assess pumping function and valves
  2. Blood pressure review and cholesterol assessment
  3. Stress imaging or angiography if coronary disease is suspected
  4. In heart failure, LBBB is a reason to consider a special pacemaker (CRT)

If you have LBBB, keep a copy of your ECG. If you ever go to an emergency department with chest pain, having an older tracing to compare against can speed up decisions. Our guide to reading an ECG report explains the other measurements printed alongside it.

Frequently asked questions

Is left bundle branch block serious?

It is more significant than right bundle branch block and usually leads to an echocardiogram, but many people with LBBB and a normal echo do well.

Can LBBB be reversed?

Rarely. It can occasionally appear only at fast heart rates (rate-related LBBB) and disappear when the heart slows.

Does LBBB need a pacemaker?

Not on its own. A pacemaker or CRT device is considered if there is heart failure or higher-grade block.

Can you exercise with left bundle branch block?

Most people with LBBB and a normal echocardiogram can stay active. Your doctor may suggest a stress test first, and exercise advice depends on any underlying heart condition.

Does LBBB cause symptoms?

LBBB itself usually causes no symptoms. Symptoms such as breathlessness or tiredness come from the underlying condition, such as a weakened heart muscle.

Can an ECG show a heart attack if you have LBBB?

It is harder, because LBBB already alters the ST segments. Doctors use the Sgarbossa criteria, troponin blood tests and comparison with older ECGs to look for one.
