AV Dissociation: What It Means on an ECG

Needs clinician review

AV dissociation means the atria and the ventricles are beating independently of each other, each driven by its own pacemaker, rather than in the normal coordinated way. It is a description rather than a single diagnosis — it can be benign or a sign of a serious conduction problem — so the cause needs to be identified.

What "AV dissociation" means

Normally the atria fire first (the P wave) and the signal passes to the ventricles (the QRS) in step. In AV dissociation the two are out of step: the atria follow one pacemaker and the ventricles another, so P waves and QRS complexes march to different drummers. It is not a diagnosis in itself but a pattern with several possible causes.

What it looks like on the ECG

P waves and QRS complexes occur at their own independent rates, so the PR interval constantly changes and P waves drift in and out of the QRS complexes. Depending on the cause, the ventricular rate may be slower than, similar to, or faster than the atrial rate.

Common causes

  • A slow sinus rate letting a junctional or ventricular back-up pacemaker take over
  • An accelerated lower pacemaker overtaking the sinus node
  • Complete (third-degree) heart block, where the signal cannot pass at all
  • Ventricular tachycardia
  • Certain medicines and times of high vagal tone

Why it matters

The importance depends entirely on the cause. Brief AV dissociation from high vagal tone in a young person is benign, whereas AV dissociation from complete heart block or ventricular tachycardia is serious and needs urgent treatment. That is why the underlying mechanism must be worked out.

Seek prompt care if AV dissociation comes with fainting, a very slow or very fast pulse, chest pain or severe breathlessness.

A common benign form

A common and usually benign type is "isorhythmic" AV dissociation, where the atria and ventricles happen to beat at almost the same rate, so P waves drift slowly in and out of the QRS complexes. This often occurs with a slightly slow sinus rate and a junctional back-up pacemaker, is frequently seen in young people with high vagal tone, and typically needs no treatment once a more serious cause has been excluded.

What usually happens next

  1. Careful analysis of the P waves and QRS rates to find the mechanism
  2. Comparison with old ECGs and review of medicines
  3. Monitoring to capture the rhythm over time
  4. Treatment of the specific cause — which may range from observation to a pacemaker or arrhythmia treatment

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What is causing my atria and ventricles to beat separately?
  • Is this a benign type or a type that needs treatment?
  • Do I need a pacemaker or rhythm treatment?

Frequently asked questions

Is AV dissociation the same as heart block?

Not always. Complete heart block is one cause of AV dissociation, but AV dissociation can also occur without block, for example when a lower pacemaker speeds up or the sinus node slows down.

Is AV dissociation dangerous?

It depends on the cause — some forms are benign, while others, such as those from complete heart block or ventricular tachycardia, are serious.

How is the cause found?

By analysing the relationship and rates of the P waves and QRS complexes, comparing old ECGs, and often using continuous monitoring.
