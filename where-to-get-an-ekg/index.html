Where to Get an EKG and How Much It Costs

An EKG is one of the quickest and cheapest heart tests there is, but finding where to get one, and what you will pay, is not always obvious. Prices vary a lot between a doctor's office, an urgent care center, a hospital outpatient department and an emergency room. This page covers where you can get an EKG, whether urgent care does them, what it typically costs with and without insurance, how long it takes, what to wear, and when you should skip all of that and call 911.

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBS · Medical DoctorPMDC 54440-SUpdated 2026-10-26
Quick answer

How much an EKG costs in the US depends on where you get it. A standard 12-lead EKG often costs about $40 to $150 self-pay at clinics and hospitals; urgent care adds a visit fee. With insurance you usually pay a copay or coinsurance. Medicare Part B covers medically needed EKGs at 20% after the deductible. Prices vary widely.

First: when not to shop around for an EKG

Get urgent help: If you have chest pain or pressure, pain spreading to your arm, jaw, neck or back, shortness of breath, a cold sweat, fainting, or a racing heartbeat that will not settle, call 911 (999 in the UK, 112 in Europe). Do not drive to urgent care for an EKG. Paramedics can record an EKG on the way and alert the hospital, and urgent care centers usually cannot provide heart attack treatment. They will send you to the emergency room anyway, losing time.

Everything else on this page is for non-emergency EKGs: routine checks, pre-surgery tests, follow-ups, or mild symptoms your doctor wants to look into.

Where can I get an EKG?

An EKG (also called an ECG; they are the same test) needs a 12-lead machine, a trained person to place the stickers, and a clinician to read the result. Here are the usual options in the US. If you are not sure whether you need one, see what is an EKG? and what can an EKG detect?

Does urgent care do EKGs?

Yes, many urgent care centers can do a 12-lead EKG, often as a walk-in. They are a reasonable choice for non-urgent reasons: an EKG needed before surgery or for a job, palpitations that have settled, or a follow-up your doctor asked for. Call ahead, because not every center has the equipment or offers it at all hours.

Urgent care is not the right place for chest pain that could be a heart attack. If the EKG shows a heart attack, the center will call an ambulance to take you to an emergency room, which delays treatment.

What about smartwatch EKGs?

Some smartwatches and handheld devices record a single-lead EKG. They are useful for capturing a rhythm during heart palpitations, such as atrial fibrillation, while symptoms are happening. But one lead is not twelve. A smartwatch cannot detect most heart attacks, cannot measure all intervals reliably, and can mislabel normal rhythms. Treat it as a helpful clue to show your doctor, not as a full test.

How much does an EKG cost?

Prices vary by state, facility and insurance plan, so treat these as rough guides and always ask for a quote.

Some background on these figures:

  • Hospital price-transparency data compiled by CarePriceGuide puts the typical cash price for a 12-lead EKG with interpretation (CPT 93000) at roughly $110, with most listed prices between about $43 and $154. Negotiated insurer rates listed there mostly fall between about $55 and $100.
  • As one example of an urgent care price, WellNow Urgent Care lists a self-pay EKG visit at $175, which covers the visit, test and interpretation.
  • Medicare covers a one-time screening EKG if your doctor refers you during the "Welcome to Medicare" preventive visit, and covers diagnostic EKGs when they are medically needed.

How much is an EKG without insurance?

Expect roughly $40–$150 for the test itself at a clinic or hospital outpatient department, and more if a visit fee is added. To keep costs down:

  1. Ask for the self-pay or cash price up front. Many facilities have a lower cash rate than their list price.
  2. Ask whether the reading is billed separately. A cardiologist's interpretation can be a second bill.
  3. Compare a primary care office or community health center with urgent care; prices can differ a lot.
  4. Check federally qualified health centers, which offer sliding-scale fees based on income.

How much is an EKG with insurance?

If the EKG is part of an office visit with an in-network doctor, you may pay only your visit copay. If you have not met your deductible, you may pay the insurer's negotiated rate. Call the number on your insurance card and ask about CPT code 93000 to get an estimate.

How long does an EKG take?

The recording itself takes about 10 seconds. With undressing, placing the ten stickers and removing them, the whole appointment usually takes about 5–10 minutes. It does not hurt, uses no needles and no radiation, and sends no electricity into your body.

What to wear and how to prepare

  • Wear a top that is easy to remove or open. Stickers go on your chest, arms and legs.
  • Skip body lotion or oil on the day. It can stop the stickers from sticking well.
  • Avoid full-length tights or pantyhose, which block the ankle stickers.
  • You can eat and drink normally unless you are told otherwise.
  • Bring a list of your medicines, and a copy of any older EKG if you have one.
  • Lie still and breathe normally. Talking, moving or shivering adds noise to the tracing.

Men with a lot of chest hair may have small areas shaved so the stickers make good contact.

When will I get my EKG results?

The machine prints the tracing straight away, often with a computer-generated reading. A clinician still needs to review it.

  • In the emergency room or urgent care: usually within minutes.
  • In a doctor's office: often during the same visit.
  • For a routine or pre-surgery EKG: it may be a few days before you hear back.

If your report includes phrases you do not understand, see how to read your ECG report or look up the phrase on this site. Common ones include abnormal ECG and borderline ECG.

Do you need a doctor's order?

Usually, yes. Most clinics and insurers require an order from a clinician, though urgent care and some retail clinics can see you as a walk-in and order the test themselves during the visit. Some direct-to-consumer services offer EKGs without a referral, but make sure a qualified clinician reads the result and that you get a copy.

What usually happens next

  1. Normal EKG: usually no further action, unless symptoms suggest a problem the EKG cannot see.
  2. Minor or borderline finding: often a repeat EKG or a comparison with an older one.
  3. Abnormal finding or ongoing symptoms: your doctor may order blood tests, an echocardiogram, a heart monitor or a stress test.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Why are you ordering this EKG, and what are you looking for?
  • Will you compare it with my previous EKGs?
  • Is the reading billed separately?
  • If my symptoms come and go, would a longer heart monitor be more useful?

Frequently asked questions

Can you get an EKG at urgent care?

Yes, many urgent care centers offer walk-in EKGs for non-emergency reasons. Call ahead to check, and go to the emergency room or call 911 for chest pain.

Can I get an EKG without seeing a doctor?

Most places need a clinician's order, but urgent care and some retail clinics can order one during a walk-in visit. Make sure a qualified clinician reviews the tracing.

Does Medicare cover an EKG?

Yes. Medicare Part B covers a one-time screening EKG with a referral from your "Welcome to Medicare" visit, and diagnostic EKGs when they are medically necessary. You usually pay 20% after the deductible.

Is an EKG painful?

No. The stickers only record signals. The only discomfort is a slight tug when they are pulled off.

How accurate are smartwatch EKGs?

They are good at spotting atrial fibrillation during symptoms, but they record one lead only and cannot replace a 12-lead EKG or detect most heart attacks.

Is an EKG the same as an echocardiogram?

No. An EKG records electrical signals; an echocardiogram is an ultrasound that shows the heart's structure and pumping. They are priced and booked separately.
