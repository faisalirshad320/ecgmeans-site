Normal QT Interval: QT and QTc Normal Range Explained

The QT interval is the time the ventricles take to activate and then recover. Because it naturally shortens at faster heart rates, the machine corrects it for rate and prints the QTc. Normal QTc is under about 440 ms in men and 460 ms in women; over 500 ms is clearly prolonged and raises the risk of a dangerous rhythm called torsades de pointes.

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBS · Medical DoctorPMDC 54440-SUpdated 2026-10-26
Quick answer

A normal QT interval, once corrected for heart rate (QTc), is under about 440–450 ms in adult men and under about 460 ms in adult women. Values a little above this are borderline. A QTc over 500 ms is clearly prolonged and raises the risk of a dangerous rhythm called torsades de pointes. A QTc under about 350–390 ms is unusually short.

What "QTc normal range" means

People searching for the normal QT interval usually have a number from their ECG (or EKG — the same test) and want to know whether it is fine. The key point is that the raw QT on its own is hard to judge, because it depends on heart rate. The figure to look at is the QTc — the QT corrected for heart rate. That is the number the normal ranges below refer to.

Recovery (repolarization) is controlled by potassium and sodium channels in the heart cells. Genetic variants (long QT syndrome), many medicines and low potassium, magnesium or calcium all slow recovery and stretch the QT. A long QT makes the ventricles vulnerable to a chaotic rhythm, which is why it is watched closely.

Automatic QTc measurement is imperfect — it is unreliable at very fast or slow rates and in wide-QRS rhythms — so a borderline machine value is usually re-measured by hand.

QT interval normal range by sex and age

Women have a slightly longer QTc than men after puberty, so the limits differ. Guideline cut-offs vary a little; these are the commonly used adult values.

QTc (adults) Men Women What it usually means
Short Under about 350–390 ms Under about 350–390 ms Unusually short; needs review if very short or with symptoms
Normal Under about 440 ms Under about 450 ms Normal
Borderline About 440–450 ms About 450–460 ms Usually not significant on its own
Prolonged About 450 ms or more About 460–470 ms or more Check medicines and electrolytes
Markedly prolonged Over 500 ms Over 500 ms Clearly higher risk of torsades de pointes; prompt review

A few notes on the table:

  • The American Heart Association's ECG standards define a prolonged QTc as 450 ms or more in men and 460 ms or more in women, and a short QTc as 390 ms or less. Other references use 440 ms for men. That is why reports and websites quote slightly different numbers.
  • Children before puberty have similar values for boys and girls, roughly under 440–450 ms. Newborns are judged on separate charts.
  • An increase of more than 60 ms from your own baseline after starting a medicine is treated as significant even if the absolute number is still below 500 ms.

How the QT interval is measured

The QT runs from the start of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave. It covers both ventricular activation (the QRS) and recovery (the ST segment and T wave).

What it looks like on the ECG

Measured from the start of the QRS to the end of the T wave. As a rule of thumb, at normal heart rates the QT should be less than half the distance between two R waves.

Measuring tips used by clinicians

  1. Pick the right lead. Lead II or V5–V6 is used, choosing the lead where the QT looks longest.
  2. Find the true end of the T wave. Draw a line along the steepest part of the T wave's downslope; where it meets the baseline is the end of the T wave (the "tangent method"). This avoids guessing where a shallow tail finishes.
  3. Handle U waves carefully. A small, separate U wave is left out. A large U wave merged with the T wave may need to be included.
  4. Average several beats. Measure three to five beats and use a typical or the longest value. In atrial fibrillation, beat-to-beat variation is large, so more beats are averaged.
  5. Allow for a wide QRS. In bundle branch block or a paced rhythm, part of the QT is lengthened by the slow activation. Clinicians adjust for this, often by looking at the JT interval (QT minus QRS).

At 25 mm per second, each small square is 40 ms, so a QT of 10 small squares is 400 ms. If you want to work out a QTc from your own numbers, our QTc calculator does the arithmetic.

How the QTc is calculated

The QT naturally shortens as the heart speeds up and lengthens as it slows. Correction formulas convert it to what it would be at 60 beats per minute.

Formula How it works Strengths and limits
Bazett QT ÷ √(RR interval in seconds) Most widely used and printed by most machines; over-corrects at fast rates and under-corrects at slow rates
Fridericia QT ÷ ∛(RR interval in seconds) More accurate at fast and slow rates; often preferred for drug monitoring
Framingham QT + 0.154 × (1 − RR) Linear correction; stable across rates
Hodges QT + 1.75 × (heart rate − 60) Linear correction; simple to calculate

Between about 60 and 100 beats per minute the formulas give similar answers. At a fast heart rate, Bazett can make a normal QT look long — a common reason a machine flags "prolonged QT" during sinus tachycardia when a hand measurement with Fridericia is normal.

Common causes

  • Medicines: some antibiotics (macrolides, fluoroquinolones), antipsychotics, antidepressants, antiemetics, antiarrhythmics, methadone
  • Low potassium, magnesium or calcium
  • Congenital long QT syndrome
  • Underactive thyroid, hypothermia
  • Heart attack, myocarditis, stroke or bleeding in the brain
  • Sinus bradycardia (lengthens uncorrected QT)

Risk adds up. Being female, older, having heart failure or kidney disease, and taking two QT-prolonging medicines at once all increase the chance of a significantly long QT. For more detail on the causes and management of a long QT, see QT prolongation; see hypokalemia on an ECG for the effect of low potassium.

What causes a short QT interval?

A short QTc is much less common. Causes include a high calcium level, digoxin effect, and a rare inherited condition called short QT syndrome, which can cause fainting and arrhythmias. A QTc only slightly under the normal range in a well person is usually not significant.

Should you worry?

A borderline or mildly prolonged QTc (roughly 440–470 ms) is usually a reason to review medicines and electrolytes and repeat the ECG. A QTc over 500 ms, or a family history of sudden death, fainting or seizures, needs prompt assessment. Many QT-prolonging medicines are safe individually but risky in combination.

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

Why a long QT matters: torsades de pointes

When recovery of the ventricles is delayed, an early extra beat can land on the vulnerable part of the T wave and set off torsades de pointes — a fast ventricular rhythm whose QRS complexes appear to twist around the baseline. It may stop by itself, causing a faint or a seizure-like episode, or it can progress to ventricular fibrillation. This is why a very long QTc is taken seriously, and why fainting in someone with a long QT is always an urgent symptom. See also R on T phenomenon.

Practical tips if your QTc is long

These are general points to discuss with your doctor, not instructions to change treatment:

  • Keep an up-to-date medicine list, including over-the-counter remedies and supplements, and show it to every prescriber and pharmacist. Some antihistamines, anti-sickness medicines and antibiotics can add to the effect.
  • Watch for fluid losses. Vomiting, diarrhoea and some water tablets lower potassium and magnesium, which lengthens the QT. Your doctor may check blood levels when you are unwell.
  • Report fainting, seizures or palpitations promptly, especially on starting a new medicine.
  • Tell your family if you are diagnosed with congenital long QT syndrome; relatives may be offered an ECG and genetic testing.

QT interval in special situations

  • Bundle branch block or pacing: the wide QRS lengthens the QT without any added risk from recovery, so the QTc looks falsely long. Clinicians adjust for this.
  • Atrial fibrillation: because each R-R gap differs, the QT changes beat to beat. Several beats are measured and averaged. See atrial fibrillation on an ECG.
  • Slow heart rates: at rates under 60, the uncorrected QT is long and Bazett may under-correct, so the formula choice matters.
  • Sleep and time of day: the QTc varies by a few milliseconds through the day, so small differences between two ECGs are not significant.

What usually happens next

  1. Manual re-measurement of the QTc
  2. Review of all medicines against a QT-drug list (crediblemeds.org)
  3. Potassium, magnesium and calcium blood tests
  4. Cardiology and genetic assessment if congenital long QT is suspected

Do not stop a prescribed medicine on your own because of a QTc result. Your doctor may change a dose, swap to an alternative, or decide the benefit outweighs a small change in QT, and will often repeat the ECG.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What is my QTc, and was it measured by hand?
  • Are any of my medicines lengthening it?
  • Should my family be checked?
  • Which correction formula was used, and does my heart rate affect the result?
  • Do I need a repeat ECG after any medicine change?

A worked example

Report line: "Sinus tachycardia, rate 112. Prolonged QT. QT/QTc 340/465 ms." Patient: 29-year-old woman with a fever and a chest infection, started on an antibiotic the day before.

The machine used Bazett's formula, which overestimates QTc at fast heart rates. Re-measured by hand and corrected with Fridericia, the QTc was about 430 ms — normal. Potassium and magnesium were checked and normal. Because the antibiotic was a macrolide, the doctor kept it but repeated the ECG once the fever settled; at a rate of 78 the QTc was 432 ms. No further action was needed.

This is a common story: a "prolonged QT" flagged at a fast heart rate that is normal on careful measurement.

Frequently asked questions

What is a normal QTc?

Roughly under 440 ms for men and 460 ms for women; the exact cut-offs vary a little between guidelines.

What QTc is dangerous?

Over 500 ms is associated with a clearly increased risk of torsades de pointes.

Can a long QT be reversed?

Often, if it is caused by a medicine or a low electrolyte. Congenital long QT is managed long-term with beta-blockers and lifestyle measures.

What is the difference between QT and QTc?

QT is the raw measured interval. QTc is the QT adjusted for heart rate, so results taken at different heart rates can be compared. Normal ranges refer to the QTc.

What is a normal QT interval in seconds?

A normal QTc is under about 0.44 seconds for men and 0.46 seconds for women. On standard ECG paper that is roughly 11 small squares.

Is a QTc of 450 normal?

For women, yes. For men it sits at the borderline-to-prolonged cut-off and is usually just a reason to review medicines and electrolytes and, if needed, repeat the ECG.

Why is my QTc high on the ECG machine but normal when the doctor measured it?

Machines often use Bazett's formula, which overstates QTc at fast heart rates, and can misjudge where the T wave ends. Hand measurement is more reliable for borderline values.
