Left Ventricular Hypertrophy (LVH): What It Means on an ECG

Needs clinician review

Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) means the main pumping chamber's muscle is thicker than normal. The ECG only measures the electricity, so it infers LVH from tall QRS complexes using "voltage criteria". These criteria miss many thick hearts and flag many normal ones — especially slim, young or athletic people — so the ECG statement is a prompt for an echocardiogram, not a diagnosis.

What "left ventricular hypertrophy" means

The left ventricle thickens when it has to pump against high pressure for years, most often from high blood pressure or a tight aortic valve. More muscle produces a bigger electrical signal, so the R waves over the left side of the heart (leads V5–V6, I, aVL) grow taller and the S waves over the right (V1–V2) grow deeper. Software adds these heights up against fixed thresholds such as the Sokolow-Lyon or Cornell criteria and, if they are exceeded, prints "voltage criteria for LVH" or "possible left ventricular hypertrophy".

When the thick muscle also strains, the ST segments and T waves in the lateral leads bend the opposite way to the QRS — the "LVH with strain" pattern, which is more specific than voltage alone.

What it looks like on the ECG

Tall R waves in V5–V6, I and aVL, deep S waves in V1–V2, often with left axis deviation. In the "strain" pattern there is down-sloping ST depression and asymmetrical T wave inversion in the lateral leads.

Common causes

  • Long-standing high blood pressure (by far the commonest)
  • Aortic valve narrowing (aortic stenosis) or leakage
  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
  • Chronic heavy athletic training (a normal adaptation)
  • Being young and slim with a thin chest wall (false positive on voltage alone)

Should you worry?

Voltage criteria on their own, in a slim young person with normal blood pressure, are frequently a false positive and need no action beyond a blood-pressure check. Genuine LVH matters because it raises the long-term risk of heart failure, atrial fibrillation and stroke — so a confirmed thick ventricle is a reason to treat blood pressure well. The "strain" pattern, or LVH found alongside symptoms, deserves a closer look.

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

What usually happens next

  1. Blood pressure measurement and review of any treatment
  2. An echocardiogram, which measures the wall thickness directly and is the real test for LVH
  3. Assessment for aortic valve disease and, in selected people, for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
  4. Tighter control of blood pressure, cholesterol and weight if LVH is confirmed

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Was this voltage criteria only, or was there a strain pattern too?
  • Should I have an echocardiogram to confirm it?
  • Is my blood pressure well enough controlled?

Frequently asked questions

Is left ventricular hypertrophy on an ECG serious?

It depends on whether it is real. The ECG over-calls it; a confirmed thick ventricle is a meaningful marker of cardiovascular risk and a reason to treat blood pressure well.

How accurate is the ECG for LVH?

Not very. Voltage criteria miss many enlarged ventricles and flag many normal hearts, which is why an echocardiogram is used to confirm it.

Can LVH be reversed?

Yes, often. Lowering blood pressure over months to years can reduce the thickness, especially when it is caught early.
