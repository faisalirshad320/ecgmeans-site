QRS Interval: Normal QRS Duration and What a Wide QRS Means

The QRS interval (or duration) is how long it takes the electrical impulse to spread through both ventricles. Normal is under 120 ms — less than three small squares — and many cardiologists prefer under 100–110 ms. A wide QRS means the impulse took a slow route: usually a bundle branch block, a beat starting in the ventricle, a pacemaker, or a drug or electrolyte effect.

Quick answer

The QRS interval is the time the electrical impulse takes to spread through both lower chambers of the heart. A normal QRS duration in adults is about 70–110 ms and always under 120 ms, which is under three small squares on ECG paper. A QRS of 110–119 ms suggests a mild conduction delay; 120 ms or more is a wide QRS, most often from a bundle branch block.

What "QRS interval normal range" means

The QRS interval is one of the five key numbers printed at the top of an ECG (EKG) report, alongside heart rate, PR, QT/QTc and axis. It is often written as "QRS duration" or "QRSd". It measures the time from the very start of the QRS complex to its very end — in other words, how long it takes to switch on the ventricles.

The ventricles are wired with a fast-conducting network (the His-Purkinje system) so that they contract almost simultaneously. If the impulse uses that network, the QRS is narrow. If part of the network is blocked, or the beat originates in ventricular muscle, the impulse spreads cell-to-cell and the QRS widens.

In adults, values of 110–119 ms are sometimes labelled intraventricular conduction delay (IVCD) or incomplete bundle branch block.

Normal QRS duration: ranges at a glance

QRS duration (adults) Small squares at 25 mm/s What it usually means
Under 100 ms Under 2.5 Normal, narrow QRS
100–109 ms 2.5–2.7 Usually still normal; seen in many healthy adults, especially men and larger hearts
110–119 ms 2.75–3 Mild delay: may be reported as incomplete right or left bundle branch block (if the shape fits) or nonspecific intraventricular conduction delay
120 ms or more 3 or more Wide QRS: complete bundle branch block, ventricular beat or rhythm, paced beat, pre-excitation, or drug/electrolyte effect
Very wide (over about 160–200 ms) 4–5 or more Suggests ventricular origin, pacing, severe high potassium or sodium-channel drug toxicity

Notes:

  • The 120 ms cut-off is the one used for complete left and right bundle branch block in American Heart Association ECG standards. The 110–119 ms band is used for the incomplete forms — see incomplete right bundle branch block and incomplete left bundle branch block.
  • Normal QRS values are a little shorter in women than in men on average.
  • Children have shorter QRS durations, so a value normal for an adult can be wide for a young child. Paediatric ECGs are judged against age-based charts.

How to measure the QRS interval

What it looks like on the ECG

Measured from the start of the Q (or R) wave to the end of the S wave. Narrow: under 3 small squares. Wide: 3 small squares or more, often with a notched or bizarre shape.

Counting it yourself

  1. Choose the widest-looking lead. The QRS can look narrower in some leads because part of it lies along the baseline. Machines measure across all 12 leads at once and report the widest overall duration, which is why a hand measurement in one lead may come out shorter.
  2. Find the start. The first deflection from the baseline after the P wave — a downward Q wave or, if there is no Q, the upward R wave.
  3. Find the end (the J point). Where the last wave of the QRS — usually the S wave — meets the ST segment.
  4. Count the small squares. At the standard paper speed of 25 mm/s, each small square is 40 ms. Three small squares = 120 ms.

If the PR and QT are also on your mind, see the PR interval and normal QT interval pages, or the summary in normal ECG values.

Narrow vs wide QRS: what each tells you

Narrow QRS (under 120 ms) Wide QRS (120 ms or more)
Route of the impulse Down the normal fast wiring Slow, cell-to-cell spread through muscle
Where the beat started Above the ventricles — sinus node, atria or AV junction Ventricles, or above with a blocked or bypassed pathway
Common rhythms Sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, SVT Bundle branch block, PVCs, ventricular tachycardia, paced rhythm
Fast rhythm with this QRS Usually supraventricular tachycardia Treated as ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise

Common causes

  • Left or right bundle branch block
  • Ventricular escape or ectopic beats, ventricular tachycardia
  • Paced rhythm
  • High potassium
  • Sodium-channel blocking drugs (flecainide, tricyclic antidepressants, some others)
  • WPW pre-excitation
  • Left ventricular hypertrophy (mild widening)

Others include hypothermia and rate-related aberrancy, where a bundle branch blocks only at faster heart rates. For a deeper look at each cause, see wide QRS complex, intraventricular conduction delay, hyperkalemia on an ECG and Wolff-Parkinson-White.

Should you worry?

A wide QRS always has a reason and the reason determines the importance. A stable bundle branch block found by chance is often benign; a new wide QRS with a fast rhythm, high potassium or a drug overdose is an emergency.

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

Is a QRS of 110–119 ms a problem?

Usually not. A QRS in this band in a well person, especially with an incomplete right bundle branch block shape, is very common and generally harmless. It is worth a closer look if it is new compared with an older ECG, if it has a left bundle branch shape, or if there are symptoms such as breathlessness, because it can occasionally be an early sign of heart muscle disease.

Why a new wide QRS matters more than an old one

A wide QRS that has been unchanged for years usually reflects stable wiring. A wide QRS that is new — especially with chest pain, fainting, a fast rate, or a known kidney problem — can point to a heart attack, a dangerous rhythm, high potassium or medicine toxicity. Keeping a copy of an old ECG makes this comparison possible.

QRS duration in special situations

  • Pacemakers. A pacemaker that paces the ventricles usually produces a wide QRS (often 140–180 ms or more) after a sharp pacing spike. This is expected and is not a fault. See paced rhythm.
  • Heart failure. In people with a weakened heart, a wide QRS with a left bundle branch block pattern — especially 150 ms or more — means the two ventricles contract out of step. This is one of the main reasons doctors consider cardiac resynchronisation therapy (a biventricular pacemaker), which can help the ventricles beat together again.
  • High potassium. As potassium rises, T waves peak first, then the QRS widens and P waves flatten. A newly wide QRS with a high potassium level is a medical emergency.
  • Medicine overdose. Tricyclic antidepressants and some other sodium-channel blockers widen the QRS in overdose. A QRS over about 100 ms in this setting is treated as a warning sign.
  • Athletes and larger hearts. Training can lengthen the QRS by a few milliseconds and commonly produces an incomplete right bundle branch block pattern, which is a normal adaptation.
  • Thickened heart muscle. Left ventricular hypertrophy can widen the QRS slightly, usually staying under 120 ms, along with tall voltages.

What usually happens next

  1. Identify the pattern — bundle branch block, paced, ventricular
  2. Electrolytes and medication review
  3. Echocardiogram for new bundle branch block
  4. Urgent treatment if the cause is hyperkalaemia or drug toxicity

Your doctor may not need any of this for a narrow QRS or a stable incomplete block. Do not stop a prescribed medicine yourself; your doctor may review it if it can widen the QRS.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What is my QRS duration?
  • Which pattern does it fit?
  • Is this new?
  • Do I need an echocardiogram to check how my heart is pumping?
  • Could any of my medicines be widening the QRS?

A worked example

Report line: "Sinus rhythm, rate 66. Incomplete right bundle branch block. QRS 112 ms. Borderline ECG." Patient: 24-year-old student, pre-sports screening, no symptoms.

The QRS of 112 ms sat in the 110–119 ms band, with an rSR′ shape in V1 typical of incomplete right bundle branch block. This is common in healthy young people and athletes. There were no other abnormalities, no symptoms and no family history of heart disease. No further tests were needed. If a heart murmur or breathlessness had been present, an echocardiogram would have been arranged to look for a hole in the heart between the atria, which can sometimes produce this pattern.

Frequently asked questions

What is a normal QRS interval?

Under 120 ms; ideally under 100–110 ms.

Is a wide QRS dangerous?

It depends on the cause. Long-standing bundle branch block is usually not; a new wide-complex tachycardia is.

What does narrow QRS mean?

That the ventricles were activated through the normal fast pathway — the impulse came from above the ventricles.

What is a normal QRS duration in seconds?

About 0.07 to 0.11 seconds in adults, and always under 0.12 seconds.

Is a QRS of 100 ms normal?

Yes. A QRS of 100 ms is within the normal range for adults.

Is a QRS of 120 ms normal?

No. 120 ms is the cut-off for a wide QRS. It usually means a complete bundle branch block or another conduction change, and a clinician will review the pattern.

Can exercise or a fast heart rate widen the QRS?

In some people a bundle branch blocks only at faster rates, so the QRS widens during exercise and narrows again at rest. This is called rate-related bundle branch block.
