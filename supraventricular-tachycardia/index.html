SVT on an ECG: What Supraventricular Tachycardia Looks Like and Means

Needs clinician review

Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) is a fast heart rhythm that starts at or above the AV node, in the upper chambers or the junction, rather than in the lower chambers (the ventricles). It usually begins and ends suddenly. SVT can feel unpleasant, but it is rarely dangerous in an otherwise healthy heart, and recurrent episodes can very often be cured. Because it can sometimes need urgent treatment, a doctor should review it.

Quick answer

On an ECG, SVT (supraventricular tachycardia) usually looks like a fast, perfectly regular rhythm at about 150–250 beats per minute with narrow QRS complexes and P waves that are hidden or hard to see. It starts and stops suddenly. SVT is rarely dangerous in an otherwise healthy heart, but it needs a doctor's review, and urgent help if it causes chest pain or fainting.

SVT ECG at a glance

If you have been handed an SVT ECG strip — or "SVT" appears on your report — the short version is: the heart was racing, perfectly evenly, using its normal wiring to the ventricles but driven from above. The ECG is the test that makes the diagnosis, and a tracing captured during an episode is the single most useful thing your doctor can have. (ECG and EKG are the same test, so "SVT on EKG" means the same thing.)

What "Supraventricular tachycardia" means

"Supraventricular" means "above the ventricles", and "tachycardia" means a fast heart rate. Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) is a fast heart rhythm that starts at or above the AV node, the electrical relay station between the upper and lower chambers of the heart. It begins in the atria (the upper chambers) or the junction, not in the ventricles.

The two commonest types are:

  • AVNRT (AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia). A re-entry circuit inside or around the AV node lets the electrical signal loop round and round, driving the heart fast.
  • AVRT (atrioventricular re-entrant tachycardia). The circuit uses an extra electrical connection called an accessory pathway, as in Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome.

A typical feature of SVT is that it begins and ends abruptly. People often say it feels like a switch has been flicked on, and later flicked off.

Strictly, "supraventricular tachycardia" is an umbrella term that also includes sinus tachycardia, atrial flutter and atrial fibrillation. In everyday use, though — and on most ECG reports — "SVT" means the sudden-onset, regular re-entry rhythms described on this page (sometimes called paroxysmal SVT, or PSVT).

What does SVT look like on an ECG?

What it looks like on the ECG

On an ECG, SVT usually appears as a regular, narrow-QRS tachycardia. In plain terms:

  • Fast and regular: the rate is typically 150-250 bpm, and the beats come at a steady, even pace.
  • Narrow QRS: the QRS complex, which shows the ventricles being activated, is usually narrow at less than 120 ms. This tells your doctor the rhythm is using the heart's normal wiring to reach the ventricles.
  • Hidden P waves: the P waves, which reflect activity in the upper chambers, are often hidden in or just after the QRS. That is why they can be hard to spot.
  • Sudden onset and offset: when an episode is captured from start to finish, it starts and stops suddenly.

Sometimes SVT can look wide on the ECG. This happens if the person already has a bundle branch block, or if one develops because of the fast rate (called aberrancy). Doctors take wide-complex fast rhythms very seriously, as explained below.

Reading an SVT ECG strip step by step

  1. Rate: count the QRS complexes in a 6-second strip and multiply by 10. SVT is usually 150–250.
  2. Regularity: measure the gaps between R waves. In SVT they are virtually identical.
  3. QRS width: under 3 small boxes (120 ms) is narrow.
  4. P waves: look just after the QRS, in the ST segment, and in lead V1 and the inferior leads (II, III, aVF) for small notches that are really hidden P waves.
  5. Compare with a tracing in normal rhythm: a delta wave on the normal-rhythm ECG points to an accessory pathway (AVRT).

There are also rarer forms, such as junctional tachycardia. For students, the position of the P wave relative to the QRS (the "RP interval") is the classic clue to which type you are looking at.

Symptoms of SVT

During an episode people commonly notice:

  • A sudden, rapid, regular pounding in the chest
  • A pulsing or "frog-like" flapping feeling in the neck (especially with AVNRT)
  • Breathlessness or chest tightness
  • Light-headedness, and occasionally fainting
  • Needing to pass urine afterwards

Episodes can last seconds, minutes or hours, and some people go months between them. Many first notice SVT on a smartwatch reading a very high, steady heart rate.

Common causes

  • A re-entry circuit at the AV node (AVNRT). This is the most common cause.
  • An accessory pathway (AVRT or WPW).
  • Atrial tachycardia, coming from an ectopic (extra) focus of electrical activity in the atria.
  • Triggers that can set off episodes, including caffeine, alcohol, stress, lack of sleep, stimulant medicines and an overactive thyroid.

How doctors catch SVT on an ECG

Because SVT comes and goes, the hardest part is often recording it. Options include:

Method How long it records Best for
12-lead ECG in clinic or ER About 10 seconds Episodes that last long enough to get to a doctor
Holter monitor 24 hours to about 2 weeks Daily or near-daily episodes
Event monitor or patch Up to about 30 days Episodes every few weeks
Implantable loop recorder Up to several years Rare episodes, especially with fainting
Smartwatch or phone ECG 30 seconds when you trigger it Capturing an episode at home to show your doctor

A single-lead watch tracing can strongly suggest SVT, but doctors usually confirm the type with a 12-lead ECG or an electrophysiology study before ablation.

Should you worry?

SVT is often uncomfortable. It can cause palpitations, a pounding feeling in the neck, breathlessness or light-headedness, but it is rarely dangerous in an otherwise healthy heart. Get urgent help if a fast heartbeat comes with chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting, or if it does not stop. Any wide-complex fast rhythm is treated as ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise, so your doctor will check your ECG carefully.

If your episodes come and go and have never been caught on an ECG, see heart palpitations with a normal ECG for how doctors capture them.

What usually happens next

Your doctor will look at your ECG and symptoms to confirm SVT and work out which type it is likely to be. What happens next depends on whether you are having an episode now or have had one in the past.

Stopping an episode:

  • Vagal manoeuvres can sometimes stop SVT. These are simple techniques such as bearing down, and the modified Valsalva manoeuvre is one your doctor or nurse may teach you.
  • Adenosine is a medicine given in hospital to stop an episode that does not settle with vagal manoeuvres.

Looking for an underlying cause:

  • Your doctor may arrange an echocardiogram (an ultrasound scan of the heart) to check its structure and pumping.
  • They may also check your thyroid function with a blood test, because an overactive thyroid can be a trigger.

Longer-term options:

  • If SVT keeps coming back, catheter ablation is a very effective cure. Your doctor can explain whether it is suitable for you.
  • Cutting back on known triggers may help. These include caffeine, alcohol, stress, lack of sleep and stimulant medicines.

If you have been told you have SVT, it helps to note when episodes happen, how long they last and what you were doing at the time. This information is useful for your doctor.

Frequently asked questions

What does supraventricular tachycardia mean on my ECG?

It means your heart was beating fast with a rhythm that starts at or above the AV node, in the upper chambers or the junction, rather than in the ventricles. On the ECG it usually looks like a regular, narrow-QRS rhythm, typically at 150-250 bpm. It usually starts and stops suddenly.

Is SVT dangerous?

SVT is rarely dangerous in an otherwise healthy heart, although it can feel unpleasant, with palpitations, a pounding neck, breathlessness or light-headedness. It becomes urgent if it causes chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting, or if it does not stop. In those cases, seek urgent medical help.

What causes SVT?

The most common cause is a re-entry circuit at the AV node (AVNRT). Other causes include an accessory pathway (AVRT or WPW) and atrial tachycardia from an extra electrical focus in the atria. Episodes can be triggered by caffeine, alcohol, stress, lack of sleep, stimulant medicines or an overactive thyroid.

How is an SVT episode stopped?

Vagal manoeuvres, such as bearing down or the modified Valsalva manoeuvre, can stop an episode. If that does not work, a medicine called adenosine can be given in hospital. Seek urgent help if an episode does not stop or causes worrying symptoms.

Can SVT be prevented or cured?

Avoiding known triggers such as caffeine, alcohol, stress, lack of sleep and stimulant medicines may help reduce episodes. If SVT keeps coming back, catheter ablation is a very effective cure. Your doctor may also arrange an echocardiogram and thyroid tests to look for contributing causes.

Can an ECG show SVT if I am not having an episode?

Usually not. Between episodes the ECG is often normal, although it can show clues such as a delta wave. A Holter, event monitor or smartwatch recording during symptoms is often needed.

What heart rate is SVT on an ECG?

Typically 150–250 beats per minute, sometimes a little lower or higher. A regular rate around 150 should also make doctors consider atrial flutter.
