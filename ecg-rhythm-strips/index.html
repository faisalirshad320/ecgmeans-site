Quick answer

An ECG rhythm strip is a recording of the heart's electrical activity over several seconds, usually from one lead. To identify the rhythm, check five things in order: rate, regularity, P waves, PR interval and QRS width. This library shows 36 common EKG rhythms side by side with those features and the single most useful clue for each.

How to use this ECG rhythm strip library

This library shows 36 of the rhythms most often printed on ECG reports and rhythm-strip tests, each drawn on standard ECG paper (25 mm per second, 10 mm per millivolt). For every rhythm you get the strip, the four features that identify it — rate, regularity, P waves and QRS width — the single most useful clue, and a plain-English verdict on how serious it usually is. Click any strip for the full explanation.

The drawings are schematic teaching illustrations built from the textbook criteria, not real patient tracings. Real EKG rhythm strips are noisier and vary from person to person, so use these to learn the patterns, and use your own report and your clinician to understand your own heart.

Identify any rhythm strip in five questions

Every ECG rhythm, however exotic, can be worked out with the same five questions, asked in the same order:

Step Question What to look for Normal answer
1 How fast? Count QRS complexes in 6 seconds × 10, or 300 ÷ large boxes between beats 60–100 per minute
2 Regular or irregular? Compare the gaps between R waves (R-R intervals) Regular (slight variation with breathing is normal)
3 Is there a P wave before every QRS? Upright P waves in lead II, one per QRS Yes
4 How long is the PR interval? From the start of P to the start of QRS 120–200 ms (3–5 small boxes)
5 Is the QRS narrow or wide? Width of the QRS complex Under 120 ms (3 small boxes)

A narrow QRS means the beat travelled down the normal wiring, so the rhythm started above the ventricles (sinus, atrial or junctional). A wide QRS means either the beat started in the ventricles or it was conducted abnormally (bundle branch block). Irregularity with no P waves at all is atrial fibrillation until proven otherwise. The rhythms that are emergencies

Most rhythms on this page are common and many are harmless. A small group are not. These are the rhythms emergency teams are trained to recognise within seconds, because they need immediate treatment:

Rhythm Why it is dangerous Immediate response
Ventricular fibrillation The ventricles quiver and pump no blood CPR and defibrillation
Pulseless ventricular tachycardia Too fast to fill and pump effectively CPR and defibrillation
Asystole No electrical activity CPR and adrenaline (epinephrine); not shockable
Pulseless electrical activity Electrical rhythm without a heartbeat strong enough to give a pulse CPR and finding the cause
Third-degree heart block Very slow ventricular rate, risk of collapse Urgent pacing assessment
Torsades de pointes Can turn into ventricular fibrillation Magnesium, correcting the cause, defibrillation if unstable

Get urgent help — call 911 (US), 999 (UK) or 112 — if anyone has chest pain, collapses, faints, or has a racing heartbeat with breathlessness or dizziness. Do not wait to read a strip; symptoms matter more than any drawing.

Rhythm strips vs a 12-lead ECG

A rhythm strip is usually a single lead (most often lead II) recorded over 6 seconds or longer. It is ideal for working out the rhythm — fast or slow, regular or not, where beats start. A full 12-lead ECG records the same heartbeat from twelve angles, which is what is needed to diagnose a heart attack, axis deviation, hypertrophy or a bundle branch block with confidence. Telemetry and bedside monitors usually display a rhythm strip from a 3-lead or 5-lead setup.

Frequently asked questions

What are the basic EKG rhythms everyone should know?

Normal sinus rhythm, sinus bradycardia and tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, SVT, PVCs, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, asystole and the three degrees of heart block cover the large majority of rhythm strips.

How long is a standard rhythm strip?

Most rhythm strips are 6 seconds long, which makes the rate easy to estimate: count the QRS complexes and multiply by 10. Many machines print 10 seconds.

Which lead is used for a rhythm strip?

Lead II is the usual choice because it shows P waves clearly. Lead V1 is often added because it helps tell atrial activity and bundle branch patterns apart.

What are the four lethal rhythms?

Ventricular fibrillation, pulseless ventricular tachycardia, asystole and pulseless electrical activity — the four rhythms of cardiac arrest.

Can I diagnose my own rhythm from these pictures?

No. These strips are teaching illustrations. Your own tracing should be interpreted by a clinician who also knows your symptoms, history and full 12-lead ECG.

Why does my smartwatch rhythm look different from these strips?

Smartwatches record a single lead from the wrist, at lower quality, so the waves are smaller and noisier. They are good at spotting atrial fibrillation but cannot show everything a 12-lead ECG does.
