QT Prolongation on an ECG: What It Means

Needs clinician review

QT prolongation means the QT interval, the time your heart's lower chambers (the ventricles) take to contract and then reset electrically, is longer than normal. The number doctors actually judge is the rate-corrected value, called the QTc. A long QT doesn't usually cause symptoms by itself. It is a warning sign worth taking seriously because it can set the stage for a dangerous heart rhythm, and the cause can often be found and corrected.

What "QT prolongation" means

Every heartbeat involves an electrical cycle. The ventricles, the heart's main pumping chambers, are triggered to contract and then electrically "reset" so they are ready for the next beat. The QT interval on an ECG measures how long that whole process takes.

QT prolongation means this interval is longer than normal. In other words, the ventricles are taking more time than expected to recharge. This matters because a long QT can set the stage for a dangerous rhythm called torsades de pointes.

The raw QT measurement changes with heart rate, so doctors judge the rate-corrected value (QTc) rather than the raw number. If your report mentions a QTc, that is the figure your clinician will focus on.

What it looks like on the ECG

On the tracing, the QT interval is measured from the start of the QRS complex (the tall spike showing the ventricles being activated) to the end of the T wave (the gentler bump showing them resetting). With QT prolongation, this stretch is visibly long.

Because the QT naturally shortens as the heart rate rises, it has to be corrected for rate. This is usually done with the Bazett formula (QTc = QT / square root of RR). The RR is the time between one heartbeat and the next.

As a general guide:

  • A normal QTc is under about 440 ms in men and 460 ms in women.
  • A QTc over 500 ms is clearly prolonged and markedly raises the risk of torsades de pointes.

Values between the normal range and 500 ms still count as prolonged. Your clinician will interpret them alongside your overall situation.

Common causes

  • Medicines. Many common drugs can lengthen the QT, including some antibiotics, antiarrhythmics (heart rhythm drugs), antipsychotics, antidepressants and anti-sickness drugs.
  • Low blood salts. This includes low potassium, low magnesium or low calcium.
  • Inherited long QT syndrome. Some people are born with a tendency to a long QT.
  • Bradycardia and some heart disease. A slow heart rate and certain heart conditions can lengthen the QT.
  • Underactive thyroid.

Often more than one factor is involved, such as a QT-prolonging medicine combined with low potassium.

Should you worry?

Needs clinician review

A prolonged QT is a warning sign rather than a problem that causes symptoms by itself. Most people with a long QT feel nothing from it. The real concern is torsades de pointes, a dangerous rhythm that can cause fainting or cardiac arrest. The longer the QTc and the more risk factors present, the greater the concern. A doctor should review the result and look for the cause.

Get urgent help if you faint, collapse, or someone with you becomes unresponsive, because these can be signs of torsades de pointes.

What usually happens next

The main step is finding the cause and correcting anything reversible. This commonly means:

  • Reviewing your medicines. Any drugs that may be lengthening the QT are stopped or changed. Don't stop a prescribed medicine on your own. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist first.
  • Checking and replacing blood salts. Potassium and magnesium are replaced if they are low.
  • Monitoring the QTc. Repeat ECGs show whether the interval is coming back down once causes are addressed.

If inherited long QT syndrome is suspected, a specialist manages it. Treatment sometimes includes beta-blockers or a defibrillator, a device that can correct a dangerous rhythm.

It helps to bring a full list of your medicines to your appointment, including anything bought over the counter. Mention any fainting episodes or family history of heart rhythm problems too.

Frequently asked questions

What does QT prolongation mean on my ECG?

It means the QT interval is longer than normal. This is the time your heart's ventricles take to contract and then reset electrically. Doctors judge the rate-corrected value, called the QTc, because the raw QT changes with heart rate.

Is a prolonged QT dangerous?

A long QT is a warning sign rather than something that causes symptoms on its own. The danger is that it can lead to a rhythm called torsades de pointes, which can cause fainting or cardiac arrest. The longer the QTc and the more risk factors present, the greater the concern. That is why a clinician should review it.

What QTc is considered normal?

A normal QTc is under about 440 ms in men and 460 ms in women. A QTc over 500 ms is clearly prolonged and markedly raises the risk of torsades de pointes. Your doctor will interpret your number in the context of your overall health and medicines.

What causes QT prolongation?

Common causes include many everyday medicines, such as some antibiotics, antiarrhythmics, antipsychotics, antidepressants and anti-sickness drugs. Low potassium, magnesium or calcium can also lengthen the QT. Other causes include inherited long QT syndrome, a slow heart rate (bradycardia), some heart disease and an underactive thyroid.

What happens next, and can it be corrected?

Your doctor will look for the cause and correct anything reversible. This might mean stopping or changing a culprit medicine and replacing low potassium or magnesium. Repeat ECGs are used to monitor the QTc. Inherited long QT syndrome is managed by a specialist, sometimes with beta-blockers or a defibrillator.
