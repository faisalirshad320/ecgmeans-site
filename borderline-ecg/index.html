Borderline ECG: What It Means and When to Worry

Usually harmless

A "borderline ECG" means the machine found something just outside its normal limits, but not far enough to call the tracing abnormal. It is the software's way of saying "probably fine, worth a human glance". In people without symptoms it is most often a normal variant or a technical artefact.

Quick answer

A borderline ECG means the machine's automated reading found one or two measurements just outside its normal limits, but nothing it rates as clearly abnormal. Common triggers are a heart rate slightly under 60, a mildly leftward axis, a PR interval just over 200 ms, or minor T wave flattening. In people without symptoms it is usually a normal variant and rarely needs treatment.

What "Borderline ECG" means

A borderline ECG is one of the overall verdicts that ECG software prints at the end of its automated report. (ECG and EKG are the same test; the spelling depends on the country.) The word does not name a disease. It is a grade.

ECG software grades its overall verdict. Normal means every measurement is inside the limits. Borderline means one or two measurements are marginally outside. Abnormal means a finding that the program considers clinically significant. Borderline is therefore the mildest of the non-normal verdicts.

The verdict is always driven by a named finding printed above it. Typical triggers are a heart rate a few beats under 60 or over 100, an axis that leans slightly left, a PR interval of just over 200 ms, minor T wave flattening, or voltage that just meets the criteria for a thickened ventricle in a slim young person.

Where "borderline" sits among ECG verdicts

Verdict on the report What the software is saying Typical next step
Normal ECG Every measurement and shape inside its limits None
Otherwise normal ECG One minor, named finding (for example sinus bradycardia), everything else normal Usually none
Borderline ECG One or two values marginally outside limits, or a soft pattern it cannot confidently grade Clinician over-read; sometimes a repeat
Abnormal ECG A finding the software rates as clinically significant Clinician review; further tests depend on the finding — see abnormal ECG

Exact wording varies by manufacturer, but the logic is the same: the computer is cautious by design. It is built to flag anything near a cut-off so that a human looks at it, and that means it flags many healthy hearts. Studies of computer-interpreted ECGs have shown repeatedly that the automated statement needs confirmation by an experienced reader.

What does borderline ECG mean on your report?

The useful question is not "what does borderline mean?" but "which finding made it borderline?" Look at the lines above the verdict. There will be one or two statements — for example "sinus bradycardia", "borderline left axis deviation" or "nonspecific T wave flattening". That line is the real result. The word "borderline" just summarises it.

Then look at the measurements across the top of the page. A borderline verdict nearly always traces back to one number sitting close to a cut-off. Our guide on how to read your ECG report explains each box.

What it looks like on the ECG

To the eye a borderline ECG usually looks normal. The difference is in the numbers at the top of the page — rate, PR, QRS, QT/QTc and axis — one of which will be close to a cut-off.

Typical adult limits and borderline values

Measurement Usual adult normal range What often triggers "borderline"
Heart rate 60–100 bpm 52–59 bpm at rest, or 100–110 bpm when nervous
PR interval 120–200 ms 200–220 ms (mild first degree AV block)
QRS duration Under 120 ms (usually under 110 ms) 110–119 ms, or an incomplete bundle branch block pattern
QTc Under about 450 ms (men), under about 460 ms (women) 450–470 ms
Axis About −30° to +90° −30° to −45° (borderline left axis deviation)
QRS voltage Varies with build, age and sex Tall voltages in a slim or athletic young adult
T waves Upright in most leads Mild flattening in one or two leads

Values differ slightly between machines and guidelines; see normal ECG values for a fuller list.

What can cause a borderline ECG?

Common causes

  • Heart rate slightly below 60 or above 100 at the time of recording
  • Borderline left axis deviation (a common age-related shift)
  • PR interval at the upper limit of normal
  • Minor, nonspecific T wave or ST changes
  • High QRS voltage in thin or athletic people
  • Chest electrodes placed slightly too high or too low

These causes fall into three groups:

  1. Normal variation in healthy people. A slow resting rate in a fit person (sinus bradycardia), early repolarization in young adults, tall voltages in a thin chest, or an incomplete right bundle branch block pattern, which is common and usually harmless.
  2. Temporary effects at the moment of recording. Anxiety, a fast breath, a recent meal or cold drink, a cold room, shivering, or moving during the test. These can shift the heart rate and flatten T waves. See nonspecific T wave abnormality.
  3. Technical factors. Electrodes in slightly the wrong place, a poor skin contact, or electrical artifact. Misplaced chest leads can mimic poor R wave progression or an old infarct pattern.

Less often, a borderline finding reflects something real but mild — slightly raised blood pressure thickening the heart wall, a medicine that slows conduction (such as a beta-blocker), or a low potassium level. These are worth knowing about but are rarely urgent.

Should you worry?

On its own, in someone who feels well, a borderline ECG rarely leads to any treatment. It becomes more relevant if you have symptoms, a strong family history of heart disease or sudden death, or if the tracing has changed since your last one.

Get urgent help if this finding comes with chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, fainting, or a fast heartbeat that will not settle. Those symptoms matter more than any single ECG phrase.

Should I be worried about a borderline ECG?

For most people, no. Ask yourself four questions — they are the same ones a doctor asks:

Question Reassuring answer Answer that deserves a closer look
Do I have symptoms? None, or only anxiety about the test Chest pain, breathlessness on effort, fainting, racing heart
Which finding made it borderline? Rate, axis, PR or voltage just past a cut-off A QTc approaching 470–500 ms, new conduction block, new T wave inversion
Is it new? Same as a previous ECG Different from an older tracing
What is my background? Young, fit, no family history Known heart disease, diabetes, high blood pressure, family history of sudden death under 50

If all of your answers sit in the left column, a borderline ECG is very unlikely to mean anything important. If one sits in the right column, it is a reason to let your doctor review the tracing properly — not a reason to panic.

It is also worth knowing what a single resting ECG cannot do. A normal or borderline ECG does not rule out every heart condition, and it cannot show narrowed arteries while you are at rest. See if my ECG is normal, is my heart OK? for what the test can and cannot tell you.

Borderline ECG in special situations

  • Athletes. Training causes a slow heart rate, larger voltages and sometimes mild first degree AV block. These often trigger a borderline verdict and are usually healthy adaptations.
  • Pre-employment, insurance or pre-operative checks. A borderline verdict may prompt a request for a doctor's over-read. A short note confirming a normal variant usually settles it.
  • Pregnancy. The heart rate rises and the axis can shift slightly as the uterus pushes the heart upward, both of which can trigger a borderline reading.
  • Older adults. A gradual leftward shift of the axis and a slightly longer PR interval are common with age.
  • Smartwatch or single-lead ECGs. These report "inconclusive" rather than borderline. That is a different thing — usually poor contact or an extra beat — and does not carry over to a 12-lead result.

What usually happens next

  1. Your clinician checks which measurement was borderline
  2. Often nothing further is needed
  3. Sometimes the ECG is simply repeated on another day with careful lead placement
  4. Further tests are reserved for people with symptoms or risk factors

Those further tests, when needed, are guided by the specific finding: blood tests (potassium, magnesium, thyroid) for T wave changes, an echocardiogram if voltages suggest a thickened wall, a Holter monitor for an irregular rhythm, or an exercise test if there are symptoms on effort.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Which measurement was borderline?
  • Is this a normal variant for someone of my age and build?
  • Should I have it repeated?
  • Is it different from any ECG I have had before?
  • Could any of my medicines explain it?

A worked example

Report line: "Sinus bradycardia, rate 54. Borderline left axis deviation. Borderline ECG." Patient: 46-year-old who cycles 100 miles a week, having a routine health check. No symptoms.

The doctor looked at the two named findings. A resting heart rate of 54 is normal for a trained cyclist. The axis was −32°, just past the −30° cut-off — a small, common shift that, on its own, has no effect on health. PR, QRS and QTc were all normal, and the T waves were upright. No further tests were needed, and the doctor wrote "normal variant" on the report.

That is the typical borderline ECG: two numbers a whisker past their limits, in a healthy person.

Frequently asked questions

Is a borderline ECG bad?

Usually not. It is closer to normal than to abnormal and most people with this verdict have a healthy heart.

Can a borderline ECG become normal?

Yes. Heart rate, breathing, body position and electrode placement all vary between recordings, so a repeat tracing is frequently reported as normal.

Will a borderline ECG affect a medical or insurance exam?

It may prompt a request for a doctor's over-read or a repeat ECG. A clinician's confirmation that it is a normal variant generally settles the matter.

What is the difference between a borderline ECG and an abnormal ECG?

Borderline means one or two values sit just past a cut-off. Abnormal means the software found something it considers clinically significant. Both need a clinician's read, but borderline is far more often a normal variant.

Can anxiety cause a borderline ECG?

Yes. Anxiety can raise the heart rate above 100 and briefly flatten T waves, both of which can push the automated verdict into the borderline range. A calm repeat ECG is often normal.

Does a borderline ECG mean I had a heart attack?

No. A borderline verdict is not a heart attack diagnosis. If a report mentions a possible old infarct, that is a separate statement and is often caused by lead placement; your doctor will check it against your history.

What does "borderline ECG" mean for a child?

Children have different normal values — faster rates and different T wave patterns — so adult limits do not apply. A paediatric reading by a clinician is what counts.
