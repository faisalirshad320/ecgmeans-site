Digoxin Effect on an ECG: What It Means and How It Differs From Toxicity

Needs clinician review

Digoxin (brand names include Lanoxin) is an old, still widely used heart medicine for atrial fibrillation and some types of heart failure. It changes the shape of the ECG in a very recognizable way, so reports often state "digoxin effect" or "ST changes consistent with digitalis." That phrase on its own is expected and usually not a cause for alarm. The important job for you and your doctor is to make sure nothing on the tracing, in your blood tests, or in how you feel suggests the level has drifted too high. This page explains both sides clearly.

Quick answer

Digoxin effect on an ECG is a scooped, downsloping ST depression (often called a "reverse tick" or "Salvador Dali moustache"), with a shortened QT and flattened T waves, seen in people taking digoxin. It shows the medicine is present, not that it is toxic. Digoxin toxicity is different: it causes new rhythm problems such as heart block, frequent PVCs or bidirectional VT and needs prompt review.

What "digoxin effect" means on an ECG report

Digoxin effect on an ECG is the set of changes the medicine normally produces at ordinary doses. Digoxin belongs to a group called cardiac glycosides, or digitalis drugs, originally made from the foxglove plant. It works in two main ways:

  • It blocks a pump in heart muscle cells (the sodium–potassium pump), which leaves more calcium inside the cells and makes each contraction a little stronger.
  • It increases the activity of the vagus nerve, which slows conduction through the AV node — the junction between the upper and lower chambers. That is why it slows the heart rate in atrial fibrillation.

These same actions change how the ventricles recover after each beat, and that recovery is what the ST segment and T wave show. So the ECG "signature" of digoxin is mostly in the ST–T part of the tracing.

The classic ECG features of digoxin effect

Feature What it looks like Why it happens
Scooped ST depression A curved, downsloping dip in the ST segment, like a reverse check mark ("reverse tick") or the curled moustache of the painter Salvador Dali Altered recovery of the ventricular muscle
Short QT interval The QT is shorter than usual Faster repolarization of the ventricles
Flattened, inverted or biphasic T waves Smaller T waves, sometimes dipping below the line Same as above
Prominent U waves A small extra bump after the T wave Repolarization changes; see U wave
Mildly long PR interval PR slightly over 200 ms Slowed AV node conduction from vagal effect

The ST changes are typically most visible in leads with tall R waves, such as V4–V6. They can look worrying to someone who is not expecting them, because ST depression can also mean reduced blood supply to the heart. The smooth, scooped shape is the clue: ischemic ST depression is more often flat (horizontal) or straight and downsloping.

It is important to know that digoxin effect does not tell you the blood level. People with perfectly normal levels can show marked scooping, and people with toxic levels can show very little. The ECG shape simply confirms the drug is on board.

Digoxin effect vs digoxin toxicity

This is the distinction that matters most. Digoxin has a narrow margin between a helpful level and a harmful one, and toxicity can happen even on a long-standing dose if something else changes — most often kidney function or potassium.

Digoxin effect Digoxin toxicity
Meaning The expected footprint of the medicine Too much digoxin acting on the heart and body
ECG Scooped ST, short QT, flat T waves Rhythm problems: new heart block, frequent PVCs, atrial tachycardia with block, bidirectional VT, very slow heart rate
Symptoms Usually none from the ECG change Nausea, vomiting, poor appetite, confusion, tiredness, visual changes, palpitations, dizziness
Blood level Usually within the target range Usually raised, but can occur at "normal" levels when potassium is low
Action Routine monitoring Prompt medical assessment; the medicine is usually held while the cause is checked

Digoxin toxicity ECG signs

Digoxin toxicity can cause almost any rhythm disturbance, but some patterns are especially characteristic because they combine two opposite effects: the heart muscle becomes more excitable (extra beats and fast rhythms), while the AV node conducts more slowly (heart block).

  1. Frequent premature ventricular contractions (PVCs). The most common sign, often as ventricular bigeminy (every other beat an extra beat). See PVCs.
  2. Atrial tachycardia with block. A fast atrial rhythm with only some beats getting through to the ventricles. This combination — fast top, blocked middle — is a classic pointer to digoxin. See atrial tachycardia.
  3. AV block of any degree. From first-degree to complete heart block.
  4. "Regularized" atrial fibrillation. Atrial fibrillation is normally irregular. If someone with AF on digoxin suddenly has a perfectly regular slow pulse, it can mean complete heart block with a junctional or ventricular escape rhythm taking over — a well-known toxicity sign.
  5. Accelerated junctional rhythm. See accelerated junctional rhythm.
  6. Sinus bradycardia or sinus pauses. A heart rate that becomes unusually slow.
  7. Bidirectional ventricular tachycardia. A rare, serious rhythm where the QRS direction flips from beat to beat. It is so closely associated with digoxin toxicity that seeing it should prompt the question straight away. See ventricular tachycardia.

Late, severe toxicity can lead to ventricular fibrillation, which is a cardiac arrest.

Symptoms of digoxin toxicity

Symptoms often build gradually, especially in older adults, and are easy to blame on something else.

  • Stomach and gut: nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, abdominal pain, diarrhea. These are often the earliest signs.
  • Vision: blurred vision, seeing yellow or green tints, or halos around lights. Altered color vision (xanthopsia) is a famous but not universal sign.
  • Brain: confusion, drowsiness, weakness, headache, unusual tiredness.
  • Heart: palpitations, dizziness, fainting, or a pulse that is unusually slow or irregular.

Get urgent help if you take digoxin and develop fainting, chest pain, severe breathlessness, a very slow or racing pulse, or confusion — or if someone has taken too much digoxin, including a child taking a tablet by accident. Call 911 (US), 999 (UK) or 112 (EU), or your local poison center (in the US, 1-800-222-1222).

Who is at risk of digoxin toxicity?

Digoxin is cleared mainly by the kidneys, and its effect on the heart is strongly influenced by blood salts. The main risk factors are:

Risk factor Why it matters
Reduced kidney function Less digoxin is removed, so levels climb. Dehydration or a kidney infection can tip someone over
Low potassium (hypokalemia) Increases digoxin's binding to heart cells, so toxicity can occur even at a normal blood level. Often caused by water tablets (diuretics), vomiting or diarrhea
Low magnesium or high calcium Both make the heart more sensitive to digoxin
Older age and low body weight Smaller volume to distribute the drug and often lower kidney function
Interacting medicines Some antiarrhythmics (such as amiodarone), certain calcium channel blockers (such as verapamil), and some antibiotics and antifungals can raise digoxin levels
Hypothyroidism Slows clearance of the drug
Acute overdose Accidental or intentional; often causes high potassium rather than low

Digoxin blood levels

A blood test measures the digoxin concentration. For the result to mean anything, the sample is usually taken at least 6 hours after the last dose, because levels taken sooner are falsely high while the drug is still spreading into the tissues.

Typical reference ranges quoted by laboratories are around 0.5 to 2.0 ng/mL (about 0.6 to 2.6 nmol/L). For heart failure, many clinicians now aim for the lower end — roughly 0.5 to 0.9 or 1.0 ng/mL — because higher levels have not shown extra benefit. Levels above about 2 ng/mL raise concern for toxicity, but the level must always be read with potassium, kidney function and symptoms. Toxicity can occur below that level when potassium is low, and some people tolerate higher levels without problems.

Alongside the digoxin level, your doctor will usually check potassium, magnesium, kidney function (creatinine and eGFR) and sometimes thyroid function.

This page does not give dosing advice. Never change or stop digoxin on your own; your prescriber will adjust it based on your levels and kidney function.

How digoxin toxicity is treated

Treatment is a hospital or specialist decision and depends on how severe the problem is. In general terms:

  • The digoxin is usually paused, and interacting medicines are reviewed.
  • Potassium and magnesium are corrected if low; high potassium in acute overdose is treated urgently.
  • Heart rhythm is monitored continuously.
  • For serious toxicity — dangerous rhythms, very high potassium, or a large overdose — doctors may give digoxin-specific antibody fragments (an antidote that binds digoxin in the blood).
  • Slow rhythms may need temporary pacing in some cases.

What usually happens next

If your report mentions digoxin effect and you feel well:

  1. Your doctor confirms you take digoxin and that the ECG matches what they expect.
  2. Blood tests may be checked or updated — digoxin level, potassium, magnesium and kidney function — especially if it has been a while, your kidney function has changed, or a new medicine has started.
  3. Rhythm review. The doctor looks closely for the toxicity patterns above, and compares with older ECGs.
  4. Routine follow-up continues if everything is in range.

If the report also mentions new heart block, frequent PVCs, a regular slow rhythm in someone with known AF, or an atrial tachycardia with block, expect same-day review.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Is this ECG change just digoxin effect, or is there any sign of toxicity?
  • When was my last digoxin level, and was it taken at the right time after my dose?
  • Are my potassium, magnesium and kidney function in a safe range?
  • Do any of my other medicines interact with digoxin?
  • Which symptoms should make me call you or seek urgent help?

Frequently asked questions

What does "digitalis effect" mean on an EKG?

It is another name for digoxin effect: the scooped ST depression, short QT and flattened T waves caused by digoxin or related medicines. ECG and EKG are the same test.

Does digoxin effect mean I have digoxin toxicity?

No. Digoxin effect only shows that the medicine is present and is common at normal doses. Toxicity is judged from rhythm problems, symptoms, potassium, kidney function and the blood level.

What is the Salvador Dali sign on an ECG?

It is a nickname for the curved, downsloping ST depression seen with digoxin, which resembles the painter's upturned moustache. It is also called the reverse tick sign.

What is the most common ECG sign of digoxin toxicity?

Frequent premature ventricular contractions, often as bigeminy, are the most common. Atrial tachycardia with block and bidirectional VT are the most characteristic.

Can digoxin toxicity happen with a normal digoxin level?

Yes. Low potassium, low magnesium or high calcium can make the heart more sensitive, so toxicity can occur at a level that looks normal.

Does digoxin cause yellow vision?

It can. Seeing yellow or green tints or halos around lights is a recognized sign of digoxin toxicity, though many people with toxicity do not have it. Report any new vision change to your doctor.

Can digoxin effect be mistaken for a heart attack?

The ST depression can resemble reduced blood supply, but its smooth scooped shape and the patient's medicine list usually make the difference clear. Symptoms and blood tests settle any doubt.
