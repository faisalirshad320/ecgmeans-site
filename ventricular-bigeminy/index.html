Ventricular Bigeminy: What It Means on an ECG

Depends on context

Ventricular bigeminy means the ECG shows a repeating pattern where every normal beat is followed by a premature ventricular contraction (PVC) — an early, wide beat from the ventricles. It often feels like skipped beats or a flip-flop in the chest. It is frequently harmless, but a doctor should check how often it occurs and whether the heart is otherwise normal.

What "ventricular bigeminy" means

A premature ventricular contraction (PVC) is an extra heartbeat that starts in the ventricles instead of the normal pacemaker. In ventricular bigeminy, PVCs alternate one-for-one with normal beats: normal – PVC – normal – PVC, in a steady repeating pair. "Bi-" means two, describing the repeating pair.

What it looks like on the ECG

A regular alternation between a normal narrow QRS (preceded by a P wave) and a wide, oddly shaped QRS with no preceding P wave. The wide beat usually comes early and is often followed by a slightly longer pause before the next normal beat.

Common causes

  • Perfectly normal hearts — PVCs are very common and often mean nothing
  • Caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, stress and tiredness
  • Low potassium or magnesium
  • An overactive thyroid
  • Underlying heart disease (previous heart attack, cardiomyopathy or valve disease)
  • Some medications and stimulants

Should you worry?

Occasional bigeminy in a structurally normal heart is usually benign, even though it can feel unsettling. What matters is how many PVCs there are over a day and whether the heart is otherwise healthy. A very high PVC burden (often quoted as more than 10–15% of beats) can, over time, weaken the heart and deserves follow-up.

Get checked promptly if bigeminy comes with fainting, chest pain, severe breathlessness, or a family history of sudden cardiac death.

How common is it?

Occasional PVCs occur in most healthy people, and short runs of bigeminy are a frequent, often incidental finding. They become more common with age, caffeine and stress, and during illness. In a normal heart they rarely cause harm, but because a very high PVC burden can occasionally weaken the heart muscle over months, the total number over a day is what guides whether any treatment is needed.

What usually happens next

  1. A review of symptoms, caffeine, alcohol and medications
  2. Blood tests for potassium, magnesium and thyroid function
  3. A Holter monitor to count the PVC burden over 24–48 hours
  4. An echocardiogram if the burden is high or the heart may be abnormal

Questions to ask your doctor

  • What percentage of my beats are PVCs?
  • Is my heart structurally normal on an echo?
  • Do I need treatment, or just reassurance and lifestyle changes?

Frequently asked questions

Is ventricular bigeminy dangerous?

In a structurally normal heart it is usually benign, but a very high PVC burden over time can weaken the heart, so the frequency and the underlying heart should be checked.

What does ventricular bigeminy feel like?

Many people feel skipped beats, a thud or a fluttering in the chest; others feel nothing at all.

How is it treated?

Often just reassurance and cutting triggers like caffeine. If PVCs are very frequent or symptomatic, medication or a catheter ablation may be considered.
