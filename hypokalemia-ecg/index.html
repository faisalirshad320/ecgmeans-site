Hypokalaemia on an ECG: What It Means

Needs clinician review

Hypokalaemia means a low level of potassium in your blood. It leaves characteristic fingerprints on the ECG, mainly affecting the T wave, the U wave and the QT interval. It is usually very treatable, but it should be checked by a clinician. Low potassium can make the heart prone to abnormal rhythms, and ECG changes suggest the heart is already being affected.

What "hypokalaemia" means

Hypokalaemia (spelled hypokalemia in the US) simply means a low blood potassium level. Potassium is a mineral that your heart muscle relies on to keep its electrical signals running smoothly.

When potassium falls too low, those electrical signals change in a recognisable way. High potassium leaves its own distinctive marks on an ECG, and low potassium does too. The changes mainly affect three parts of the tracing: the T wave, the U wave and the QT interval.

When a report mentions hypokalaemia, it usually means the person reading your ECG noticed this pattern. It does not tell you your exact potassium level. Only a blood test can confirm that.

What it looks like on the ECG

An ECG records each heartbeat as a series of waves. With low potassium, the doctor may see:

  • Flattening of the T waves. The T wave is the recovery phase of each beat. It looks lower and flatter than usual.
  • Prominent U waves. This is an extra small bump that appears just after the T wave. U waves are normally tiny or absent, so a noticeable one is a classic clue.
  • ST-segment depression. The flat stretch between beats dips below its usual level.
  • Apparent QT prolongation. The QT interval looks longer than normal. In reality, the T wave and U wave tend to merge, so what is being measured is really a QU interval.

Normal blood potassium is about 3.5-5.0 mmol/L. U waves and T-wave flattening tend to appear when the level drops below ~3.0 mmol/L. The risk of a dangerous heart rhythm rises as the level falls further.

In severe hypokalaemia, the heart becomes more prone to ventricular ectopics. These are extra beats arising from the lower chambers. It also becomes more prone to a serious rhythm called torsades de pointes.

Common causes

  • Diuretics ('water tablets'). This is the commonest cause.
  • Vomiting, diarrhoea or laxative overuse, which make the body lose potassium through the gut.
  • Low magnesium. This has to be corrected before potassium can recover properly.
  • Some hormonal conditions, such as Cushing's or hyperaldosteronism.
  • Poor intake or refeeding, for example after a period of eating very little.

Should you worry?

Needs clinician review

Low potassium matters most because it can trigger dangerous rhythms in the lower chambers of the heart. The risk is greatest in people taking digoxin or those with underlying heart disease. If the ECG shows these changes, it suggests your heart is being affected. That is a signal to correct the potassium promptly.

The reassuring part is that the usual causes are common and the low level can be treated. Get urgent help if you feel very unwell, faint, or notice a racing or irregular heartbeat.

What usually happens next

First, a blood test confirms your actual potassium level. ECG changes are a clue, but the blood result guides treatment.

If the level is low, potassium is replaced:

  • By mouth, as tablets or liquid, for milder cases.
  • By drip, if the level is severe. You will usually have heart monitoring while this is given.

Your magnesium is checked and replaced as well. Potassium may not recover until magnesium is corrected.

Finally, your clinician looks for the cause. Most often this is a diuretic or losses through the gut, such as vomiting or diarrhoea. They may adjust a water tablet or treat the underlying illness. If those causes don't fit, they may look at hormonal causes.

If you take diuretics or digoxin, or you have a heart condition, let the team know. This helps them judge how quickly the potassium needs correcting.

Frequently asked questions

What does hypokalaemia mean on my ECG report?

It means your ECG shows a pattern suggesting low blood potassium. Typical signs are flattened T waves, prominent U waves (an extra small bump after the T wave), ST-segment depression and an apparently long QT interval. A blood test is needed to confirm the actual level.

Is low potassium dangerous?

It can be, which is why it needs clinician review. Low potassium can trigger dangerous rhythms in the lower chambers of the heart. The risk is higher in people on digoxin or with underlying heart disease. It is usually treatable once it is identified.

What is a normal potassium level?

Normal blood potassium is about 3.5-5.0 mmol/L. The typical ECG changes of U waves and T-wave flattening tend to appear below about 3.0 mmol/L. The risk of a dangerous heart rhythm rises as the level falls further.

What causes low potassium?

The commonest cause is diuretics, often called water tablets. Other causes include vomiting, diarrhoea, laxative overuse, low magnesium, some hormonal conditions such as Cushing's or hyperaldosteronism, and poor intake or refeeding.

How is hypokalaemia treated?

The blood level is checked first. Potassium is then replaced, by mouth or by drip with heart monitoring if it is severe. Magnesium is checked and replaced too, because potassium may not recover without it. The underlying cause, usually a diuretic or gut losses, is also addressed.
