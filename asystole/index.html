Asystole on an ECG: What a Flatline Means

Seek prompt care

"Flatline" is the most recognizable image in medicine, and it is also one of the most misunderstood. Television often shows doctors shocking a flat line back to life, but real defibrillators cannot restart a heart that has no electrical activity at all. This page explains what asystole looks like on an ECG (EKG — the two abbreviations mean the same test), how clinicians make sure a flat line is genuine and not a loose lead, why the treatment is CPR and medicine rather than a shock, what causes it, and what families can realistically expect.

Quick answer

Asystole is the absence of any electrical activity in the heart, seen on an ECG as a flat or almost flat line with no P waves, no QRS complexes and no T waves. It is a form of cardiac arrest. It is not treated with a shock; treatment is immediate CPR, epinephrine, and a search for reversible causes. Survival is low, especially outside the hospital.

What asystole means

The heart's pumping is triggered by electrical signals. Normally the sinus node fires, the signal spreads through the atria and down to the ventricles, and the ECG records that journey as a P wave, a QRS complex and a T wave.

In asystole, none of that is happening. There is no organized electrical activity and no disorganized activity either — the heart muscle is electrically silent, so it does not contract and no blood is pumped. A person in asystole is unconscious, not breathing normally and has no pulse. It is one of the four rhythms of cardiac arrest, alongside ventricular fibrillation, pulseless ventricular tachycardia and pulseless electrical activity (PEA).

The word comes from Greek: a- (without) and systole (contraction).

What asystole looks like on an ECG

The asystole ECG is a flat or gently wandering baseline:

  • No P waves (sometimes isolated P waves remain — see "P-wave asystole" below)
  • No QRS complexes
  • No T waves
  • The line is rarely perfectly straight. Small drifts from chest compressions, breathing or electrical noise are common, which is why the rhythm is better described as "no organized activity" than as a ruler-straight line.

A few related patterns are worth knowing:

Pattern What the strip shows What it means
Asystole Flat line, no complexes No electrical activity; cardiac arrest
P-wave asystole (ventricular standstill) P waves continue but no QRS follows The atria fire but the signal never reaches the ventricles — often the end stage of complete heart block
Agonal rhythm Very slow, wide, irregular complexes (often under 20 per minute) A dying heart; usually treated as PEA and often slides into asystole
Fine ventricular fibrillation Low, chaotic wiggles that can look almost flat A shockable rhythm that can be mistaken for asystole
Lead off / artifact Perfectly flat line, sometimes with a "lead off" warning A disconnected wire, not a heart problem — check the patient

How clinicians confirm a flatline is real

Because a flat line on a monitor can also come from equipment, resuscitation teams run a quick check — often called the "flatline protocol" — before calling a rhythm asystole:

  1. Check the patient. A conscious, talking person with a flat monitor trace has a lead problem, not asystole.
  2. Check the leads and cables are attached to the patient and plugged into the monitor or defibrillator.
  3. Increase the gain (amplitude). Turning up the size setting can reveal fine ventricular fibrillation hiding in what looked like a flat line.
  4. Look in a second lead. StatPearls notes asystole should be confirmed in at least two leads, because the heart's electrical activity can run at right angles to one lead and appear flat in it.
  5. Use ultrasound if available. Bedside echo can show whether there is any heart movement at all.

Getting this right matters. Fine VF can be shocked and has a better outlook; asystole cannot. An unplugged wire on a living patient obviously needs no resuscitation. Our page on ECG artifact covers other ways the tracing can mislead.

Is asystole shockable?

No. This is the single most important point about asystole.

A defibrillator works by stopping all electrical activity at once, so the heart's own pacemaker can restart an organized rhythm. That helps in ventricular fibrillation, where the heart is electrically chaotic. In asystole the heart is already electrically silent — there is nothing to reset, and shocking it does not help.

Pacing (using electrical pulses to drive the heart) also sounds logical, but the American Heart Association's 2020 guidelines found it does not improve survival in established cardiac arrest and do not recommend it routinely.

How asystole is treated

Treatment follows the advanced life support (ACLS) cardiac arrest algorithm for "non-shockable" rhythms:

  1. High-quality CPR immediately. Chest compressions at about 100–120 per minute, at least 2 inches (5 cm) deep in adults, with minimal pauses. CPR keeps some blood flowing to the brain and heart.
  2. Epinephrine (adrenaline) as soon as possible. The AHA guideline supports giving epinephrine early in non-shockable arrest, then repeating it every few minutes while CPR continues. It tightens blood vessels to push more of the CPR-generated flow to the heart and brain.
  3. Airway and breathing support, often with a breathing tube or other advanced airway, and oxygen.
  4. Rhythm checks about every 2 minutes. If the rhythm changes to VF or pulseless VT, a shock is given. If an organized rhythm with a pulse returns, post-resuscitation care begins.
  5. Search for and treat reversible causes — the "Hs and Ts" below.

Medicines once used for asystole, such as atropine, are no longer part of the standard algorithm.

The Hs and Ts: reversible causes of asystole

Hs Ts
Hypovolemia — severe blood or fluid loss Tension pneumothorax — collapsed lung under pressure
Hypoxia — lack of oxygen (drowning, choking, respiratory failure) Tamponade — fluid squeezing the heart (cardiac tamponade)
Hydrogen ions — severe acidosis Toxins — overdoses, poisoning
Hypo- or hyperkalemia — very low or high potassium (hyperkalemia) Thrombosis, pulmonary — large pulmonary embolism
Hypothermia — very low body temperature Thrombosis, coronary — heart attack

Asystole often appears at the end of a long arrest: VF or PEA that was not reversed gradually loses its electrical energy and fades to a flat line. It can also be the first rhythm seen when severe oxygen lack, extreme slowing of the heart, or a major blood-loss injury is the cause.

Asystole vs other cardiac arrest rhythms

Rhythm ECG appearance Shockable? Typical outlook
Ventricular fibrillation Chaotic, irregular waves Yes Best of the arrest rhythms if shocked quickly
Pulseless VT Fast, wide, regular complexes Yes Similar to VF
PEA Organized complexes but no pulse No Depends heavily on cause
Asystole Flat line No Lowest survival

If you are comparing VF with atrial fibrillation, see AFib vs VFib — they share a word but are completely different in danger.

Can you survive asystole?

Some people do, but the odds are low. The figures depend on where the arrest happens and how quickly help arrives:

  • Out of hospital: StatPearls, citing US registry data, reports that only about 2% of people found in asystole outside the hospital survive to discharge, compared with roughly 27% for shockable rhythms.
  • In hospital: Outcomes are better, because help is immediate and causes such as low oxygen or high potassium may be found and reversed quickly.
  • Witnessed vs unwitnessed: Asystole found after an unwitnessed collapse often means the heart has been without blood flow for a long time. Asystole that appears suddenly in front of a team — for example from a very slow rhythm or a drug reaction — has a better chance.

Survivors may face brain injury from the time without circulation, so care after a return of pulse focuses on protecting the brain, temperature control and finding the cause. Teams also consider when continued resuscitation is unlikely to help; that decision weighs the duration of asystole, the cause, the person's prior health and, wherever known, their wishes.

Get urgent help: if someone collapses and is not breathing normally, call 911 (999 in the UK, 112 in Europe), start hands-only CPR — push hard and fast in the center of the chest — and use an AED if one is nearby. An AED will not shock asystole, but it will shock VF if that is the rhythm, and it will tell you to keep doing CPR.

Asystole in a hospital report or monitor alarm

Sometimes people read "asystole" on a Holter, event monitor or telemetry report and fear it meant cardiac arrest. On ambulatory monitors the word is often used for a pause — a few seconds with no beats, after which the heart restarts on its own. That is a different situation from cardiac arrest and is usually caused by sinus pauses, sick sinus syndrome or heart block. Long pauses, especially with dizziness or fainting, can need a pacemaker, so they still deserve prompt review. Monitor alarms can also trigger on a lead falling off during sleep.

What usually happens next

For a person who has been resuscitated from asystole:

  1. Intensive care with breathing support, blood pressure support and careful temperature management.
  2. Tests to find the cause — blood tests (potassium, blood gases), a 12-lead ECG, echocardiography, chest imaging, and sometimes coronary angiography.
  3. Brain assessment over the following days, because recovery of consciousness can take time and early predictions are unreliable.
  4. Treatment of the underlying cause and, if a rhythm problem such as heart block was responsible, a pacemaker.

For a person whose monitor report mentioned a pause or "asystole" lasting a few seconds, the next step is usually a cardiology review of the recording, symptoms and medications.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Was this true cardiac arrest, or a pause on a monitor that ended on its own?
  • How long did the asystole or pause last, and did it match any symptoms?
  • What do you think caused it, and can that cause be treated?
  • Is a pacemaker or other device needed?
  • If my family member was resuscitated, what are the signs you will watch for brain recovery?

Frequently asked questions

What does asystole mean?

Asystole means the heart has no electrical activity, so it is not beating at all. It shows as a flat line on the ECG and is a form of cardiac arrest.

Can a defibrillator restart a flatline?

No. Shocks treat chaotic electrical activity such as ventricular fibrillation. In asystole there is no activity to reset, so treatment is CPR, epinephrine and correcting the cause.

What is the difference between asystole and PEA?

In asystole the ECG is flat. In pulseless electrical activity the ECG shows an organized rhythm, but the heart produces no pulse. Both are non-shockable and are treated with CPR and epinephrine.

How do you confirm asystole?

Check the patient, check the leads and cables, turn up the gain, and confirm the flat line in at least two leads. This rules out a disconnected lead and fine ventricular fibrillation.

What is fine VF and why does it matter?

Fine ventricular fibrillation is low-amplitude chaotic activity that can look almost flat. Unlike asystole it is shockable, so it must not be missed.

Is a flatline the same as death?

Not immediately. Asystole is cardiac arrest, and some people are resuscitated, especially when it is witnessed and treated quickly. Without effective treatment it leads to death within minutes.

Can asystole show up on a Holter monitor?

Holter and telemetry reports sometimes use "asystole" for a pause of several seconds that ends on its own. That is not cardiac arrest, but long pauses need prompt review.
