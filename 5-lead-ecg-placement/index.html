5 Lead ECG Placement and ECG Leads Explained

The 5-lead setup is the workhorse of hospital heart monitoring. It is what keeps a rhythm on the screen above a bed on a cardiac ward, in step-down units, recovery rooms and ambulances. It is quicker than a 12-lead, can stay on for days, and shows more than a basic 3-lead system — including a chest lead that helps tell different fast rhythms apart. This guide covers exactly where each electrode goes, the two color systems (and why they cause mix-ups), simple memory tricks, and a plain explanation of what people mean when they say "ECG leads."

Quick answer

5 lead ECG placement uses four limb electrodes placed on the torso and one chest electrode. In the US (AHA) colors: white below the right collarbone, black below the left collarbone, green on the lower right abdomen, red on the lower left abdomen, and brown on the chest, usually at the V1 position in the 4th intercostal space right of the sternum.

5 lead ECG placement step by step

Correct 5 lead ECG placement puts four electrodes on the torso, near the shoulders and lower ribs, and one on the chest. Unlike a diagnostic 12-lead ECG, the limb electrodes are not placed on the wrists and ankles. Moving them onto the trunk keeps arm and leg movement from shaking the tracing, which matters when someone is monitored for hours.

Using the US (AHA) colors:

  1. White (RA) — right upper chest, just below the right collarbone, near the shoulder (in the hollow below the clavicle, toward the arm).
  2. Black (LA) — left upper chest, just below the left collarbone, mirror of white.
  3. Green (RL) — right lower abdomen or lower right rib margin. This is the ground (reference) electrode.
  4. Red (LL) — left lower abdomen or lower left rib margin, mirror of green.
  5. Brown (V, chest) — most often at the V1 position: 4th intercostal space, just right of the sternum. It can be moved to another V position if the clinical team wants a different view.

Place the limb electrodes over bone or firm tissue rather than over large muscles, and away from where defibrillator pads, wounds or central lines may need to go. Keep the four limb electrodes roughly in a rectangle around the heart.

ECG lead color codes: AHA vs IEC

Two color systems are in use. North America mostly uses the AHA standard; the UK, Europe, Australia and much of the rest of the world use the IEC standard. The positions are identical — only the colors differ — which is why staff who move between countries sometimes swap cables.

Electrode Position AHA color (US, Canada) IEC color (UK, Europe, Australia) IEC label
RA – right arm Below right clavicle White Red R
LA – left arm Below left clavicle Black Yellow L
RL – right leg (ground) Lower right abdomen Green Black N
LL – left leg Lower left abdomen Red Green F
V / C – chest Usually V1 position Brown White C

Notice the trap: red means left leg in the AHA system but right arm in the IEC system. Always read the printed letters on the lead (RA, LA, RL, LL, V — or R, L, N, F, C) rather than relying on color alone.

5 lead ECG placement mnemonics

Memory tricks for the AHA colors:

  • "White on right" — the white electrode goes on the right upper chest.
  • "Smoke over fire" — black (smoke) sits above red (fire) on the left side.
  • "Snow over grass" — white (snow) sits above green (grass) on the right side.
  • "Chocolate close to the heart" — brown goes on the chest.
  • "Clouds over grass, smoke over fire" — another version of the same layout.

For IEC colors, a common trick is "Ride Your Green Bike" — clockwise from the right shoulder: Red, Yellow, Green, Black.

What does "ECG leads" mean? Lead vs electrode

"ECG leads" is used in two different ways, and the confusion is understandable.

  • In everyday speech, nurses and patients say "leads" to mean the wires and the sticky pads — "put the leads on."
  • In ECG science, a lead is a view of the heart's electrical activity, calculated from two or more electrodes. An electrode is the physical pad on the skin.

So a 5-electrode system does not give only five views. From the four limb electrodes the monitor can calculate leads I, II, III, aVR, aVL and aVF, and from the chest electrode one V lead — up to seven views, though most monitors show one or two at a time. Similarly, a 12-lead ECG uses only 10 electrodes to make 12 leads.

System Electrodes Leads available Typical use
3-lead 3 I, II, III Basic rhythm monitoring, transport
5-lead 5 I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF + one V lead Ward and ICU monitoring, telemetry
12-lead 10 All 12 (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF, V1–V6) Diagnosis of heart attack and other changes

What a 5-lead ECG is used for

A 5-lead system is designed for continuous monitoring, not full diagnosis. Common uses include:

  • Cardiac and telemetry wards — watching for rhythm changes such as atrial fibrillation, pauses or runs of extra beats.
  • Intensive care and recovery rooms — tracking heart rate and rhythm after surgery or during serious illness.
  • ST-segment monitoring in some patients with suspected coronary problems, where the monitor trends ST changes over time.
  • Ambulance and emergency transport, alongside a 12-lead when needed.
  • Drug monitoring, for example when a medicine that can lengthen the QT interval is started.

Why the chest electrode usually goes at V1

Current American Heart Association monitoring standards note that V1 is commonly the preferred lead for arrhythmia monitoring in adults. V1 shows P waves fairly clearly and helps tell a ventricular tachycardia from a supraventricular tachycardia conducted with a bundle branch pattern. A common monitor display is lead II plus V1: lead II for P waves and rate, V1 for QRS shape.

Common 5-lead placement mistakes

Most monitoring problems come down to a handful of avoidable errors:

  • Swapping the arm electrodes. If white and black are reversed, lead I turns upside down and the displayed rhythm may look unfamiliar. Check that white is on the patient's right.
  • Mixing color systems. A nurse trained on IEC cables may put red on the right shoulder out of habit. On an AHA cable that puts the left-leg electrode on the right arm, which inverts lead II.
  • Placing the lower electrodes too high. Green and red should sit below the heart, on the lower ribs or abdomen. If they creep up onto the chest, the triangle around the heart shrinks and the complexes get smaller.
  • Chest electrode drifting off V1. If the brown electrode ends up a rib space too high or too far left, the QRS shape changes and comparisons over time become unreliable. Mark the position with a skin pen if your policy allows.
  • Electrodes over large muscles. The pectoral muscles and the upper abdomen tense with movement and breathing, adding noise.

Special situations

Surgery and procedures. Keep electrodes away from the operative field and the planned site of defibrillator pads. Surgical teams sometimes move the chest electrode to V5, which is better at picking up ST changes from the lateral wall during anesthesia.

Women. Place the chest electrode on the chest wall at the landmark, not on breast tissue. The upper electrodes sit below the collarbones, above the breast.

Children. The same layout applies, using smaller pediatric electrodes. In children, lead II is commonly chosen as the main monitoring lead.

Burns, wounds and dressings. Use the nearest intact skin and keep the four limb electrodes as symmetrical as possible. Document any change from the standard layout so the next shift places them the same way.

Limitations of a 5-lead ECG

  • It is not a 12-lead ECG. With only one chest lead and torso-mounted limb electrodes, it can miss or mislabel ST changes. If a monitored patient develops chest pain, a full 12-lead ECG should be recorded.
  • Torso placement changes the waveforms slightly compared with a standard 12-lead, so measurements on the monitor are not always comparable with a formal ECG.
  • Movement, shivering and loose pads cause artifact, which can trigger false alarms. Poor skin contact is the most common cause of a noisy trace or "leads off" alarm — see ECG artifact.
  • Alarm fatigue is a real risk on busy wards. Good electrode placement, fresh electrodes and well-chosen alarm limits reduce false alarms.

Skin preparation and electrode care

  1. Clip chest hair if it prevents contact.
  2. Clean with soap and water or an alcohol wipe, then dry thoroughly.
  3. Lightly abrade if your local policy recommends it.
  4. Attach fresh electrodes with moist gel; check the use-by date.
  5. Connect the cables without tension, and avoid looping or pulling them tightly across the chest.
  6. Many hospitals change monitoring electrodes about every 24 hours, or sooner if they peel or the signal degrades. Follow local policy.
  7. Check the skin under electrodes for irritation, especially in older adults and people on long admissions.

If you are the patient on a 5-lead monitor

You can usually move around in bed, eat and talk normally. Alarms often go off because an electrode has lifted or a cable is pulled, not because something is wrong with your heart; staff check each alarm. Let your nurse know if a pad feels loose or itchy rather than removing it yourself.

Get urgent help — press the call bell, or call 911/999/112 if you are at home — if you have chest pain, severe breathlessness, feel faint, or feel your heart racing or pounding in a way that does not settle.

Frequently asked questions

Where does the brown lead go on a 5-lead ECG?

Usually at the V1 position, in the 4th intercostal space just right of the sternum. Your team may move it to another chest position for a different view.

What is the mnemonic for 5 lead ECG placement?

"White on right, smoke over fire, chocolate close to the heart" covers the AHA colors: white right upper chest, black above red on the left, and brown on the chest.

What do the colors on ECG leads mean?

They identify which electrode is which. In the US system white is right arm, black left arm, green right leg, red left leg and brown chest. The UK/European system uses different colors for the same positions.

Is a 5-lead ECG the same as a 12-lead ECG?

No. A 5-lead is for continuous monitoring and shows a limited set of views. A 12-lead uses 10 electrodes and is the standard test for diagnosing a heart attack.

How many leads does a 5-lead ECG show?

Up to seven: leads I, II, III, aVR, aVL and aVF from the limb electrodes, plus one chest lead. Most monitors display one or two at a time.

Where does the green ECG lead go?

In the AHA system, the green electrode is the right-leg ground, placed on the lower right abdomen or lower right ribs. In the IEC system green is the left leg electrode.

Can a 5-lead ECG detect a heart attack?

It can show some ST changes, but it is not reliable enough to diagnose or rule out a heart attack. A 12-lead ECG and blood tests are needed.
