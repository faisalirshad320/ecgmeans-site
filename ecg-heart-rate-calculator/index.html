How to Calculate Heart Rate on an ECG: 6 Second, 300 and 1500 Methods

Every ECG report prints a heart rate, but you can also work it out from the tracing itself. Choose a method below, enter what you count on the strip, and the calculator converts it to beats per minute.

Quick answer

To calculate heart rate on an ECG, use the 6 second ECG method for any rhythm: count the QRS complexes in a 6-second strip (30 large boxes) and multiply by 10. For a regular rhythm, divide 300 by the large boxes between two R waves, or 1500 by the small boxes. At standard paper speed, a resting adult rate of 60–100 bpm is normal.

How to calculate heart rate on an ECG

You can calculate heart rate on an ECG with nothing more than the grid printed on the paper. The 6 second ECG method works for every rhythm, while the 300 and 1500 rules are quicker for regular rhythms. All three rely on the paper moving at a fixed speed.

Ways to calculate heart rate on ECG paper

Standard ECG paper runs at 25 mm per second. Each small box is 1 mm wide and lasts 0.04 seconds (40 ms). Each large box holds five small boxes and lasts 0.2 seconds (200 ms), so 300 large boxes or 1500 small boxes pass in one minute.

Method How to do it Best for
300 rule 300 ÷ large boxes between two consecutive R waves A quick estimate when the rhythm is regular
1500 rule 1500 ÷ small boxes between two consecutive R waves The most precise method for a regular rhythm
6-second rule Count R waves in a 6-second strip (30 large boxes) and multiply by 10 Irregular rhythms such as atrial fibrillation

Understanding the ECG grid

Unit on the paper Size Time at 25 mm/s
1 small box 1 mm 0.04 s (40 ms)
1 large box 5 mm (5 small boxes) 0.2 s (200 ms)
5 large boxes 25 mm 1 second
15 large boxes 75 mm 3 seconds
30 large boxes 150 mm 6 seconds
300 large boxes 1.5 m 1 minute

Some machines and countries record at 50 mm/s instead. At that speed every box represents half the time, so the box rules change to 600 ÷ large boxes and 3000 ÷ small boxes. Check the paper speed printed at the bottom of the tracing before you calculate. The calculator above assumes 25 mm/s.

The 6 second ECG method

The 6 second ECG method (also called the 6-second rule or the "times ten" method) is the most practical way to estimate heart rate when the beats are irregular — for example in atrial fibrillation, with frequent extra beats, or with some types of heart block. It is also how many monitors, defibrillators and rhythm-strip courses teach rate counting.

How to do it, step by step

  1. Find a 6-second section of strip. At 25 mm/s this is 30 large boxes (150 mm). Many rhythm strips have small tick marks at the top every 3 seconds (15 large boxes), so two gaps between tick marks equal 6 seconds.
  2. Count the QRS complexes (the tall spikes) that fall inside the 6 seconds. Count partial complexes at the edges consistently — for example, include one that starts inside the window and exclude one that starts outside.
  3. Multiply by 10. Six seconds × 10 = 60 seconds, so the result is beats per minute.
  4. Optionally, count the P waves too. Multiplying P waves by 10 gives the atrial rate, which is useful when the atria and ventricles are beating at different rates, as in third degree heart block.

Strengths and limits of the 6 second ECG method

  • Works with irregular rhythms, because it averages the beats across several seconds.
  • Fast and simple, so it suits emergency settings and rhythm strips printed from a monitor.
  • Less precise than the box methods: each beat counted adds or removes 10 bpm, so a count of 7 or 8 complexes gives 70 or 80 bpm.
  • Less reliable at slow rates. If only three or four beats fall in six seconds, counting over a longer strip (for example 10 seconds × 6, or a full minute) is more accurate. A standard 12-lead ECG prints 10 seconds of rhythm, so you can also count all QRS complexes along the bottom strip and multiply by 6.

The 300 rule: quick sequence

Find an R wave that sits on a thick line. Then count the thick lines to the next R wave: 300, 150, 100, 75, 60, 50. If the next R wave lands on the third thick line, the rate is about 100 bpm. If it lands between two lines, the rate is between those two values.

Worked examples

  • 20 small boxes between R waves: 1500 ÷ 20 = 75 bpm.
  • 4 large boxes between R waves: 300 ÷ 4 = 75 bpm.
  • 9 R waves in a 6-second strip: 9 × 10 = 90 bpm, a useful average when the beats are irregular.
  • RR interval of 600 ms: 60,000 ÷ 600 = 100 bpm.

The 1500 rule for a precise rate

The 1500 rule divides 1500 by the number of small boxes between two consecutive R waves. Because it uses the smallest unit on the paper, it is the most precise manual method when the rhythm is regular, and it is especially helpful at fast rates where the 300 rule's steps (300, 150, 100, 75…) are far apart. For example, 7 small boxes gives 1500 ÷ 7 ≈ 214 bpm, a rate that is hard to estimate by the large-box sequence. If you already know the RR interval in milliseconds, 60,000 ÷ RR gives the same answer.

Which method should you use?

Situation Best method Why
Regular rhythm, quick look 300 rule Fastest; no counting of small boxes
Regular rhythm, exact number 1500 rule or RR interval Most precise
Irregular rhythm 6 second ECG method (or 10-second strip × 6) Averages variable beat-to-beat intervals
Very slow rhythm Count over 10 seconds or a full minute Too few beats in 6 seconds for accuracy
Atria and ventricles at different rates Count P waves and QRS complexes separately Gives both atrial and ventricular rates

Normal heart rate ranges by age

The adult "60–100 bpm" rule does not apply to infants and children, whose hearts normally beat faster. The figures below are typical resting ranges for a calm, awake person; individual normal values vary, and sleep, fever, crying or exercise change them.

Age Typical resting heart rate (bpm)
Newborn (0–1 month) 70–190
Infant (1–11 months) 80–160
1–2 years 80–130
3–4 years 80–120
5–6 years 75–115
7–9 years 70–110
10 years and older, including adults 60–100
Well-trained athletes About 40–60

Source: MedlinePlus (US National Library of Medicine). Pediatric reference charts differ slightly between sources, so your child's doctor may use a different table.

What the number means

  • Under 60 bpm is called bradycardia. It is common and normal in fit people and during sleep; see sinus bradycardia.
  • 60–100 bpm is the normal resting range in adults.
  • Over 100 bpm is tachycardia. It is normal with exercise, fever, pain or anxiety; see sinus tachycardia.

Children have faster normal heart rates than adults, so these adult ranges do not apply to them.

When the rate is not the whole story

The heart rate is only one part of reading an ECG. A normal rate can still come with an abnormal rhythm, and a slow or fast rate can be perfectly healthy. After the rate, clinicians look at the rhythm, the P waves, the PR interval, the QRS interval and the QT interval. Our normal ECG values page lists the usual ranges, and how to read an EKG strip walks through the full sequence.

Common reasons a printed ECG rate differs from your own pulse count include a rhythm that changes over time, extra beats that are too weak to feel at the wrist, and the machine averaging over the 10-second recording.

Get urgent help if a very fast or very slow heart rate comes with chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness or confusion. Call 911 (US), 999 (UK) or 112 (EU) in an emergency.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate heart rate on an ECG quickly?

Divide 300 by the number of large boxes between two R waves. Four large boxes means about 75 bpm.

How do you calculate heart rate when the rhythm is irregular?

Count the R waves in a 6-second strip (30 large boxes) and multiply by 10. Box methods only work when the beats are evenly spaced.

What is the 1500 rule on an ECG?

Divide 1500 by the number of small boxes between two R waves. It is the most precise box method because there are 1500 small boxes in one minute.

How many seconds is one large box on an ECG?

0.2 seconds. Each small box is 0.04 seconds at the standard paper speed of 25 mm/s.

How many boxes are in a 6 second ECG strip?

30 large boxes (150 small boxes) at the standard paper speed of 25 mm/s. Many rhythm strips also mark every 3 seconds at the top of the paper.

Why multiply by 10 in the 6 second rule?

Because 6 seconds × 10 = 60 seconds. Counting the beats in 6 seconds and multiplying by 10 gives the number of beats in one minute.

What is a normal heart rate for a child on an ECG?

It depends on age. Infants commonly run 80–160 bpm and young children 75–130 bpm; the adult range of 60–100 bpm applies from about age 10.
