What "block" means on an ECG

"Block" on an ECG report means a delay or interruption in how the electrical signal travels, not a blocked artery. AV blocks affect the junction between the upper and lower chambers; their degree (first, second or third) describes how many signals get through. Bundle branch and fascicular blocks affect the wiring inside the ventricles and change the shape and width of the QRS complex.

Some of these findings are common and usually harmless, such as first degree AV block or right bundle branch block in a healthy person. Others, such as Mobitz II or complete heart block, can cause dizziness or fainting and often need a pacemaker. Each page below says which group a finding belongs to and what usually happens next.

Types of heart block

There are two main families of heart block, named after where the signal is held up:

  • AV blocks happen at the AV node or just below it, between the atria and the ventricles. They are graded first, second (Mobitz I, Mobitz II and 2:1) and third degree, according to how many signals get through.
  • Bundle branch and fascicular blocks happen further down, in the cables that spread the signal through the ventricles. They widen or reshape the QRS complex but do not, on their own, stop beats from getting through.

A related but different finding is sinoatrial block, where the signal is held up as it leaves the sinus node, before it reaches the AV node.

Degrees of AV block, from mild to severe

It helps to picture the AV node as a gate between the upper and lower chambers:

  1. First degree — the gate opens slowly, but every signal passes.
  2. Second degree — the gate sometimes stays shut. In Mobitz I it closes after a predictable warning (the PR lengthening); in Mobitz II it closes without warning.
  3. Third degree — the gate is shut completely. The ventricles rely on a slower back-up pacemaker in the junction or the ventricles themselves, such as a junctional escape rhythm.

The higher the degree, and the lower in the conduction system the block sits, the more likely it is to cause symptoms and to need a pacemaker. For a side-by-side look at the two bundle branch patterns, see LBBB vs RBBB.

Common causes of heart block

  • Age-related scarring of the conduction system (the most common cause in older adults)
  • Heart attack, especially an inferior heart attack for AV block
  • Heart-rate-slowing medicines such as beta-blockers, some calcium-channel blockers and digoxin
  • High vagal tone in athletes and during sleep (usually first degree or Mobitz I)
  • High potassium levels
  • Heart valve disease and valve procedures
  • Inflammatory and infectious conditions such as myocarditis and Lyme disease
  • Congenital heart block, present from birth

Symptoms and when to get help

Many blocks — especially first degree AV block, Mobitz I, RBBB and LAFB — cause no symptoms and are found by chance. Higher-grade blocks can slow the heart enough to cause tiredness, breathlessness, light-headedness or fainting.

Get urgent help if a heart block finding comes with fainting, near-fainting, chest pain, severe breathlessness or confusion, or if the pulse is very slow and you feel unwell. Call 911 (US), 999 (UK) or 112 (EU).

What usually happens next

  1. Comparison with earlier ECGs to see whether the block is new
  2. Medication review and blood tests, including potassium and thyroid
  3. An echocardiogram if a bundle branch block or higher-grade AV block is found
  4. Ambulatory monitoring (Holter or patch) if symptoms come and go
  5. A pacemaker for Mobitz II, complete heart block, or other blocks that cause symptoms without a reversible cause
