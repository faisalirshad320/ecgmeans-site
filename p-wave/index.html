What Does the P Wave Represent on an ECG?

Usually harmless

Every normal heartbeat starts with a P wave. It is a small bump, but it tells you a lot: where the beat began, whether the upper chambers are stretched or thickened, and whether the signal is getting through to the lower chambers. If your ECG report mentions the P wave (as "abnormal P wave", "P mitrale", "inverted P wave" or "no P waves"), this page explains what the P wave represents, what normal looks like, and which changes are harmless and which need a closer look.

Medically reviewed by Dr. Faisal Irshad, MBBS · Medical DoctorPMDC 54440-SUpdated 2026-10-26
Quick answer

The P wave represents atrial depolarization, the electrical signal that spreads across the heart's two upper chambers (the atria) just before they contract. It is the first small, rounded bump of each heartbeat on an ECG. A normal P wave is under 120 ms long, under 2.5 mm tall in lead II, and upright in leads I, II and aVF.

What does the P wave represent?

The P wave represents atrial depolarization. Depolarization is the electrical "switch-on" that tells heart muscle cells to contract. Each beat starts in the sinus node, a small cluster of pacemaker cells at the top of the right atrium. From there the signal spreads:

  1. First across the right atrium, which makes the early part of the P wave.
  2. Then across the left atrium, which makes the later part.
  3. Then it reaches the AV node, where it pauses briefly before passing to the ventricles.

The combined signal from both atria is what the ECG records as the P wave. The atria contract a fraction of a second later, topping up the ventricles with blood before the main pumping beat.

The atria also recover (repolarize), but that small signal, called the atrial T wave or Ta wave, is usually hidden inside the much larger QRS complex, so you do not normally see it.

Where the P wave sits on the ECG

ECG part What it represents
P wave Atria depolarize (upper chambers activate)
PR segment Signal delayed in the AV node
PR interval Start of P to start of QRS: total atrial-to-ventricular time (120–200 ms)
QRS complex Ventricles depolarize (main pumping chambers activate)
T wave Ventricles repolarize (recover)

The PR interval begins at the start of the P wave, so a P wave problem and a PR problem often appear together.

What a normal P wave looks like

Feature Normal value
Duration Under 120 ms (fewer than 3 small boxes)
Height (amplitude) in lead II Under 2.5 mm (2.5 small boxes)
Lead I, II, aVF Upright
Lead aVR Inverted (normal, because aVR looks at the heart from the opposite direction)
Lead V1 Often biphasic: a small upward part (right atrium) then a small downward part (left atrium)
Shape Smooth and rounded; all P waves on the strip look the same
Relationship to QRS One P wave before every QRS, with a constant PR interval

When all of these are true, the rhythm is coming from the sinus node: sinus rhythm. This is what most people's ECG shows.

P wave ECG: abnormal shapes and what they mean

Most P wave abnormalities point to one of three things: the atria are enlarged or under strain, the beat started somewhere other than the sinus node, or the atria are not contracting in an organized way.

P wave finding What it looks like Usual meaning Read more
P mitrale Wide (≥ 120 ms), often notched "M" shape in lead II; deep negative end-part in V1 Left atrial enlargement or slow conduction between the atria P mitrale, left atrial enlargement
P pulmonale Tall, peaked (≥ 2.5 mm) in lead II Right atrial enlargement, often from lung disease or high lung blood pressure P pulmonale, right atrial enlargement
Both features Tall and wide Both atria enlarged Biatrial enlargement
Inverted P wave (in II, III, aVF) Points downward where it should point up Beat started low in the atria or near the AV node; or limb leads swapped; or dextrocardia Inverted P wave
Changing P wave shapes Three or more different P shapes on one strip Several sites firing: wandering pacemaker or multifocal atrial tachycardia Wandering atrial pacemaker
Early, odd-shaped P One P wave arrives early and looks different A premature atrial beat (PAC) Sinus rhythm with PACs

The ECG criteria for atrial enlargement are easy to trigger and are not very accurate. An echocardiogram is the test that actually measures atrial size, which is why a report usually says "possible" or "consider" atrial enlargement.

When there is no P wave

A missing P wave is one of the most useful clues on an ECG.

  • Atrial fibrillation. The atria quiver chaotically instead of firing as one, so there are no P waves, just a wavy baseline, and the rhythm is irregularly irregular. See atrial fibrillation.
  • Atrial flutter. Regular "sawtooth" flutter waves take the place of P waves, often at about 300 per minute.
  • Junctional rhythm. The beat starts at the AV junction. The P wave may be hidden in the QRS, or appear inverted just before or after it. See junctional rhythm.
  • Severe high potassium. P waves flatten and can disappear as potassium rises. This needs urgent attention. See hyperkalemia ECG.
  • Sinus arrest or SA block. The sinus node pauses, so both the P wave and the beat are missing for a moment.

When there are more P waves than QRS complexes

If some P waves are not followed by a QRS, the signal is being blocked between the atria and the ventricles (AV block):

Pattern Name
PR gets longer each beat, then one P has no QRS Second-degree block, Mobitz I (Wenckebach)
PR constant, then a P suddenly has no QRS Second-degree block, Mobitz II
P waves and QRS complexes completely unrelated Third-degree (complete) heart block
Early P wave with no QRS after it Blocked (non-conducted) PAC, which is usually harmless

Mobitz II and complete heart block can cause fainting and usually need prompt specialist care, sometimes a pacemaker.

Normal P wave variations that are not a problem

Not every unusual-looking P wave is abnormal. Several harmless things change its shape:

  • Heart rate. At faster rates the P wave can grow slightly taller and may run into the end of the previous T wave, which makes it hard to see.
  • Breathing. The heart's position shifts as you breathe in and out, so P wave height can vary a little from beat to beat, especially in lead III.
  • Body build. In tall, thin people the heart hangs more vertically, and P waves in the inferior leads can look taller without any atrial enlargement.
  • Lead placement. Swapping the left and right arm electrodes turns the P wave upside down in lead I. That is a recording error, not heart disease, and repeating the ECG with correct placement fixes it.
  • Low atrial rhythm in young people. A slightly different P wave axis is common in children and fit young adults and usually comes and goes.

How doctors measure the P wave

The P wave is measured in the lead where it is clearest, usually lead II for height and duration and V1 for the two-part (biphasic) shape. Duration runs from where the P wave leaves the baseline to where it returns. Height runs from the baseline to the peak. In V1, a deep, wide final negative part (at least 1 small box deep and 1 small box wide) suggests left atrial enlargement. A tall first positive part (over 1.5 mm) suggests right atrial enlargement.

Is an abnormal P wave serious?

Usually not on its own. A borderline P wave in a person with no symptoms is often a normal variant or a sign of a manageable condition such as high blood pressure.

It matters more when:

  • it suggests left atrial enlargement, which is linked to high blood pressure, valve disease and a higher chance of later atrial fibrillation;
  • it suggests right atrial enlargement in someone who is short of breath, which can point to lung disease or pulmonary hypertension;
  • the P waves are absent and the rhythm is irregular (possible AFib, which affects stroke risk);
  • P waves are not being conducted to the ventricles (heart block).

Get urgent help if an ECG finding comes with fainting, chest pain, severe breathlessness, or a very slow or very fast heartbeat. Call 911 (US), 999 (UK) or 112 (EU).

What usually happens next

  1. Your doctor compares the ECG with any earlier ones. A P wave shape you have always had matters less than a new one.
  2. Blood pressure check and review of symptoms such as breathlessness or palpitations.
  3. Echocardiogram if atrial enlargement is suggested, to measure chamber size and check the valves.
  4. Heart rhythm monitor (Holter or patch) if AFib or heart block is suspected but not caught on the resting ECG.
  5. Blood tests such as potassium and thyroid function where relevant.

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Which P wave change did the report describe, and in which leads?
  • Was it present on my previous ECGs?
  • Do I need an echocardiogram to check the size of my atria?
  • Does this change my risk of atrial fibrillation, and should I be monitored?

Frequently asked questions

What is the P wave in an ECG?

It is the first small upward bump of each heartbeat on the ECG. It shows the electrical signal spreading through the two upper chambers (atria).

What does the P wave represent: atrial contraction or depolarization?

Strictly, depolarization, which is the electrical activation. The atria physically contract a few milliseconds later, triggered by that signal.

Why is the P wave inverted in aVR?

Lead aVR looks at the heart from the upper right, opposite to the direction the atrial signal travels. An inverted P in aVR is therefore normal.

Where is atrial repolarization on the ECG?

It happens at the same time as the QRS complex and is normally hidden by it, so no separate wave is seen.

What does a tall P wave mean?

A P wave 2.5 mm or taller in lead II (P pulmonale) suggests right atrial enlargement, often related to lung disease. It needs confirming with other tests.

What does a wide or notched P wave mean?

A P wave 120 ms or longer, especially with an M-shaped notch (P mitrale), suggests left atrial enlargement or slow conduction between the atria.

Can you have a heartbeat without a P wave?

Yes. In atrial fibrillation, junctional rhythms and ventricular rhythms the ventricles still beat, but no normal P wave comes before the QRS.
