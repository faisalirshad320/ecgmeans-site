Fusion Beat: What It Means on an ECG

Usually harmless

A fusion beat happens when the heart is activated from two directions at once — the normal pathway from above and a ventricular beat from below meet in the middle, producing a single hybrid-shaped beat. Fusion beats are usually harmless in themselves, and they are a helpful clue that confirms a ventricular rhythm.

What a "fusion beat" means

Normally each beat is activated from one source. A fusion beat occurs when two wavefronts collide: the normal signal coming down from the atria and an impulse arising in the ventricles reach the ventricular muscle at nearly the same moment. The resulting QRS is a blend — part normal, part ventricular — so its shape sits between the two.

What it looks like on the ECG

A single QRS whose shape is intermediate between the patient's normal narrow beat and their wide ventricular beats. It often appears among premature ventricular contractions or during a ventricular rhythm, when timing allows the two sources to overlap.

Why it matters

Fusion beats are generally not dangerous on their own. Their real value is diagnostic: when a wide-complex tachycardia is in question, the presence of fusion beats (and capture beats) is strong evidence that the rhythm is ventricular tachycardia rather than a supraventricular rhythm conducted abnormally. So they help doctors identify the rhythm correctly.

Get checked if fusion beats appear during a sustained fast heartbeat, or with symptoms such as palpitations that do not settle, fainting, chest pain or breathlessness.

Fusion and capture beats together

Fusion beats are often mentioned alongside "capture beats". During a ventricular rhythm, an occasional normal beat from above may slip through and briefly activate the ventricles normally (a capture beat), or partly blend with a ventricular beat (a fusion beat). Seeing either during a fast, wide-complex rhythm is strong evidence that the rhythm is ventricular tachycardia, which is why doctors look for them so carefully.

What usually happens next

  1. Using the fusion (and capture) beats to help confirm a ventricular rhythm
  2. Assessing the underlying rhythm and the heart's structure
  3. Monitoring if a ventricular tachycardia is suspected
  4. Treating the underlying rhythm rather than the fusion beats themselves

Questions to ask your doctor

  • Do my fusion beats confirm a ventricular rhythm?
  • Is the underlying rhythm something that needs treatment?
  • Does my heart need further imaging or monitoring?

Frequently asked questions

Is a fusion beat dangerous?

The fusion beat itself is usually harmless. It is mainly useful as a clue that helps confirm a ventricular rhythm.

What causes a fusion beat?

It forms when a normal beat from above and a ventricular beat from below activate the ventricles at the same time, blending into one hybrid-shaped QRS.

Why are fusion beats important?

Together with capture beats, they are strong evidence that a wide-complex tachycardia is ventricular tachycardia rather than another rhythm.
